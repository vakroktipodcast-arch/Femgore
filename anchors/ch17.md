# Chapter 17

*Lesson Two. Subject: The Architect. Function: to design the room in which the thing did not happen. You draw the plans before the complaint is made. You set the walls where the complainant will be standing. You furnish the room with a better story, you leave a light on so that anyone who looks in can see there is nothing there, and you sign nothing yourself. You have performed this function for ██ years without once lying to yourself about what it is. Your colleagues at ████████ mistake this for integrity, and you have let them. You see every room exactly as it is. You have concluded that to see it plainly was the hard part, the unkind part, the part that cost, and that having paid it you owe nothing further. Sight is not a payment. Sight is the least that was owed. What you built after you had seen is the work under evaluation. Evaluation: You mistook clarity for cruelty.*

My sleeves were already at my elbows. I took the gloves from the box the way I take them at work, two at a time, left and then right, and snapped each cuff over the wrist, and in the morgue nobody ever hears that snap because the extraction fans are louder than God. In the suite there were no fans. The record had come to the end of its side a minute before, and the tone arm had lifted and gone home by itself, and the room, which a man had paid a great deal of money to make deaf, took the sound of the nitrile and gave none of it back. I heard my own gloves the way you hear yourself swallow when you have a cold. I heard him breathing. It did not sound as if it came from the desk. It sounded as if it were inside my ear, and had been invited.

Smell first. It always is, with me; I was a nose before I was a pair of hands, and fourteen hundred cases have only made the nose vain. He smelled of vetiver, the soap rather than the cologne, that dry green rootiness men buy because they have been told it smells of money and which in fact smells of a good cellar. Under the vetiver, rye, on his breath and on the rim of the glass beside the photograph. Under the rye, the warm dust that comes off amplifiers and screens that have been on all day, a smell like an ironing board left standing. And under all of it, new, rising while I stood there from the hair of his axillae into the charcoal cotton of his shirt, the sharp first note of apocrine sweat. Not the sweat of heat. The other one. On a decedent I would have written it down: fear, recent onset. On him I wrote nothing down. I breathed in through my nose, slowly, the way you smell a glass before you are allowed to drink it, and it was the first time in my working life that a body had been afraid of me.

He was lying on his back on the long edit desk, which I had cleared, with his wrists taped palm-up to its edge and zip-tied through the cable grommets, his ankles done the same at the far end, and a band of industrial tape across his thighs, because I am thorough and not because he was going anywhere. The one lamp was behind his head. The standby lights of the equipment ran in a row along the wall at knee height, small and amber and patient, like the eyes of animals at the edge of a road. I laid a towel under each of his arms and a towel along each of his sides. I opened his shirt with a blade from collar to hem, the way you open a letter you have waited a long time for and want to keep the envelope, and laid the two halves back, and there he was.

"Should I watch?" he said.

"Most of my patients don't get a vote."

"That isn't an answer."

"Watch, then."

I examined him first. I always do. You don't open a book at the index.

"The body is that of a well-nourished adult male," I said, in the voice I use for the dictaphone, "consistent with the stated age of forty-eight years. Length approximately one hundred and eighty-three centimetres. Weight estimated at eighty-four kilograms."

"Eighty-two."

"Clothed."

He laughed. It went through his abdomen in a wave, rectus abdominis tightening segment by segment and letting go, and I put my palm flat on him below the costal margin to feel the last of it, and he was warm. I had known he would be warm. I had known it for weeks, the way you know a fact about a country you have read about. It is a different thing to put your hand on the country.

"Well-healed linear surgical scar, right lower quadrant, approximately six centimetres, consistent with appendicectomy."

"I was nine."

"History is not usually available," I said, and it was true, and it was the funniest thing I had ever said at work, because fourteen hundred patients and not one of them had ever told me where it hurt.

I went on. Nails clean, short, filed square; he did them himself, and well, the cuticles pushed back. The pads of the distal phalanges of both hands broadened and flattened, with a fine callus at the fingertips of the right, consistent with forty years of keyboard. Chest hair sparse, going to ash at the sternum. No tattoos. No injection marks. He had showered before I came, and shaved, and there was a small nick at the angle of his jaw where the razor had caught, already sealed, the first wound of the night and his own work. I graded him for it in my head. Preparation: excellent. He had dressed for me too.

The forearm first. I had planned it for weeks and I am going to tell you why, because you will want to know and you won't want to ask. It was his hands. I had stood in his house and touched one key of his piano, and it had been in tune, and I had left before I could do anything else, and I had been thinking about the machinery ever since: what moves when a man like that sits down at an instrument, what it is in him that knows where the notes are in the dark.

I set the blade to the volar surface of his left forearm a hand's width above the wrist crease and drew it towards the elbow, ten centimetres, through the epidermis and the dermis and the thin pale web of the superficial fascia, and the skin opened behind the blade the way skin does, not like cloth but like a mouth that has decided to. And he bled.

I need you to understand about that. The dead don't bleed. The dead seep. There is no pressure behind them; what comes out of a decedent comes out the way ink comes out of a cheap biro left on its side, a slow, sullen pooling, gravity's work and nobody else's. He bled like a fountain pen, which is to say with intent. A bead stood up along the whole length of the line at once, bright as enamel, and the beads found each other and became a line, and the line overflowed and ran around the curve of his forearm onto my towel, and it was so warm that I felt it through the glove before it had properly reached the glove. Thirty-seven degrees. I had never in my life put my hand into anything at thirty-seven degrees. Everything I touch arrives at four.

It smelled of iron, but not the morgue's iron. Old blood smells of a coin that has been in somebody's mouth. His smelled of the coin being struck. Only then did I look, the way I always look last, and it was the colour of the inside of a cherry, and it was moving.

I put in a small retractor and opened the window, and there was palmaris longus lying over everything like a guitar string. Fourteen per cent of people don't have one. I had wondered. Of course he had one; he'd have had a spare in a drawer. I eased it aside, and flexor carpi radialis with it, and beneath them in the distal forearm the four tendons of flexor digitorum superficialis lay side by side in their sheaths, glossy, pale, the colour of the inside of a shell.

"Play something," I said.

He turned his head and looked at his hand. "I can't see the keys."

"You've never needed to."

He played a chord against the wood of the desk. I don't know what chord it was. Something with a lot of fingers in it. And under my forceps the tendons slid, each one drawing back into the forearm and coming forward again on its own errand, four cables in four lift shafts, each one taking its finger down to the wood and letting it up, and through the window I had made in him I saw the actual cords of music, the strings it is played on, which nobody sees, which the men who invented music never saw, and I said, "That is beautiful," and it was. I have never meant a word more. I am not going to use it again. You will have to take that one and keep it somewhere.

"Again," I said, and he played it again, and I watched, and I could have watched all night, and that was the problem with the night, and I knew it, and I picked up the larger blade.

There is a reason it is a Y and not a T. A Y keeps the neck clean. The family will want to view the neck. Nobody was going to come and view his neck, but I am a creature of habit and so I gave him one he could have been viewed in. I started at the right acromion, where the bone comes up under the skin like a knuckle, and came down beneath the swell of the pectoral to the xiphoid process, the little cartilage tongue at the foot of the sternum; then from the left acromion, to meet it; and then down the midline, the dermis parting and the fat beneath it standing up yellow on either side, curving around the umbilicus to the left the way we are all taught, for reasons nobody can remember, and on down to the pubis.

He made sounds. I am going to be exact about them, because they deserve it. They were not screams. A scream is a decision; it needs the diaphragm and the intercostals and the will. What came out of him was below decision: glottal, involuntary, the vocal folds closing and opening on air the body was pushing out of its own accord, a sound like a heavy door being tried from the other side. The room kept every one of them. That was what he had paid for. In another building those sounds would have gone into the walls and through them and into a neighbour's ear and a neighbour's phone. In this one they went nowhere. They stayed with us. I could almost hear them settle on the carpet.

I took the upper flap of the Y between finger and thumb, laid the blade nearly flat and began to reflect it, undercutting in short strokes, the way you skin a fish when you want the fillet whole. Under the skin, the subcutaneous fat, butter-yellow, and on it the blood beading up from a thousand small vessels like condensation on a glass, except that it was the reverse of condensation; it was the warm thing, sweating into the cool air of the suite. Under the fat, pectoralis major in its fan, the fibres running from the sternum and the clavicle and converging on the arm, the colour of the flesh of a plum, and it moved. It moved when he breathed. On my table it never moves.

I lifted the flap in my hand to feel what it weighed. A living weight. About what a good rib-eye weighs on the board before you salt it, and the heat of him came up through it and through the nitrile into my palm like something being handed to me across a table.

I would like to report my own findings now, since nobody else was going to. I put two fingers to his carotid and counted, and while I counted his I listened to mine, which I could hear clearly enough in that room without touching anything. His was ninety-six. Mine was a hundred and ten. One of us was having a harder night than the other, and it was not, physiologically speaking, him. My face was hot. There was a heaviness lower down, a frank, wet wanting, and I am putting it in the same column as his sweat, because it was the same finding: a body that had understood what the night was before the person inside it had admitted anything.

A drop of sweat had come out at his hairline and was running towards his eye. My gloves were not fit to touch him with, so I wiped it away with the back of my wrist, the one clean inch between the cuff and the blood, and he closed his eyes while I did it, the way a man closes his eyes when a woman touches his face. Of everything I did that night, that was the most obscene. I would like that noted.

I knew what came next, because I know what bodies do. Shock does what shock does. The skin goes pale and cold and slick as the blood is called in from the edges to keep the centre alive; the pulse climbs and thins until it is a thread you have to hunt for; and the voice goes early, because the voice is a luxury, and the body in shock is closing its luxuries one by one, like a house shutting its rooms for the winter. Fourteen hundred cases had taught me that, every one of them after the fact. I had planned the rest of the night around his silence. I had, if I am honest, looked forward to it, the way you look forward to the point in a dinner when the other person stops talking and you can eat.

"You're very good at this," he said. His voice was level. "I've been trying to think what it's like, and it's like watching someone who really knows her instrument, when you can tell from the first bar that she isn't going to make a mistake, and you can stop worrying and just listen."

It was a whole sentence. It had a subordinate clause in it, and then another, and it arrived where it had been going. I hadn't expected him to be able to. I slowed down so he could.

* * *

He talked the way he must have talked in the rooms where men paid him to, in sentences that knew where they were going before they set out, and I worked at the speed of the sentences.

"Do you know what the job is?" he said. "People think it's lying. It isn't. Lying is for amateurs and candidates. The job is making sure that nobody ever has to decide anything. Something happens. By the time it reaches anyone who could do something about it, it isn't a thing any more. It's a document, and the document is a matter, and a matter is something you manage, and nobody has ever had to look at a thing that is being managed. Nobody decides. That's the product. I was very good at it, and I want you to know I knew what it was the whole time."

"I know."

"Of course you do. You wrote it down."

I opened the anterior rectus sheath along its length. It is a glossy white aponeurosis, tough as the skin of a drum, and under it rectus abdominis lay in its segments, the tendinous intersections crossing it like the scoring on a bar of chocolate. Every man who has ever paid a trainer has paid for those lines. Here were his, and nobody was going to see them but me. I went down the linea alba, which is the body's seam, the place it was closed up before it was born, and which like every seam is where the garment gives most willingly. I went as far as the transversalis fascia and the soft fat under it and I stopped there, with the peritoneum shining beneath my fingertips like the skin on hot milk, unbroken.

I could have opened it. If I had opened it the conversation would have been over inside ten minutes, and I was not ready for the conversation to be over. If you are keeping a list, and I think by now you are, put that near the top.

He talked about himself, because I let him and because he wanted to. About the house, which had been too quiet for two years and which he had filled with records the way people leave a radio on for a dog. About his wife, without unkindness and without her name, which I noticed, and which I would have done too. About the Tabard Club.

"It's Tuesday," he said. "I'm missing my set."

"They'll get the house pianist."

"The house pianist plays like he's being paid by the note."

I laughed out loud. In that room it sounded like somebody else's laugh, played back slightly too close to the microphone. I picked up my glass, which I should not have done in that glove, and drank. It was good rye, dry and spiced like the crust of a dark loaf, and the iron in the air went into it, and the two tastes sat on my tongue together, and I would not have separated them for money. My mouth was watering. It had been for some time. I am reporting that. I am not going to explain it.

"You broke the Dolphy," he said.

"Yes."

"*Out to Lunch.* It was a first pressing."

"I chose it for the sleeve. There's a clock on it with too many hands."

That pleased him; I watched it please him, a small loosening at the corners of the eyes. "Do you know how he died?"

"No."

"Berlin, nineteen sixty-four. He was thirty-six. He collapsed on tour and they took him to a hospital, and the doctors looked at what he was and what he did for a living and decided he'd taken something, and they let him sleep it off. He was diabetic. Nobody tested him. He died of sugar."

I know that death. I have signed it, in other names. A coma that a finger-prick would have caught, a test that costs less than a sandwich, not run, because by the time the body was on the trolley everyone in the room had already decided what kind of body it was. Neither of us said what it rhymed with. He had chosen the anecdote. I would like you to notice that: a dying man with his rectus sheath open, choosing his material. I gave him full marks for it in my head before I could stop myself, and then I let myself.

"I told him," he said. "After you left me the page. I went to Holt and I told him there was a problem from the old files."

"What did he say?"

"He said, 'I thought Diane closed those.' Then he asked me what I thought of the numbers in the suburbs. The next morning I couldn't get into half the things I'd built, and nobody had told me. That's how he does it. I've watched him do it to other men for twenty years. You don't get fired. Your furniture gets moved, a little at a time, and one day you go to sit down and there's nothing there."

I had closed Diane. I didn't say that either. He was the first man in twenty years to tell me something true without counsel present, and he was doing it with his abdominal wall open to the lamp, and I was not going to interrupt him with a joke, even a good one.

"He takes one minute alone before every speech," he said. "His brother's photograph. Mark. He sits with it, or he stands with it, I've never been in the room, nobody has. The detail waits outside the door. Standing instruction. Nobody goes in, and nobody knocks, and nobody has ever asked him why, because everybody knows why, and it's the best thing about him, and it's true." He stopped for air. I waited for him. "Watch the old footage. Sixty seconds. He's never gone over." And then, as if he were handing me a key across a counter: "That's your door. It's the only one."

"Thank you," I said.

I meant it. I want to be exact about how I meant it: the way you mean it when you have come into a shop for one thing and the man behind the counter goes into the back without being asked and comes out with it in his hands.

I touched my side. The wound from the contractor's knife is on the left flank, below the ribs, and it had been quiet all night, and I touch it when I am thinking. My hands were not fit, so I pressed it with the inside of my forearm, the bare skin above the glove against the shirt above the scar, and held it there, and thought.

Something had changed in my hands, and I hadn't told them to. I had been cutting. Sharp dissection, the blade doing the separating, quick and clean. At some point I had put the blade down on the towel by his hip without deciding to, and now I was using my fingers. Blunt dissection: easing the planes apart along their own grain, the way you separate two pages that have been stuck together when you want to read both sides. It is slower. It does less damage. It is what you do when the tissue is something you mean to keep. Brandt, oral component: distinction.

I had my way into the room. It had come from the man I was taking apart, and I was taking him apart more gently now, and I noticed, and I didn't stop.

* * *

He began to go a little after one. I read it the way I would read a chart. The colour left his face first, and the skin of his forehead took on the sheen of tallow; his lips went the colour of putty; the pulse under my two fingers had climbed to a hundred and thirty and thinned to a cord. His breathing was faster and shallower, as if he were trying to get it done before something closed. I had used seven towels. I counted them, the old way, wrung weight against dry, and put him at a litre and a half, give or take what the carpet had kept for itself. A class two haemorrhage going on three. His sentences were getting shorter. They were still complete.

"There's one more thing," he said. "Then you can finish."

"All right."

"Three years ago a woman tried to get in touch with you."

I had the rib shears in my hand by then. I had moved to the chest, because the abdomen was done and the night was not, and I had set the jaws to the costal cartilages on the left, second and third, a centimetre in from the bone, the way I do it every working day.

"She left you a message. At your office. You never called her back."

"How do you know."

"I was watching her too."

He said her name. The shears went through the fourth cartilage at that moment with the sound they make, which is the sound of a stick of celery, and the name went under it. I heard that he had said a name. I am not going to give it to you here. You will have it when I had it, and not before, which seems fair. The air went into his left chest with a small, polite hiss, and the lung on that side let go of the wall and fell away from my fingers, and he took a breath with half of what he'd had, and used it.

"She died eight months later." He swallowed. "I'm sorry. I thought you should know."

They were the last complete sentences he said, and they were complete: subject, verb, object. He even got the contraction in. Three hours earlier he had told me there were eleven names, and watched me learn that I had never been alone, and been kind about it. This was the same kindness. I recognised it, in the way you recognise a voice in the next room without going in.

I finished. I took the cartilages on the right, second to seventh, and opened the sternoclavicular joints with the blade, the way you joint a chicken at the thigh: find the place, and it gives. I lifted the sternal plate away and laid it on the towel by his shoulder, and the right lung went down like the left, and the talking stopped for good. Not because he had decided to stop. Because the bellows were open to the room.

His heart was in its sac, the pericardium, fibrous and pale, and it was moving under it the way something moves under a sheet. I opened the sac with one stroke. And then I took off my right glove.

I want to be exact about this, because it is the only thing I did that night that was not in the plan. I peeled the glove down off my fingers inside out and dropped it on the towel, and I put my bare hand into his chest and took his heart in it. It was hot. It was slick and heavy and alive and much harder than you would think, a fist of muscle working against my palm, and as I held it the beat broke up and became something else, a shivering, a writhing, what the textbooks call a bag of worms, and the textbooks are right; the textbooks were written by people who had had their hands where mine was. Fibrillation. I held it through all of it. It went on for longer than you would believe, and then it slowed, and then it was still, and then his heat began to leave it, under my skin, a degree at a time. Everything I have ever touched had already done that before it reached me. I had never felt it go.

Three hundred and eighty grams, I thought, give or take. My watch said ten to two. It was Wednesday. He had missed his set altogether.

I closed him the way they close them at work when the family isn't coming: quickly, without sutures, the sternal plate set back, the flaps folded home, a tidying that is also, if you watch it often enough, a kind of love that nobody is there to see. Then the tape. Black vinyl, two strips, across his eyes. He had watched the whole time. I had let him. They were not going to close by themselves, and it seemed only polite to close them for him.

The page was on the table beside the photograph, folded in quarters, with a ring on it where he had been using it as a coaster. I chose to take that as a compliment. It was his own copy, the one I had left on his pillow beside the broken record, and he had kept it and read it until the folds had gone soft. I opened it out on his chest, smoothed it with my bare hand, and pinned it through the skin above the sternum. The marker squeaked in that room like a mouse. I circled THE ARCHITECT. The Evaluation was where I had typed it two years ago, under my own redactions. MISTOOK CLARITY FOR CRUELTY. It still read well. It had held.

Then the exit, which is the part nobody admires. The instruments into their roll, the blades into their sleeve, the rib shears last. The towels into the bags, double-bagged. My glass, with the print of my mouth on its rim. The right glove from the towel, and then I peeled off the left and put it in with its brother. The photograph of Diane I left where he had kept it. The Mingus sleeve I left beside it. The record was still on the platter, side one finished, the label turning under no needle. I didn't turn it over. I have never owned a record. I wouldn't have known how to hold it.

I opened the suite door with my elbow. When it unsealed, the corridor's ventilation came in on me like weather, a whole building's worth of moving air, and after that room it was like surfacing. I took the stairwell door with my hip and the door to the car park with my back, the way you go through doors in a hospital when you are scrubbed, and I didn't think about why, because my body was thinking about it for me and I was busy.

I was elated. I am telling you that plainly because I want it on the record before anything else is: I was elated. I came down the stairs two at a time with the bags knocking against my knee. The suite was in my hair, the vetiver and the rye and the warm dust, and him, and I could smell it every time I turned my head, and I turned my head more than I needed to. There was a chicken in my fridge that had been in brine since the day I cooked, and I had been saving it for tonight: out of the brine, patted dry, the oven as hot as it goes, the skin. I would sit down at my own table, for once, and eat half of it with my fingers. I want you to know I was thinking about the chicken. It was the best night of my life, and I was elated, and I was hungry, and I told myself the hunger was physiology, and I believed me.

I was in the car before the elation reached the part of me that had heard what he said.

The Mingus sleeve on his table, *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady* still on the platter, side two never played. My hands on the wheel, red to the wrist. I had not noticed.
