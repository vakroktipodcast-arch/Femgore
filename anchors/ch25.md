# Chapter 25

His hand was on mine. It was warm and dry. It was the hand of a man comforting someone, and he left it there, and his thumb moved once across my knuckles, the way you would do it at a funeral. The agent had reached his face. His eyes were slow to follow anything. "Take your time," he said. He said it slowly. He meant it kindly. I took my hand back. I stood up. I had not decided anything. Something came up in me then, and it was rage, and it had no heat in it at all. I waited for it to turn into the other thing. It did not. I noticed that.

Then my hands were on him. He was heavier than he looked. The chair went over and we went with it, and the dressing table rocked when his shoulder hit it, and the photograph went off the edge onto the carpet. I had one knee on the floor. I had my hands on his neck. He did not fight much. The agent was in him, and he did not understand what was happening, and he looked up at me as if I had asked him a question and he wanted very much to get it right. There was no sound I wanted to keep. There was no heat. I kept waiting for it, the way you wait for a stair in the dark that isn't there, and it wasn't there, and I went on. My side pulled, hard, where the knife had been. That was the only thing my body told me. Nothing else in me said anything at all.

He was supposed to live. That was the lesson as written: kept alive, and found, and finished by eleven women with paper. He stopped. I took my hands away. It was over before the minute was. I checked for the taste. There wasn't one.

* * *

He was on the floor.

He was on his back between the chair and the dressing table, with one arm under him and the other out across the carpet, palm up. His tie was still knotted. His jacket had ridden up under his shoulders, and the flag pin on his lapel had turned on its post so that it hung sideways. One shoe had come half off. His left cuff had come open, and the cufflink was on the carpet a little way from his hand. I looked at the cufflink for a while. Then I looked at the rest of him again.

His eyes were half open. His mouth was a little open too, as if he had been about to say something else and had been interrupted. That was what he looked like. A man who had been interrupted.

I didn't kneel.

I have knelt beside fourteen hundred bodies, give or take. I have knelt on tile and on gravel and on a kitchen floor in August and on the wet bank of a river. Kneeling is how it starts. You get down to where they are. You look at the face from the side, the way the light falls on it, and then you begin. I stood over him with my hands at my sides and did not get down to where he was. He was the first one I didn't examine.

I stood there and took stock of myself instead, because somebody had to. My knee hurt where it had been on the floor. The pull in my side had stopped as soon as my hands came away, and the place was quiet again, the way it is most days. My breathing was slow. My face was dry. I was cold, and I had been cold for weeks, and that was not news.

Something in me started anyway. "The body is that of," I said, out loud, in the voice I use for the dictaphone, and stopped. I heard it in the room. It sounded like somebody reading from a card. I didn't finish it. The rest of the list went on for a few words in my head without me, the colour of the face, the set of the hands, how long, the way a radio goes on talking in another room after you have stopped listening to it, and then I turned that off as well.

I have written a time of death for every body I have ever stood over. I did not look at the clock. It was right there on the wall.

The room smelled of the carpet cleaner they had used on the whole floor, and under that, of him.

He was the second man who had told me to take my time.

I put my tongue against the back of my teeth. Nothing. My mouth was dry, and clean, and tasted of nothing at all, the way a glass tastes when it has just come out of the dishwasher. I checked my jacket pocket with my hand, the outside one, the way you check for your keys. The marker was there. The pin was there. I don't know what else I thought would be there.

A little while before, on that floor, with his hand on mine, he had said Diane's daughter's name. He had said it crying. He had known it the way he knew all of them. He had never known mine.

He had asked me, earlier, what I wanted. Not what I was going to do. What I wanted. I still didn't have an answer. I had thought for a moment, with my hands on him, that the answer might be down there, the way it had been down there before, in other rooms. It wasn't there either.

The page was on the table. It was lying where I had put it down, beside the place where the photograph had stood, and the place was just a place now, a square of polished wood with nothing on it. I went and picked the page up. I didn't read it. I knew what it said. I had typed it two years ago, and I had read it so many times since that the words had stopped being words and become a shape. I held it the wrong way round, and through the back of the paper I could see the circle I had drawn on it, reversed, like something seen from the other side of a window.

I took the pin out of my pocket. I went back and stood over him with the page in one hand and the pin in the other, and I held the page above his chest, where it was supposed to go.

My hands didn't.

I want to be clear about this. They weren't shaking. They were steady. They have been steady for most of my life, and they were steady then. They held the page over his shirt, flat and level, a hand's width from the cloth, and they held the pin, and they did not move down. I waited for them. I watched them the way I had watched my left hand in the lift the night of the fundraiser, as if they belonged to somebody on my table and I was only there to write down what they did. They didn't do anything. They stayed where they were. After a while I could feel the muscles in my forearms beginning to ache from holding still, and that was all.

The tape was in the kit as well. I didn't take it out. His face was turned a little towards the dressing table, and I left it like that. There was nothing over his eyes and nothing over his mouth. The lesson was that he would be seen. He was going to be seen, now, by everyone. It was not the lesson.

At work we cover the face. We do it for the family, and for ourselves, because a face looking at the ceiling is hard to work beside. I left his uncovered. I didn't decide that either.

I put the pin back in my pocket.

I folded the page once. Just once, down the middle. It was a single sheet of paper, and I have folded harder things. I opened my jacket, the press assistant's jacket with the lanyard and the borrowed name, and put the page in the inside pocket, against my ribs, on the left, and closed the jacket over it. Then I kept my hand flat on the pocket. I kept it there longer than I needed to. The paper didn't move. My hand didn't either.

At home there was a page with my name on it, face-down on the desk. Now there were two I hadn't pinned.

The lesson as written had needed him alive. It had needed a man who could be asked questions, and eleven names in an agent's hands, and a story that came out in the right order, with the paper first and the body nowhere. That was the whole design. I had built it for two years. There would be no page now, and no Evaluation, and no living man to be asked anything. There would be a body on a hotel carpet and a hole in the place where the reason should have been, and somebody would have to file that.

I know who files things.

Somewhere there was a woman with a list of eleven names, or there soon would be, and she would go down it in order, and she would get to the end of it. Mine is near the end. I thought about that the way you think about weather in another city.

The folded page was the first thing in two years I had not been able to make myself do, and I didn't want to anyway.

* * *

The sound came in through the main door.

It had been there the whole time, probably. I hadn't been listening for it. Now I was. The corridor outside was filling up. There were footsteps, more than before, and voices, low and quick, the voices people use when they are working and want to sound as if they aren't. A radio close to the door said something short and was answered. Further off, through the walls and the floor, I could hear the ballroom. There was music playing, the kind they play to warm a room up, all brass and drums, and over the music a man's voice on a microphone, telling a crowd to get ready, telling them it wouldn't be long now.

Somewhere below me a room full of people was being told to welcome a man who was not coming down. His wife would be at the side of the stage by now, in the shadow of the curtain, where they always put the wife. I didn't think about her for long.

I was counting. I had been counting since I came into the room. Nobody else in the building was counting the way I was, and the time he was allowed had gone a long while ago. It had gone before he took my hand. Out in the corridor somebody said, not loudly, "We're over." Somebody else said, "Give him a second." The minute became two.

I could not hear myself breathing. Once, in another room, I had heard a man breathe as if it were happening inside my own ear. Here there was nobody breathing but me, and I couldn't hear that either. The band in the ballroom came round to the start of the same song and played it again.

The room was cold. It had been cold all evening. The air conditioning ran under the window with a low, steady hum, and I could feel it moving across the backs of my hands and the side of my neck.

I stood still. I was between him and the door. Behind me, on the far side of the room, was the service door, with the sticker across the seam where I had torn it. Someone was going to see that sticker. Not yet.

That's your door, Brandt had said. It's the only one. He had been right about that. He had been right about most things. He had said it with his chest open on a desk, and I had thanked him, and I had meant it. I thought about that for as long as it took the man on the microphone to finish his sentence, and then I put it down.

The knock came. Two knuckles, polite, the knock of someone who respected the man on the other side of the door and did not want to be the one to hurry him.

"Senator Marchetti."

I didn't move. I watched the handle. It was a long brass lever, the hotel kind, and it stayed level.

I have opened a great many doors that other people did not want to open. I had never stood on this side of one.

There was a pause. The music in the ballroom changed to something louder. The man on the microphone said a name, and the crowd answered him with a long rising noise, like a wave coming in over shingle, and went on making it.

The second knock. Firmer.

"Senator. We're at time."

I kept watching the handle. It didn't move. Nobody tried it. I could hear them, just on the other side of the wood, two of them, maybe three, shifting their weight. One of them said something I didn't catch, and another one said, quite clearly, "Standing instruction."

A different voice. "How long's he been?"

"Too long."

"Your call."

"It's not my call. It's his."

Then nothing. My hand had gone to the inside pocket again. I left it there. Just the radio, and the brass through the walls, and the air conditioning, and his cufflink on the carpet by his open hand.

I knew what they were thinking, out there, because I have stood outside enough doors. They were thinking that he was a man who had lost his brother, and that this was the one minute in his day that belonged to him, and that nobody wanted to be the one who walked in on a man like that with his head in his hands. They were thinking that he would come out in a moment and straighten his cuffs and thank them. They were good at their jobs. They were doing them.

The detail didn't open the door. Standing instruction: he was not to be disturbed. The system protected him for one more minute, and I used it.

I didn't hurry. Hurrying is how you leave things behind. I went to the chair where my case was and found the syringe where I had put it down, with the rest of the agent still in it, and I capped it and put it in my pocket. I closed the case and fastened it. I went back over the room the way I go back over a room at work, in strips, the carpet first and then the surfaces. The carpet by the chair. The table, where the page had been. The arm of the chair. The edge of the dressing table where my hip had hit it. I didn't touch him again. Whatever of mine was on him was going to stay there. I knew exactly what it was. I didn't go back for it.

I checked myself the same way. The lanyard was on its clip, the borrowed name facing out. My hair had come loose at the back and I put it up again by feel, the way I do it in the changing room at work. I straightened the jacket. Down one floor, the closet. The other jacket. Then the dock. I went through it once in my head, in order, and it was all still there, and I didn't feel anything about that either.

I touched the inside pocket once on the way past. The page was there.

The Service is very good at standing instructions. It is what they are for.

I went to the service door. I put my hand flat on it, beside the torn sticker. I didn't look to see if my hand was steady. It didn't matter to me whether it was. Through the door I could feel the stairwell, colder than the room, and hear nothing at all.

The knock came again behind me. It was harder this time. It wasn't polite any more. Somebody said his first name. Not the title. His name.

I looked back once, from the service door.

The chair on its side. Him, between the chair and the dressing table, with his palm turned up. The cufflink. The table, with nothing on it. The square of polished wood where something had stood.

The Marine's photograph was face-down on the carpet where it had fallen. I didn't turn it over. I thought about it.
