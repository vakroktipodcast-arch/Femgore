# Violence — The Core Principle, Inverted

The horror is in how much she enjoys it, and in how exactly she can describe what she enjoys. The reader does not get to imagine; the reader gets to watch, with her, and gets to feel her watching. Kills should be more disturbing on the second read because the reader will notice how happy the prose was.

- Render, don’t imply. Smell first — she is a pathologist, smell is her first sense. Then sound, texture, temperature, weight. Sight last; she looks after she knows.
- Anatomical exactness is not optional. Every cut names what it cuts. The precision is the eroticism.
- Her pleasure is on the page in every kill but one. It is physical — pulse, heat, hunger, arousal — and she reports it the way she reports a finding, which is what makes it unbearable.
- Chapter 6: on the page, enjoyed, with one line where she stops enjoying it and does not notice.
- Chapter 12: on the page, exhilarating, the one fight; she is having fun and the reader is frightened by how much.
- Chapter 17: the set piece. The longest scene. Lush, slow, tender, funny, explicit. The word beautiful is permitted here and only here, and she means it, and the reader should flinch at the sincerity. Draft this scene first if the voice is not yet found.
- Chapter 25: three paragraphs. No anatomy, no Latin, no pleasure, no jokes. She feels nothing and says so. The inverse of 17 in every choice.
- Aftermath still gets more page time than the act — but the aftermath is now the meal. She eats after she kills, enormously, and narrates it as physiology, and the reader stops believing her around Chapter 7.
- The gore is never gratuitous because it is never without her. Every explicit line is also a line about what Iris is feeling as she looks. If a sentence of violence could be lifted out without losing her, cut it.
