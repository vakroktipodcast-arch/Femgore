# Chapter 15

The stitches came out at a quarter past six on the Monday, at the bathroom basin under the fluorescent, because it was the tenth day. I keep appointments, even my own. Seven simple interrupted, four-nought nylon, the tails left long so I could find them. I am the only surgeon I know who has ever had to read her own handwriting off her own flank. I lifted each knot with the Adson forceps, slid the suture scissors under it, cut on the side nearest the skin and drew the stitch out through its own track, which is the only direction a stitch ever wants to go, and each one came with a small ticklish drag, like a hair pulled from a hem. The seam underneath was the pink of boiled ham, and shut, and a little proud at its lower end, where the skin had torn when I landed. Healing by primary intention. I put the seven black commas in a tissue and went to send an email.

At twenty to eight I was in a bus-station café on its public wifi, with my back to the dome camera over the till, the clean laptop open and an americano that tasted of the bottom of the pot and of the paper of the cup, which had got into the coffee the way the cup's shape gets into your hand. Around me people were eating eggs. I filed a complaint with the state's data-privacy regulator, anonymously, alleging unlawful interception of communications by Lantern Data Services LLC, and copied it to the abuse desk of the upstream provider that carried Lantern's line. I attached the annual report from the state's own register, the public one, with her signature at the foot. Officer of record: Diane Pell. Under it I attached her death notice. Then I sent the notice and the report again, to the bereavements desk of the bank whose statements came through his mail. Every account in that chain had one signatory and one contact, both of them her, and nobody in compliance keeps a dead woman's account open for longer than it takes to read the notice.

I had killed the officer of record, which in my experience is the fastest way to get a compliance department to return your email.

I didn't need the inquiry to succeed. I needed him dark for forty-eight hours: no feeds, no cameras, no eyes in the warehouse district or anywhere else his systems reached, starting before Tuesday night and running until well after it. The Gatekeeper was still keeping the Architect's door. She was doing it now by being dead across the threshold.

I closed the laptop, and a thought came in without knocking. I had just written to the government. Somebody else was writing to it too. E. Reyes, a signature block and an initial, had asked for every agreement under a matter number I knew by heart, and I wondered for a moment which inbox my complaint would land in, and whether one day the two would lie on the same desk. I decided it didn't matter for forty-eight hours. Forty-eight hours is a long time in a body and no time at all in a federal building. I finished the coffee, because I had paid for it, and went to work.

The suite smelled of formalin and of the floor cleaner the night staff use, which is pine, if pine had been arrested. Mara had the next table. She asked without looking up, with her blade an inch above a sternum, whether I had listened to it.

"Yes," I said, and then, because she still hadn't looked up, "Yes."

I had played it as far as a needle would have lifted, and no further, and I didn't tell her that. She said nothing else. The tops of her ears went pink, the helix first and then the lobe, the way a blush climbs when its owner is pretending it isn't there. I mark everything. I had marked the café's coffee and the regulator's online form before nine. I didn't mark that, and I didn't ask myself why.

My own case was a man of eighty-one, natural causes. The heart came out of him at four degrees, with the cooler's breath still on it, and I set it in the pan of the hanging scale and felt the cold of it through my glove the way you feel the weather through a window, a thing not yet worth a sentence. Four hundred and sixty grams. He had a big heart. It is the commonest thing in the world, and more of them die of it than of anything else I open.

At ten to five, out of the gown and out to my car in the ME's car park, I took the tablet from my work bag. The tap I had wired into Lantern's uplink sent a heartbeat every few minutes, and I had set the tablet to tell me if it ever missed one. It had missed one, at 4:41. At the same minute the two units in his house had gone dark, and a tap and two cameras have nothing in common but the line they share. So it was the line. The abuse desk had found a dead woman as its only contact and put the account on hold. Nine hours from filing. I graded the abuse desk: prompt, frightened, a B-plus, docked for not checking whether I existed.

Then the other half of it arrived, the half I had not priced in. My tap had gone dark with his line, and my eyes in his house with it. The War Room feed still ran, but Holt had seen to it that he was no longer in that room. For two days I would have no wire into him at all. I would have to see him with my own eyes, like a person. I touched my side, where the seam was.

Nothing on the syllabus had ever gone faster than I planned it. In my experience, when things go early it is because somebody at the other end didn't fight. I didn't let myself finish the thought about who.

I had more time than I needed, and I didn't trust it, and so I used it to cook. On the way home I went to the butcher's and bought a whole free-range bird with its giblets in a paper twist, and at the stove I was quick and happy. Salt went into the hot water with a sound like a breath let out through the teeth, and the water went clear. Bay, black peppercorns, three curls of lemon peel, and the kitchen smelled of a hillside somebody else owns. When the brine had cooled I put the bird in a lidded tub, breast down, and weighed it under with a plate, and it went into the fridge for Wednesday, for whoever I would be on Wednesday. The neck and wingtips went into a pot for stock. The liver I fried in butter until it was seared outside and pink in the middle, sweet and ferrous and soft as a bruise, and ate on toast standing at the counter, and that was all I ate. I am a professional. You always taste the liver first.

* * *

Herding is mostly a matter of which gates you leave open. Sheepdogs know this. So does every HR department I have ever filed a complaint with.

With the feeds gone I herded him blind, from what I already had: the dossier, eleven days of his mail, and the card I had carried out of Lantern on the night I bled on his floor. I had named every one of his companies to the regulator except one. A media consultancy he owned silently had an editing suite on the third floor of a converted printworks east of the river, and its lease, its alarm and its building cameras were paid through a holding company of their own, and the cameras routed through Lantern, so they were dark now too. Facilities mail is the least guarded mail in any company. His had given me the stairwell code and the suite keypad as plain text, the way you'd write a shopping list, and when I read the keypad code again at my own counter on Monday night the heat came up my neck and into my face the way it does at the first glass of something. The card had given me the fit-out drawing, with the cable grommets along both long edges of the edit desk, and the acoustic contractor's specification for the walls. I had shut every other door on him in order, and enjoyed each one the way you enjoy the last click of a zip-tie's ratchet, and I had left that one on the latch, the way you leave a light on for a guest who doesn't know the house.

At a quarter past ten on Monday night I rang his personal phone from a second burner. It went to voicemail, as I had meant it to. I left a message in my own voice, in the register of a business reporter from a wire service, calm and polite and a little bored, asking about Diane Pell's directorship of Lantern Data Services, and I said I would try him at the house tomorrow. I had the phrase from his own mail. It was what he wrote when he wanted somebody to stop sleeping. It put the voice I give evidence in onto a phone the police would be holding within the week, and I priced that, and paid it. I had never left a message I wanted returned less. Now his house was a place where a stranger might be standing on the step in the morning, holding a notebook.

On Tuesday morning, before my shift, I counted the car among the shut doors. His car service billed a card on the shell's account, and the shell's bank had read my notice by now, and a frozen account does not pay for cars. I would know by the evening. Holt had walled him out of the War Room on Thursday, by procedure, without a raised voice. Holt was herding for me, for nothing. He has always been good with staff.

The one thing I left alone was the Tabard Club. It was Tuesday, his night, the set from nine to eleven. I wanted to know whether, with every other door shut, he would choose the room where people listened to him or the room where nobody could.

Then I worked a whole day on competence: two cases, a report signed, my initials on every specimen label. At the sinks in the afternoon Mara hummed four bars of the Mingus, scrubbing, the tune coming out of her nose more than her mouth, and stopped in the middle of the fifth when she heard what she was doing. I didn't say anything. I didn't mark it either. That was twice.

At half past five I parked across the street from the printworks and turned off the engine, and for a while it ticked as it cooled, like a slow clock losing interest. Four storeys of soot-dark brick, the consultancy on the third. One stair served every floor, behind a column of frosted glass on the street side, with a door at its foot onto the street and another at the back onto the car park. I could not run the clap test this time. For the first time on the syllabus I was trusting somebody else's paperwork. I had read the acoustic contractor's report twice and given it a B. The seals were good. The prose was not. The tub of wipes was in the driver's door pocket, where it lives.

At ten past six the streetlights came on, the sodium warming through pink to that dense orange that makes everybody under it look embalmed. The car smelled of cold upholstery, and my skin smelled of the morgue under the soap, as it always does. I had my hands on the wheel at ten and two, and they stayed there for fifty minutes, still, bar once. I hadn't eaten since the liver. The wound pulled a little from my sitting twisted to watch the door, and my right hand came off the wheel to it, and the fingertips stayed there while I thought, and then went back.

At twenty past six, the hour he would have been sitting down to dinner before his set, a taxi pulled in across the street with its indicator going. Not his car. The shell had stopped paying for cars. My pulse went up in my throat with the indicator, on the same beat, as if the two of them were on one circuit. He got out in charcoal. He had a paper bag in one hand with the neck of a bottle showing, and under his arm a record in a card mailer. The last record of his I had touched, I had snapped in half and left on his pillow. He was bringing another. He looked up the street and down it once, not afraid, the way a man looks when he is early for a table and wonders whether she is too. Then he crossed under the sodium, and I let myself look at him the way I had looked at the heart in the pan: eighty-odd kilos, at blood heat all the way through, and under the charcoal, at the top of his breastbone, the sternal angle, the small ridge where the second ribs come in, which was where the shears would start. I stopped there.

He had a bottle. You don't bring a bottle to a place you are hiding in. You bring a bottle to a dinner.

He let himself in at the street door with his own key. Behind the frosted glass the light came on at the first landing, and then the second, and stopped at the third.

He had read the syllabus. He knew what a soundproofed room was for. He went willingly.

* * *

At seven I cleared the kitchen table and laid a clean white towel on it, and set the instruments out on the towel in the order I would want them, left to right, the way you lay a table for a tasting menu so that nobody has to ask which fork. The small handle and its blade first. The small retractor. The toothed forceps. The larger handle and its blade. The spare blades in their sleeve. The rib shears last, the handles cold enough to feel through the skin of my palm when I set them down. The rib shears were the cheese course: heavy, optional, and the real reason you book. The towel's nap took each piece with a small soft give.

Off to one side, like cutlery set out for the table, I put the rest. A box of nitrile gloves. The thin black leather gloves for the car and the suite door. The red marker. A steel pin. The black vinyl, with its dull shine. Zip-ties. Industrial tape. Two foil sleeves, one for his phone and one for my own. Ten towels, folded. The bags. I had laid a place for him at my own table, and he was not going to be there, which is the usual arrangement with my guests.

What was not on the table was a page. I had watched him on the feed find his copy on his pillow and laugh and keep it. I was going to a lesson without the lesson, and I was trusting him to bring it. I noticed that I trusted him, and I didn't take it back.

I rolled the instruments into the canvas roll the way a cook rolls her knives, tied it and set it in the bag. Then I showered. Hot and short. The forty-minute shower is for after. The seam on my flank was a line the colour of cooked prawn with seven dots either side of it, like a zip drawn on by a child. I washed around it, and then, deliberately, over it, and it took the water and the soap as if it had never been anything else.

I filed my nails short and square over the kitchen sink, where the dust could go down the drain and not onto the towel. Then I scrubbed at the same sink, with the nail brush and the soap, two minutes a hand by the clock on the cooker, palms, backs, between the fingers, every finger root to tip, up to the wrists and past them.

You don't eat before. It is a rule from a different kind of evening, and it is also, I noted, the rule for the patient and not for the surgeon. Nil by mouth is an instruction to the patient. I had never in my life followed one. At half past seven I warmed a cup of Monday's stock and salted it and drank it standing, golden, with a lens of fat across the top, and the faint sweetness the neck bones give up if you let them, and I ate one heel of yesterday's bread with it, the crust gone tough as a thumbnail and the crumb still soft enough to take the stock. When I opened the fridge for the stock I lifted the lid of the tub a finger's width, and the brine breathed out at me, bay and lemon peel and raw bird, and there was the chicken on the bottom shelf, pale under its plate, waiting for Wednesday. I put the lid down and shut the door on it. The waistband of my trousers, when I put them on, sat against a stomach that had been given almost nothing for two days, and I felt the pull of that against the wool, and I liked it. I am reporting that.

A black silk shirt with sleeves I could push to the elbow. It was cool going on, over skin that had just been scrubbed, and it hung loose over the seam. Black wool trousers. A black jacket over the silk, with a pocket deep enough for a phone. Flat shoes with a good sole, for stairs. Hair washed and pinned up tight, because I would be working, and because one hair of mine on his carpet would come home to me. There was no hair cover in this kit. You don't wear one to dinner. No jewellery. Rings tear gloves, and a woman who is going to be elbow-deep in something should not be wearing anything she'd mind losing in it.

Then lipstick, which I do not wear to work. Oxblood, and unscented, because I own nothing that smells. When I pressed my lips together it tasted of wax and nothing else. I can give you a reason for it. The mouth is the part of a face that gives least away, and a colour on it gives it something to do, so that whoever is looking at you looks there and not at your eyes. It is a good reason. I didn't believe it while I was putting it on, and I'm telling you that I didn't.

I went through the bag once more. The roll. The kit. Then the jacket: my own phone in its pocket, sealed into its foil sleeve at the counter, so that the last place it could vouch for was my kitchen and after that it would say nothing at all, which is not an alibi and is a flag of its own, and was still better than a line drawn from my flat to his door. The two codes, written nowhere, because I had them by heart. My car keys, with the drawer key on the same ring, as always. My pulse was up and my face was warm. I didn't put either of them down to nerves. I began to give the preparation a mark and stopped, because there was nobody to give it to.

On the way out, the hall mirror.

"You look like you've got a date," I told her, out loud.

I didn't correct it.

I left at a quarter past nine and parked in the rear car park at five to ten with the leather gloves on. His set was an hour old by then, and going on without him. The stair, by its code, from the car-park door. The corridor with its one working light. At the end of it, the suite's door, not quite pressed into its seals, and under it, faint, Mingus: *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*, turned down so that he would hear me knock.
