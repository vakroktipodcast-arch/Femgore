# Chapter 16

I didn't knock.

The corridor had one working light, humming the way a tube hums when it has been asked to do the work of three. Under the hum, the Mingus, coming round the edges of the door at the far end, thin, a bass line and a muttering of brass, the way a party sounds from the stairs. He had turned it down so that he would hear me knock. It was the most considerate thing a man had done for me in years, and I ignored it, which is also traditional.

The keypad took the code I had carried in my head and nowhere else. The door came away from its frame with a small sucking sound, a fridge opened at night, and swung to behind me and seated itself into its seals with a soft pressure in my ears, the way your ears go in a lift. The room had swallowed the corridor.

Smell first. Rye, opened and poured, a dry grainy sharpness at the height of the table. The warm dust that comes off equipment left on all day. The new-foam smell of the acoustic panels, faintly sweet, like a packet of earplugs. And at the height of a seated man, the dry green note I had last met on the brass pull of my own drawer, when it had been a trespass with nobody attached. Now it had a man attached. I looked last, the way I always do.

One lamp. A row of amber standby lights along the wall at knee height. The long edit desk under its monitors, matte as slate, giving nothing back. A low table, a leather chair beside it, a second chair across. In the leather chair, Brandt, in a charcoal shirt with no tie and the sleeves down, his jacket over the chair back and a glass in his hand.

He looked up.

"I wondered if it would be you."

Most men say that at a door with flowers in their hand. He had a rye in his. He said it the way you say a line you have chosen, and then he didn't add anything to it, which is the other half of choosing.

He was not afraid. I read him the way I read anyone who comes in through my doors. Pupils appropriate to the light. Hands loose on the chair arms. Respiration slow. No sweat at the hairline or the lip. His head a little on one side, like a man being shown the movement of a watch. Curious. Composure: excellent. He had been sitting here since twenty past six with that sentence in his mouth, and he delivered it as if it had just occurred to him.

Then the *if*. An *if* has an alternative in it, the way a cut has a direction. Somebody else might have come through that door first: Holt's people, now that Holt had walled him out of his own campaign. I had watched that on the feed, a chair at the end of the table that was suddenly somebody else's. He had been waiting to see which of us arrived first, and he was glad it was me. I noticed that I was pleased he was glad, and I let myself be.

The photograph was on the low table beside his glass.

It was mine. The eight-by-ten I had printed from the case file and left squared on his kitchen counter nine days before: the official close-up of Diane's mouth, the jaw open around the paper, the shredded pages showing at her lips, and in the footer, under its thin rule, half of LC-0311, which was enough. I read it the way I read a body, from the outside in. The corners had gone soft, the gloss worn through to the fibre at the tips. A crease ran across the lower third where it had been folded once and carried and opened again, many times, the line gone white. At the left edge, ghosted into the gloss, a thumbprint, his, the whorl clear enough to lift. I have seen less handling on a family Bible. I have seen less handling on some of my patients. Beside it stood the Mingus sleeve, and beside that, folded in quarters under the foot of his glass, my page.

He had kept it. He had been looking at it every day.

I have been read before. A War Room read me on a Sunday night, and I watched them do it on a recording. But being read happens to you once, over coffee, on the way to something else. I had never been kept. Nobody had ever looked at my work as long as he had looked at that photograph. Including me.

"Phone?" I said.

"Jacket. Inside left."

He leaned forward to give me the pocket, the way a man leans forward in a restaurant so the waiter can reach behind him. I took the phone out with two fingers of my glove, thin black leather, worn from the car, and slid it into the second foil sleeve from my bag. Mine was already in the first, in my jacket, asleep. If he had kept the picture from the pump station on it, and a man whose thumb stops on a screen the way his had stopped keeps things, it went dark in my hand. I folded the sleeve's mouth over twice and put it back in his pocket. I didn't need it. I needed it not to talk. He watched me do it and gave a small nod, one tradesman watching another use the right tool.

"Your reporter," he said. "Good phone voice."

"Thank you."

"Didn't fight it. The farm."

"I know."

I stood by the door longer than I needed to. My body had gone over the room before I asked it to: one other person breathing; no second door; nothing behind the charcoal baffles but more baffle. The music was low and very close, with nothing to bounce off, like music in a padded case. My pulse was up against the silk of my collar, and I took the reading and filed it, and it was not fear. I had eaten nothing since a cup of stock at half past seven, and the hunger was sharp and pleasant, high under the ribs, the way it is on the way to a dinner you booked long ago. The wound in my flank was eleven days old and had nothing to say.

Then I saw what else was on the table. Beside his glass and the photograph, set square to the table's edge the way I would have set it myself: a second glass. Heavy, clean, dry, nothing in it yet. It had been put out before I came.

He had set out two glasses.

* * *

He poured for me without asking how I took it. Two fingers, neat. He had either guessed or read it somewhere, and I had never written it down, so he had guessed, which was worse.

I took off my right glove to hold it, laid the glove across my knee, and told myself the inventory, the way you count swabs before you close: this hand would touch the glass and nothing else, and the glass would leave with me. The left glove stayed on. I sat on the arm of the second chair, not in it. Sitting in it would have been a different evening.

The glass was heavy and cool and wet at the rim, and my palm on it was the first bare skin of the night. The rye was dry. Pepper first, the char of the cask behind it, no sweetness anywhere, and the heat arriving late at the back of the tongue, the way the sting of a cut arrives after you have looked at your hand. I took the first mouthful slowly and held it, and my mouth stayed dry afterwards, which good rye does. It takes more than it gives.

He had drawn the page out from under his glass and laid it on his knee, still folded.

"Mine," he said. "Read it a lot."

"I can see."

"I read the other one too. In your drawer."

I said nothing. He didn't say the name on it. He could have; it would have cost him nothing and bought him my face for a second. He let the page be a page. I graded his restraint, and it was high.

"How did you come to it?" he said. "The pages. Not before. Not the..." He moved his hand an inch on the chair arm and put it back. "The pages."

"I reported it," I said. "I kept records. I signed, in Diane's office. Then the blind items came." I looked at him. "Yours."

He didn't look away. He didn't explain them and he didn't apologise for them, and I gave him a mark for each.

That was all I said out loud. Here is the rest, because you are not him. Two years ago, at about this time of night, at my writing desk, a sentence arrived. It came already addressed. Not *she*, not *I*. *You*. I had spent the time since the agreement writing things down in the third person for people whose job was to file them, and the first sentence I wrote for myself came out talking to somebody, and I knew who. I sat there until the first bus went past under the window, and wrote the rest.

Somewhere in the middle of my answer the side ended. I didn't hear the arm go home; my voice covered it, and his listening. I only noticed that the room had got bigger, the way a kitchen does when the fridge stops.

He was quiet for a while. Then he said, with every word set down where it belonged, "I would have done the same thing."

He didn't mean the agreement. He meant the pages.

I didn't know what to do with that, so I drank. I have a procedure for every kind of sentence a man can say to me in a room: threats, charm, lawyers, *I don't know what you're talking about*. I had come up those stairs with a joke ready for each. I had not prepared a joke for being understood. In fourteen hundred cases nobody has ever agreed with me. It isn't a thing the dead go in for.

"After the photograph," he said, "I went down the list. The signatory list, privately. I was looking for one name, and I found it, and that is how I found you. Not from your agreement. The pages in her mouth don't say whose they are. The footer is the matter, not the woman. You need the list for the woman." He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the page. "Counsel wrote the template. I want to be accurate about that. The only thing on those pages that is mine is the face. I chose the typeface. Century Schoolbook, because it looks like a judge wrote it."

Since the night of the quarter-inch I had believed that he came to me through my own agreement: one paper, one woman, one drawer. I had laid that under everything since, the way you lay a floor.

"You think you're the only one," he said.

He stopped there. He gave me the pause the way you give someone a chair.

"There are eleven names on that list."

He said it flatly. Eleven. Not *about*, not *at least*. He didn't read them to me. He didn't say which one of the eleven knew where the larynx was, because he didn't need to. She was sitting on the arm of a chair across from him with a glass in her hand.

My body got there before I did. My left hand, the gloved one, went to my flank and pressed the wound through the silk, and I watched it go the way I would watch a hand in footage. I touch it when I am thinking. I wasn't thinking. It was doing that for me.

*We were the same kind of alone.* I had thought that a month ago, watching him lay his hand flat on a pocket on a feed, and I had liked it so much that I kept it. It came back now for the length of a clause and fell over. Eleven women had signed what I signed.

My face was hot. Not cold; I want that on the record. The heat came up out of my collar and stayed, the way it does when you have been caught at something in a room full of people, and there was only one person in the room.

He looked as if he would say something more. His mouth began it. Then he decided, and I watched him decide, *not yet*, and let it go, and put his glass down on the page instead.

I had believed myself alone. I had built two years on being the only one, and every page in that drawer stood on it, and he had just told me the floor was a list. He watched it land. He was kind about it. He got up out of the leather chair and turned his back on me, so that I could have my face to myself, and went to the turntable and lifted the arm and set the needle down at the start of side one again. The lead-in groove crackled like a fire being lit in another room. He turned it up a notch, so that neither of us would have to say anything for a while. He did it without being asked. It cost him nothing he would ever need again, and that was what made it unbearable.

* * *

The side ran out again a little after eleven, and the quiet after it was my cue.

I put my glass down beside the photograph and drew the right glove back on. I took off my jacket, with my own phone asleep in its foil in the pocket, and hung it over the back of the second chair. Then I stood up and cleared his desk.

The long edit desk was ninety-six inches, which is two hundred and forty-four centimetres. I always do the conversion. That night the furniture was going to hold a body, and I wanted them in the same units. The keyboards went down on the carpet along the wall, the jog wheel beside them, the coiled cables laid down in loops the way you lay down a hose. I pushed the monitors back on their arms, dark, showing nothing. The cable grommets ran along the front edge exactly where the floor plan had put them. It is a deep pleasure when a building agrees with its paperwork. Ask anyone who has bought a house how rarely it does.

I unrolled the instrument roll on the low table between the photograph and the two glasses. Small handle and blade, retractor, toothed forceps, large handle, spare blades in their sleeve, and the rib shears last, heavy, the cheese course. The box of nitrile and the towels in their bags went on the floor beside the second chair. The tasting menu, served.

He came to look at it with his hands in his pockets. He stood over it the way a man reads a wine list he can't afford, which, in fairness, he couldn't. He looked at each piece in order and didn't ask what any of it was for. Not one question. I noticed, and I graded it, and it was the highest mark I had given him yet.

"On the desk," I said. "On your back."

"One thing first."

He went to the turntable and lifted the arm and put the needle back at the start of side one, a third time. I let him. I wouldn't have known how. Then he sat on the edge of the desk, swung his legs up and lay down without being helped, his head towards the lamp, and laid his arms out along the edges of the desk, palms up, and waited. A man on a desk with room to spare at both ends. I have never in my life been so well supplied with margin.

I unbuttoned his left cuff and rolled the sleeve to the elbow, two neat turns and a third to lock it, and then the right. I like the forearms bare. That is as far as I will go about it here.

Then the tape, industrial, round each wrist, palm up, and down to the edge of the desk. Coming off the roll in that room it was enormous, a long rip like canvas, and the room kept every inch of it. Then a zip-tie round each wrist, through a grommet and back, and the ratchet went under the Mingus with its small quick sound, a watch being wound. The ankles the same, at the far end. A band of industrial tape across his thighs, which was thoroughness and not need. I kept the leather gloves on for all of it. Tape adhesive takes a print better than glass does.

He tested each tie as I finished it, a small courteous flex against the plastic, the way a man tries a door after he has locked it for you, to show you that it holds. I have tied a great many tags to a great many toes. None of them had ever helped.

His hands lay open on the wood, the fingers a little spread, the way hands rest on a closed lid. I looked at them for longer than I needed to, and I didn't touch them.

The smell had changed now that he was lying down. Rye on both our breaths, and the dry green note coming up off him into the heat of the lamp, no longer at the height of a seated man but at the height of a table. The height of my work.

"Take your time," he said. His voice was level and every word in it was in its place. "I would rather it was you than anyone else."

Nobody has ever said anything like that to me. I want to say it plainly. Not a lover; I have not had the kind that talk. Not Hannah, who loves me and wants me to take a holiday. Not one colleague in fifteen years of corridors, and not one of the fourteen hundred, who never said anything to me in any form. It is what you say to a woman you are in no hurry to be finished with. I had heard it said in films. I had assumed it was a special effect.

I was going to take him at his word.

I took off the leather gloves a finger at a time and put them in the pocket of my jacket on the second chair. They were the last thing I had come in wearing that was going to come off, and I would not need them again that night. The nitrile was in its box. His jacket hung on the leather chair with his phone asleep in its pocket, and I didn't look at it again.

My pulse was high and it was unhurried, which is the best combination there is and the rarest; you find it in surgeons and in almost nobody else. My face was hot. Lower down there was the other finding, frank and plain, and I noted it in the same hand I note everything, and went on. And I was hungry, and I told myself it was because I hadn't eaten.

It was going to be the best night of my life. I knew that much, standing over him with my hands bare and the lamp behind his head. That was all I knew. I didn't know that the woman who had keyed in the code at a quarter past ten was not the woman who would walk out. They would use the same door, and the same stairs, and the same car, and neither of them would be told.

The record ended. The arm lifted and went home by itself, and in that room we could both hear it settle, and neither of us moved to change it. I rolled up my sleeves.
