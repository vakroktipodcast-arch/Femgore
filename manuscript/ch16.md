# Chapter 16

I didn't knock.

The corridor had one working light, humming the way a tube hums when it has been asked to do the work of three. Under the hum, the Mingus, coming round the edges of the door at the far end, thin, a bass line and a muttering of brass, the way a party sounds from the stairs. He had turned it down so that he would hear me knock. It was the most considerate thing a man had done for me in years, and I ignored it, which is also traditional.

The keypad took the code I had carried in my head and nowhere else. The door came away from its frame with a small sucking sound, a fridge opened at night, and swung to behind me and seated itself into its seals with a soft pressure in my ears, the way your ears go in a lift.

The room reached me before I looked at it. Rye, opened and poured, a dry grainy sharpness at the height of the table. The warm dust that comes off equipment left on all day. The new-foam smell of the acoustic panels, faintly sweet, like a packet of earplugs. And at the height of a seated man, the dry green note I had last met at his kitchen sink, worked into the webs of my own fingers. Then it had been a bar of soap. Now it was sitting down.

One lamp. A row of amber standby lights along the wall at knee height. The long edit desk under its monitors, matte as slate, giving nothing back. A low table, a leather chair beside it, a second chair across. In the leather chair, Brandt, in a charcoal shirt with no tie and the sleeves down, his jacket over the chair back and a glass in his hand.

He looked up.

"I wondered if it would be you."

Most men say that at a door with flowers in their hand. He had a rye in his. He said it the way you say a line you have chosen, and then he didn't add anything to it, which is the other half of choosing.

He was not afraid. I read him the way I read anyone who comes in through my doors. Pupils appropriate to the light. Hands loose on the chair arms. Respiration slow. No sweat at the hairline or the lip. His head a little on one side, like a man being shown the movement of a watch. Curious. Composure: excellent. If he had been turning that sentence over all evening, it didn't show; he delivered it as if it had just occurred to him.

Then the *if*. An *if* has an alternative in it, the way a cut has a direction. Somebody else might have come through that door first: Holt's people, now that Holt had walled him out of his own campaign. He had been waiting to see which of us arrived first, and he was glad it was me. I noticed that I was pleased he was glad, and I let myself be.

The photograph was on the low table beside his glass.

It was mine. The eight-by-ten I had printed from the case file and left squared on his kitchen counter nine days before: the official close-up of Diane's mouth, the shredded pages showing at her lips, and in the footer, under its thin rule, half of LC-0311, which was enough. I read it the way I read a body, from the outside in. The corners had gone soft, the gloss worn through to the fibre at the tips. A crease ran across the lower third where it had been folded once and opened many times, the line gone white. At the left edge, ghosted into the gloss, a thumbprint, his, the whorl clear enough to lift. I have seen less handling on a family Bible. I have seen less handling on some of my patients. Beside it stood the Mingus sleeve, and beside that, folded in quarters under the foot of his glass, my page.

He had kept it. He had been looking at it every day.

A War Room has read me, on a recording, on a Sunday night. Nobody had ever kept me. Nobody had ever looked at my work as long as he had looked at that photograph. Including me.

"Phone?" I said.

"Jacket. Inside left."

He leaned forward to give me the pocket, the way a man does in a restaurant so the waiter can reach behind him. I took the phone out with two fingers of my glove, thin black leather, worn from the car, and slid it into the second foil sleeve from my bag. Mine was already in the first, in my jacket, asleep. Whatever he had kept on it went deaf in my hand. I folded the sleeve's mouth over twice and put it back in his pocket. I didn't need it. I needed it not to talk. He watched me do it and gave a small nod, one tradesman watching another use the right tool.

I stood by the door and let my body go over the room. One other person breathing; no second door; nothing behind the charcoal baffles but more baffle. The music was low and very close, with nothing to bounce off, like music in a padded case. My pulse was up against the silk of my collar, and I took the reading and filed it, and it was not fear. I had eaten nothing since a cup of stock and a heel of bread at half past seven, and the hunger was sharp and pleasant, high under the ribs, the way it is on the way to a dinner you booked long ago. The wound in my flank was eleven days old and had nothing to say.

Beside his glass and the photograph, set square to the table's edge the way I would have set it myself, stood a second glass. Heavy, clean, dry, nothing in it yet. It had been put out before I came.

He had set out two glasses.

* * *

He poured for me without asking how I took it. Two fingers, neat. He had either guessed or read it somewhere, and I had never written it down, so he had guessed, which was worse.

I took off my right glove to hold it, laid the glove across my knee, and told myself the inventory, the way you count swabs before you close: this hand would touch the glass and nothing else, and the glass would leave with me. The left glove stayed on. I sat on the arm of the second chair, not in it. Sitting in it would have been a different evening.

The glass was heavy and cool and wet at the rim, and my palm on it was the first bare skin of the night. The rye was dry. Pepper first, the char of the cask behind it, no sweetness anywhere, and the heat arriving late at the back of the tongue, the way the sting of a cut arrives after you have looked at your hand. I took the first mouthful slowly and held it, and my mouth stayed dry afterwards, which good rye does. It takes more than it gives.

He had drawn the page out from under his glass and laid it on his knee, still folded.

"Mine," he said. "Read it a lot."

"I can see."

He put it back on the table and stood his glass on it again, in the same place, as if the place had been measured.

"I read the other one too. In your drawer."

I said nothing. He didn't say what was at the head of it. He could have; it would have bought him my face for a second. He let the page be a page. I graded his restraint, and it was high.

"How did you come to it?" he said. "The pages. Not before. Not the..." He moved his hand an inch on the chair arm and put it back. "The pages."

"I reported it," I said. "I kept records. I signed, in Diane's office. Then the blind items came." I looked at him. "Yours."

He didn't look away. He didn't explain them and he didn't apologise for them, and I gave him a mark for each.

That was all I said out loud. Here is the rest, since you have been through my drawer as well. Two years ago, at about this time of night, at my writing desk, a sentence arrived. It came already addressed. Not *she*, not *I*. *You*. I knew who. I sat there until the first bus went past under the window, and kept writing.

Somewhere in the middle of my answer the side ended. I didn't hear the arm go home; my voice covered it, and his listening. I only noticed that the room had got bigger, the way a kitchen does when the fridge stops.

He was quiet for a while. Then he said, with every word set down where it belonged, "I would have done the same thing."

He didn't mean the agreement. He meant the pages.

I didn't know what to do with that, so I drank. I have a procedure for every kind of sentence a man can say to me in a room: threats, charm, lawyers, *I don't know what you're talking about*. I had come up those stairs with a joke ready for each. I had not prepared a joke for being understood. In fourteen hundred cases nobody has ever agreed with me. It isn't a thing the dead go in for.

"After the photograph," he said, "I went down the list. The signatory list, privately. I was looking for one name, and I found it, and that is how I found you. Not from your agreement. The pages in her mouth don't say whose they are. The footer is the matter, not the woman. You need the list for the woman." He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the page. "Counsel wrote the template. I want to be accurate about that. The only thing on those pages that is mine is the face. I chose the typeface. Century Schoolbook, because it looks like a judge wrote it."

"You think you're the only one," he said.

He stopped there. He gave me the pause the way you give someone a chair.

"There are eleven names on that list."

He said it flatly. Eleven. Not *about*, not *at least*. He didn't read them to me. He didn't say which one of the eleven knew where the larynx was, because he didn't need to. She was sitting on the arm of a chair across from him with a glass in her hand.

My left hand, the gloved one, went to my flank before I did, and pressed the wound through the silk, and I watched it go the way I would watch a hand in footage. I touch it when I am thinking. I wasn't thinking. It was doing that for me.

*We were the same kind of alone.* I had thought that a month ago, and liked it so much that I kept it. It came back now for the length of a clause and fell over. Ten others had signed what I signed.

My face was hot. The heat came up out of my collar and stayed, the way it does when you have been caught at something in a room full of people, and there was only one person in the room.

He looked as if he would say something more. His mouth began it. Then he decided, and I watched him decide, *not yet*, and let it go, and put his glass down on the page instead.

I had believed myself alone. I had built two years on being the only one, and every page in that drawer stood on it, and he had just told me the floor was a list. He watched it go in. He was kind about it. He got up out of the leather chair and turned his back on me, so that I could have my face to myself, and went to the turntable and lifted the arm and set the needle down at the start of side one again. The lead-in groove crackled like a fire being lit in another room. He turned it up a notch, so that neither of us would have to say anything for a while. He did it without being asked. It cost him nothing he would ever need again, and that was what made it unbearable.

* * *

The side ran out again a little after eleven, and the quiet after it was my cue.

I put my glass down beside the photograph and drew the right glove back on. I took off my jacket, with my own phone asleep in its foil in the pocket, and hung it over the back of the second chair. Then I stood up and cleared his desk.

The long edit desk was ninety-six inches, which is two hundred and forty-four centimetres. I always do the conversion. That night the furniture was going to hold a body, and I wanted them in the same units. The keyboards and the jog wheel went down on the carpet along the wall, the coiled cables laid down in loops the way you lay down a hose. I pushed the monitors back on their arms, dark, showing nothing. The cable grommets ran along both long edges, a cable-tray rail under each, exactly where the fit-out drawing in his facilities attachments had put them. It is a deep pleasure when a building agrees with its paperwork. Ask anyone who has bought a house how rarely it does.

I unrolled the instrument roll on the low table between the photograph and the two glasses and fitted the blades there: a 10 onto the No. 3 handle, a 22 onto the No. 4, each slid down its bayonet with the forceps until it seated with a click. Then the small retractor, the toothed forceps, the spare blades in their sleeve, and the rib shears last, heavy, the cheese course. The box of nitrile and the towels in their bags went on the floor beside the second chair. The tasting menu, served.

He came to look at it with his hands in his pockets. He stood over it the way a man reads a wine list he can't afford, which, in fairness, he couldn't. He looked at each piece in order and didn't ask what any of it was for. Not one question. I noticed, and I graded it, and it was the highest mark I had given him yet.

"On the desk," I said. "On your back."

"One thing first."

He went to the turntable and lifted the arm and put the needle back at the start of side one, a third time. I let him. I wouldn't have known how. Then he sat on the edge of the desk, swung his legs up and lay down without being helped, his head towards the lamp, and laid his arms out along the edges of the desk, palms up, and waited. A man on a desk with room to spare at both ends. I have never in my life been so well supplied with margin.

I unbuttoned his left cuff and rolled the sleeve to the elbow, two neat turns and a third to lock it, and then the right. His forearms were long and lean, a little dark hair along the ulnar border and none on the volar skin, which was pale and fine as good paper, and when he settled his hands the tendons stood up in a fan at each wrist. I wanted my mouth on the inside of his wrist, there, over the tendons, and I wanted it the way I want to eat.

Then the tape, industrial, round each wrist, palm up, and down to the edge of the desk. Coming off the roll in that room it was enormous, a long rip like canvas, and the room kept every inch of it. Then the zip-ties: at each wrist down through a grommet and round the steel rail of the cable tray beneath, because a tie through a hole is tied to nothing. Halfway round his left wrist I thought of the other ten, ten signatures I would never see, and I folded the thought once along its length and laid it under the work, the way you fold a towel under a limb to keep the field dry. My hands didn't stop for it. Each ratchet went under the Mingus with its small quick sound, a watch being wound. The ankles to the legs at the far end, taped and tied. A band of industrial tape across his thighs, which was thoroughness and not need. I kept the leather gloves on for all of it. Tape adhesive takes a print better than glass does.

He tested each tie as I finished it, a small courteous flex against the plastic, the way a man tries a door after he has locked it for you, to show you that it holds. I have read a great many tags on a great many toes. None of them had ever helped.

His hands lay open on the wood, the fingers a little spread, the way hands rest on a closed lid. I looked at them for longer than I needed to, and I didn't touch them.

The smell had changed now that he was lying down. Rye on both our breaths, and the dry green note coming up off him into the heat of the lamp, no longer at the height of a seated man but at the height of a table. The height of my work. I read him from the top, the way I read anyone on my table before I begin: the suprasternal notch in the open collar, a shadowed well above the manubrium, and beside it the carotid lifting the skin of his neck, slow and even, a man at rest.

My pulse was high and it was unhurried, which is the best combination there is and the rarest; you find it in surgeons and in almost nobody else. My face was hot and my knickers were wet, and I noted both in the same hand I note everything in, and went on. And I was hungry. I had not eaten since the bread, and that was not why.

"Take your time," he said. "I would rather it was you than anyone else."

Nobody has ever said anything like that to me. I want to say it plainly. Not a lover; the ones who talked never talked like this. Not Hannah, who loves me and wants me to take a holiday. Not one colleague in fifteen years of corridors, and not one of the fourteen hundred, who never said anything to me in any form. It is what you say to a woman you are in no hurry to be finished with. I had heard it said in films. I had assumed it was a special effect.

I was going to take him at his word. I wanted the night to be long. I wanted it to outlast me. You came up the stairs with me, and you could go back down them now, and you are still in the room.

I took off the leather gloves a finger at a time and put them in my bag. They were the last thing I had come in wearing that was going to come off. The nitrile was in its box. His jacket hung on the leather chair with his phone asleep in its pocket, and I didn't look at it again.

The record ended. The arm lifted and went home by itself, and in that room we could both hear it settle, and neither of us moved to change it. I rolled up my sleeves.
