# Chapter 8

I had been nearer to her than this. On Monday morning, first case, half past eight, I stood on the far side of the table, which is the observer's side, where Mara had stood the week before, gowned and ungloved with my hands clasped behind my back like a woman in a gallery who has been told not to touch the frames. I had never stood at a table with my hands doing nothing. They were perfectly still. Mara had the near side and the knife. Diane lay on the steel under the overhead lights with her arms at her sides, and the suite smelled the way it always does, of chlorhexidine and cold iron under the roar of the extraction fan, and under that, faintly, absurdly, of freesia. A drugstore shampoo. Hair keeps a smell longer than a face keeps an expression. I have never found that in a textbook, and I have never once been wrong about it.

Most people have to die before they get a retrospective. I got mine on a Monday, from the other side of the table, and the subject was the one who'd had to die.

The campaign had got into the building before either of us. Through the office door, on my way in, the break-room television had been running it in captions with the sound down. *A senseless act. Our hearts are with Olivia and the whole Pell family. We ask for privacy at this time.* At six, on my phone, I had read it as grief and marked it C-minus. Read as work it was something else. Three sentences, and not a word in them a lawyer could object to. The shortest one first, to stop the eye. The daughter's name set in the second, exactly where the eye lands once it has stopped. Privacy asked for in the third as if it were being offered. I knew whose syntax it was the way you know a colleague's sutures from across the room. Brandt. Pass with distinction.

Mara began at the outside, as I taught her. I read along with her, as a critic reads.

What was elegant: there was no wasted bruise anywhere on her. None of the defence injuries a struggle writes over a body in a hurry. Every mark on her was a mark I had meant. What was rushed: an abrasion about two centimetres across over the left zygoma, where her face had met the concrete at the capture, because I had been watching her hands and not the floor. And at the wrists. Mara found the ligature furrows at both wrists and both ankles, and measured and photographed each one with the scale beside it, and the left was deeper. I had pulled the left tie one notch tighter than the right. The ties were not in the suite to be judged; they had gone wherever the rest of Sunday went. I noted it the way I would note it in a registrar. Not wrong. Not even. You can always tell the side someone started on.

"The restraints were removed after death," Mara said, into the dictaphone. "Nothing recovered at the scene. Nothing came in with the body. They were taken."

I listened to that the way you listen to a correct answer to a question you set yourself.

Then she lifted the black vinyl from the eyes. It came away from the left temple with the lashes in it, a dark fringe stuck along the adhesive like bristles left by a cheap brush, and behind it a residue on the lids and in the brows, tacky, gritted grey with dust from the pump-station floor. I had anticipated the lashes. I had not anticipated the residue. Mara worked it off with solvent in small circles, and each swab went into its own bag. I ran my list behind my back, on my own fingers: gloves, sleeves, hair. Nothing of mine in it. I ran it again anyway. Every pathologist in this office has an elimination profile on file, so that if a trace of one of us turns up on a body it can be written off as us. It is the only filing system in the building designed to make you disappear, and I had never once been grateful for it.

She found the adhesive line across the lower face, the colour of dishwater, and the torn hairs at the nape, and a band of residue at each ankle, where a tape had been and was no longer. About the face she said nothing. She photographed it. Then she took the paper out of the mouth with forceps, a little at a time, grey and soft and swollen with saliva, like bread left in the sink, and every fragment went into its own evidence bag, each label got its three strips of surgical tape, and the forceps ticked on the steel tray between mouthfuls. On some of them, under a thin rule, the footer: LC-0311. I watched them go into the tray for the people who read paper. I approved of the chain of custody. I want that on the record as approval of the chain of custody.

Inside, briefly. Petechiae in the conjunctivae under the lids where the vinyl had been, pinpricks of ink through blotting paper, exactly where I had bet on them. The congestion of a face that has run out of air. Over the manubrium, a small puncture with no vital reaction round it at all: the pin had gone in after. The page itself had been taken at the scene and was in an evidence store across the city, and I found that I missed it. Her Y was clean and her hands were quick. When she reflected the diaphragm I looked at the crura for slightly longer than the case required.

Then the stomach. She opened it along the greater curve and the smell came up, sour and sweet, and there in a thin residue, mostly gone on, were the blueberry skins, inky and split, with the grey of porridge and the dark of coffee. I had assumed breakfast at about seven. I had assumed right. My mouth filled. It came all at once, under the tongue, the way it does when someone opens an oven in the next room, and I noted it as a fact about the hours: nothing since three in the morning, and that was a long time ago.

Then the neck. She took it layer by layer, in a bloodless field, as you must, and found them at the upper border of the thyroid cartilage, one on each side. Two small bruises, symmetrical, each the size of a fingertip. The hyoid was intact. No more force had been used than was needed. They were the print of two fingers of my left hand.

Mara was quiet for a while. The fan went on. Then she said, carefully, as if setting something down that might spill, "Whoever did this has an unusual level of anatomical knowledge. They knew exactly where to put their hands."

"Yes," I said. "It looks that way."

I said it the way you'd say it looked like rain, to someone holding an umbrella. Underneath I was having a different morning. Someone had looked at my work, at the whole of it, and seen what it was. In fifteen years it was the only review I had ever had from someone qualified to give it.

She picked up the pen to write the finding on the worksheet, and stopped before the word. One beat, a stress and a fall, and I heard Diane in it, the hesitation in the pump station before the word I put the paper in front of and never got to hear. This time the word came. *Practised.* Mara wrote it down. It was in the file now. I was proud of her. I had a mark ready, and I didn't give it. At the foot of the worksheet was the reviewer's line, where my signature would go, under her word, and I was in the file.

* * *

I ate standing up, because I was planning. Strong tea with milk, which I made and then forgot, so that it stewed to the colour of a saddle and came up bitter at the back of the tongue. Rye toast, dark and sour, with salted butter put on while it was still hot enough to take it, so that the butter went in rather than on. Two anchovies from a tin, laid across it, the oil running into the butter, salt on salt, the fish soft and faintly rotten in the way that only good things are allowed to be. Outside the window the light was going sodium. I was hungry again. I put it down to twelve hours of sleep and a day with nothing in it but one meal at three in the morning, which is arithmetic, and which I am very good at.

The key was on the ring with my car key. The drawer slid out with its long wooden sigh. I took Lesson Two out of the sleeve and put it on the desk under the lamp and read it for an hour.

I am not going to give it to you yet. I will tell you only that it reads well under a lamp at night, that it is the most careful thing I have ever written, and that the Evaluation at the foot of it still held when I got there: *Mistook clarity for cruelty.* I read it the way you read a good case history, for the order in which it tells you things.

Then I lifted Diane's dossier off the stack and put it to one side, finished, and took up his, because the file I am working lives on top, squared to the knot in the drawer floor. I opened it beside the page.

It is two files, really, one laid on the other. The original is two years old: typed sheets, long-lens photographs, my pencil in the margins. Over it, later, in ballpoint, the additions: the divorce, the house sale that didn't happen, the records. At the back, held with a bulldog clip, the blind items he had planted about me three years ago. I didn't reread them. I know them by heart. I am telling you that flatly because there is no other way to tell you.

He was a good case. Mingus, Dolphy, Coltrane, on vinyl and nothing else. Tuesday nights at the Tabard Club, a private club, where he played the piano from nine to eleven for nobody in particular. Charcoal suits, every day, so that you would have to look at him to know which one. And the divorce, which he did not talk about. He didn't talk about it, which is how I knew it was the most important thing in the file. People talk about their kidneys right up until the kidneys are the problem.

There was a long-lens photograph of him leaving the Tabard on a Tuesday just after eleven, collar up, under the light over the door. I looked at his hands in it for longer than I needed to. One was in his coat pocket. The other was at his side with the fingers a little curled, the way hands are when they have been doing something for two hours and haven't finished being shaped by it.

*The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady* was on his list. I have never owned a record. I found it and played it through the laptop's speakers, and it came out small and tinny and somewhere else, a big band heard through a wall. I turned it off after a few minutes. It was like having a menu read to you when you're hungry. Accurate, and beside the point. The silence after it was better, and I sat in it with my feet cold on the floorboards and his file open in front of me.

I will tell you this straight, because it is a fact and I tell you the facts. I was interested in him in a way I had never been interested in Diane. Diane was a file. Brandt was a reader. He was the one who would read the page and see what it was doing. That was all. I moved on.

I weighed the two dossiers, one in each hand, the way I'd weigh a pair of kidneys. Diane's was thicker. Two years of Sundays will do that. His was lighter and more handled: the corners gone soft as felt, the pencil gone glossy where my thumb had been over it and over it, a shine on the graphite like the shine on a stair rail. I graded my own research. Pell: thorough. Brandt: thorough, and more than that. I gave his file the higher mark for care. I did not ask myself why I had taken more care. It was a grade, and I gave it.

Beside the photograph of him at the Tabard, in the margin, there was a note in pencil in my handwriting.

*the only one who'll understand it.*

I didn't remember writing it. I aged it the way I'd age a bruise. The slope of the hand was more upright than mine is now; I have leaned forward since. The graphite was soft and had smudged under the heel of a hand, two years of the drawer opening and shutting on it. I can age a bruise by its colour to within a day. My own handwriting I could only get to within a year, which tells you which of the two I had spent more time studying. It was mine, and it was two years old, and I could not find the night I had written it.

* * *

The recording was from Sunday night. I hadn't watched it. At 11:52 on Sunday I had been asleep, about ten hours into twelve, and when I woke I had cooked, and then I had dressed for work, and the laptop had stayed shut on the desk. I had saved it the way you save the crackling. I'm telling you that and moving on.

So it was late on Monday when I sat on the bed in the dark with the clean laptop warm on my thighs and a mug of tea going cold on the floor beside me, and opened it.

There are two units in that room, bought for cash and fixed with the same black vinyl. The one I like best sits in the bezel of the wall screen, wide on the glass-walled suite on the thirty-first floor and facing the table. Whenever anybody looks at the screen, they look into my lens. The cost of that is that the screen itself is behind me. I can never see what is on it. I can only watch faces turn to it.

The timestamp in the corner opened at about 11:40. There were a dozen of them, and nobody was sitting, and every face was lit from below by a phone, blue, the way you light a face when you want to frighten a child with a story. The sound came through the laptop's speakers flattened and a fraction late, voices over the hum of the air system. Comms wanted a statement by six. Counsel said that nobody should say anything, and then said it again, and by the end of the twelve minutes had said it four times, which is three more times than counsel says anything without billing for it.

I graded the room as a body, by what it did and in what order. First denial: people saying it couldn't be her, while holding the phones that told them it was. Then noise: everybody talking to the person beside them and nobody listening, which is the room's equivalent of a racing pulse. Then the search for someone senior, heads lifting and turning towards the door, the way a body in shock pulls its blood in towards whatever it thinks will keep it alive. Twelve people, and between them the anatomical knowledge of a butcher's calendar. They did not know what they were looking at. They knew only that it was bad.

Then the photograph came up on the wall screen. I knew it from the faces. Every one of them lifted towards me at once, and stayed there, and I watched a dozen people read my page without being able to see it myself, which is the way, I suppose, that most writers get their reviews. And then the phones came up. They photographed the screen, at an angle, from where they stood, the way tourists photograph the Mona Lisa: not to see it, but to prove they were in the room with it. Nobody yet knew what it was. My page was being copied all over the room by people who thought they were keeping evidence. As a professional observation, I noted how fast the campaign leaked. It leaked at about the speed of light. I was pleased, and I put that under professional observation too.

Somebody said his name into a phone. After that the room held itself the way a room does when the person who decides is on his way. Somebody said *activist*. Somebody else said *lunatic*. Neither of them had the anatomy to back it up.

He came in at 11:52 by the timestamp. Charcoal suit, no tie, which told me he had come from home. I switched to the unit under the credenza lip by the urn, low and close, to watch him take a coffee from it without looking at it, a hand that knew where the cup was, and then I switched back to the bezel. I checked the file against the man. Posture: upright, unhurried, the shoulders down. Hands: one round the cup, the other loose. The room: it rearranged itself around him without anybody moving very much, the way iron filings rearrange themselves when you bring the magnet in under the paper. He was exactly as I had written him. I was pleased about that as well.

"Phone," he said. "Not the wall."

Someone killed the screen. The glare went out of the room, and after that the faces were lit by their phones alone. He took his out and looked at it. The bezel had him from slightly above. I could see his face. I could not see what was on his screen.

He went still.

I timed it, because I time things. The coffee in his other hand. His thumb on the glass, not moving. No blink that I could find, and I went looking. I know exactly how long that page takes to read, because I wrote it: under a minute, read slowly, read twice if you are slow. Nobody reads it for two minutes. He stood in the middle of that room with a dozen people talking round him and did not move for two minutes and eleven seconds, a man in charcoal standing as still as a record on a shelf. The only separate sound in all of it was a phone set face down on the table somewhere behind him, a small *tock*, like a knuckle on wood.

Then someone off camera said, "Cam?"

I paused it before he answered.

I didn't know why he had gone still. I want to be exact about that, since I have been exact about everything else. I had no theory, and I didn't make one up. I had his file by heart and his hands in a photograph and his syntax on a television in the break room, and none of it told me what I had just watched. It was the first thing on the feed I couldn't read.

I rewound the feed and watched him go still again. Then once more. The third time, my own thumb had stopped on the trackpad, and I left it there.
