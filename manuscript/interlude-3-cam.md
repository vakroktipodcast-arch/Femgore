# Interlude — Cam

Twenty-six minutes past twelve. The front door, then the kitchen.

The print was on the counter beside the kettle, squared to the edge. Diane's mouth, half the matter number in the footer. Sharper than the copy on his phone. Hers.

He stood with his keys still in his hand. He did not open the fridge or look into the front room. He went to the bedroom and turned on the lamp.

The record was on his own pillow, where his head went. Two halves, a hairline apart, the sleeve beside them. Dolphy. *Out to Lunch!* His own.

He sat on his side of the bed and fitted the halves together. They met with a small tick. Under his thumb, a groove along the break. Cut first, then snapped. Not temper. Care. Anyone can break a record. It takes a steady hand to break one so it fits back together.

He sat like that a long time, doing for himself what he had done for other men for twenty years: who knew, what it cost, what came next. The sum came out the same every time.

He put on the reading glasses with the taped arm and picked up the licence. Dale Garrity, forty-three, a beard, a small red heart. The name meant nothing; he had paid not to be told it. The heavy one, then. The licence lay a quarter-inch to the left of true. His own measure, given back. Even.

Then the page, folded in quarters. Lesson Two. The Architect. He read it once, to the foot. *Mistook clarity for cruelty.*

He laughed, his head going back, too loud for the room. He did not know what at. Fair mark. He did not turn it over.

The page, refolded along her creases, went into his inside pocket with the phone, the halves and the licence into the sleeve, the sleeve back onto its shelf. Twenty to one, a rye from the tray. It did not count; a drink after midnight belonged to the day before. He played side one of the Black Saint from the chair by the piano, not touching the piano, and the tone arm lifted and went home. He slept on top of the covers in his clothes.

Monday, in front of the room, he told Theo Akers he had leaked the photograph. The boy went white and said he had deleted it, like he was told. Cam knew where the print had come from. It was the first thing in twenty years he had done without managing it.

He was home before eleven, and at eleven-forty he poured a rye at the counter beside the print. First drink before noon in nine years. He noted the time the way he would note another man's. It tasted thinner in daylight.

Monday to Wednesday he stood at the table and carried coffee he did not drink. Every night, side one only.

Wednesday evening he asked Holt for a minute. Holt walked him out of the glass room with a hand on his shoulder. Cam said there was a problem from the old files.

"I thought Diane closed those," Holt said. Then he asked about the numbers in the suburbs, and listened with every sign of interest.

Cam had written that exit for other men. He knew the next line, and it came. Holt went back after four minutes. Cam gave it two more to put his face in order, then went back and stood.

Thursday his card failed at the door, twice. A man from IT let him in with a smile that was sorry about something. Three refused logins, the last a key at a time. Through the glass Holt did not look up, which took real skill. The staff channel was gone from his phone. Nobody told him. Moved, a little at a time.

Then the farm went dark, a reporter's message promised to try him at the house, and the car service froze his card. Every shut door pointed the same way. He knew the hand. Vale's.

Tuesday. Not the Tabard. He showered and shaved and found the nick at the angle of his jaw with a fingertip. The charcoal shirt. Page and phone in the left inside pocket, the print folded once in the right. A taxi at five to six, a bottle in a paper bag, the record in a card mailer.

He let the suite door latch and lock and did not press it into its seals. The room took the sound of his feet and gave none of it back. He laid out the print and the page, poured a rye, and set the glass on the page. He put the record on and turned it almost to nothing, so that he would hear a knock. He did not know when it would come. The glass was already leaving its ring.

In the quiet he did the other sum, the one he had been putting off since the pillow, and it came out whole. She had stood in the dark at the place where his head went, and what she had done there was break a record so that it would fit back together. She could have killed him in his sleep, and she had chosen to warn him instead. She wanted him to understand first, and he found, to his surprise, that he wanted to.
