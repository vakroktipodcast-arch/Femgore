# Interlude — Cam

The print was on the counter beside the kettle, squared to the edge. Twenty-six minutes past twelve, his keys still in his hand. Diane's mouth, half the matter number in the footer. Sharper than the copy on his phone. Hers.

He stood and looked at it a long time. Not off the channel. Off the file, and her name was on the file. The house was warm, the heating still on its timer, and nothing in the kitchen was out of place but the one thing, and that was squared better than he would have squared it.

He did not open the fridge or look into the front room. He went to the bedroom and turned on the lamp.

The bed smelled faintly cold and clean. Not of him. The record was on his own pillow, where his head went. Two halves, a hairline apart, the sleeve beside them. Dolphy. *Out to Lunch!* His own.

He sat on his side of the bed and fitted the halves together. They met with a small tick. Under his thumb, a groove along the break. Cut first, then snapped. Not temper. Care.

He sat like that a long time, doing for himself what he had done for other men for twenty years: who knew, what it cost, what came next. The sum came out the same every time.

He put on the reading glasses with the taped arm and picked up the licence. Dale Garrity, forty-three, a beard, a donor heart. The name meant nothing; he had paid not to be told it. The heavy one, then. The licence lay a quarter-inch to the left of true. His own measure, given back. Even.

Then the page, folded in quarters. Lesson Two. The Architect. He read it once, to the foot. *Mistook clarity for cruelty.* Fair mark. He did not turn it over.

The page went back along her creases into his inside pocket, against the phone. He knew what the licence was, and sleeved it with the halves. Then he laughed, his head going back, too loud for the room. He did not know what at. It stopped the way it had started, without asking him.

He took the sleeve through to the front room and put it back in its place on the wall, among the D's, spine out like the rest. Twenty to one, a rye from the tray. It did not count; a drink after midnight belonged to the day before. He put on side one of the Black Saint and sat in the chair by the piano with the glass on his knee, not touching the piano. He knew every bar of it. He listened as if he did not, for anything that was not where he had left it, and found nothing. The tone arm lifted and went home, and he did not get up to turn it over.

He slept on top of the covers in his clothes, the jacket buttoned over the page, his head where the halves had been. He left the lamp on.

It was still on at six, yellow and foolish against the daylight. The page was where he had put it, warm from him. He changed his shirt and not his suit. In the kitchen the print was where Vale had squared it, and he made coffee beside it and drank it standing, and did not move it.

In the War Room he heard himself accuse the Akers boy, in front of everyone, of something the boy had not done, and he was home before eleven.

At eleven-forty he poured a rye at the counter beside the print. First drink before noon in nine years. He noted the time the way he would note another man's. It tasted thinner in daylight. He took out the page and opened it on the counter and read it again, to the foot, more slowly, and when he put the glass down it was on the page. He left it there. When he lifted it, it had left a ring.

The house was quiet the way it was only quiet on a weekday, and in the quiet he did the other sum, the one he had left on the pillow, and it came out whole. She had been in this house with a blade, and had stood in the dark at the place where his head went, and what she had cut was a record. She could have killed him in his sleep, and she had chosen to warn him instead. She wanted him to understand first, and he found, to his surprise, that he wanted to.
