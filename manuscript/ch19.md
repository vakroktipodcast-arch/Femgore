# Chapter 19

It was Sunday, the twenty-fifth of October, early in the afternoon, and I was standing at the kitchen counter in a jumper and socks. The heating had been off since the small hours of the fourteenth and I had left it off, and the flat had settled at the temperature of a room nobody is expected back to. My phone lay face-up on the counter beside a mug of tea I had made at noon. The tea had gone tepid and then cold, and grown a skin of tannin that wrinkled when I breathed on it, a fine film the colour of weak liver, like the fibrin on a wound that has been left open to the air.

The syllabus had cost me everything outside it, and I could itemise the bill. I had, at least, kept the receipts.

Item. Hannah had called three times that week, on Monday, Wednesday and Friday, and left no message.

Item. Yesterday was June's sixth birthday, at Hannah's house. I did not go. I sent no word.

Item. On Saturday evening the phone had lit with a photograph and no caption. The table after the opening. Paper torn and heaped at one end, and June in the foreground holding a child's microscope in both hands, the way you hold something that might go off, with a little round mirror under the stage catching the light. On top of the heap of paper lay the gift tag, in Hannah's handwriting: *from Iris*. My sister had bought my present for her daughter, and signed my name to it in her own hand, and sent me the photograph so that I would know what I had given.

I spread two fingers on the glass, the way I open up a wound photograph to look at the margins. Hannah has wrapped presents the same way since she was nine, as if the paper might try to escape. Every seam had been taped twice over, and where June had torn through, the clear tape had held on and taken strips of paper with it, so that the heap was ridged with little glossy flanges standing up off it and catching the flash. I have occluded an airway with less tape than that, and mine held.

Item. Mara had stopped texting. The last one had come at twenty to seven on the evening of the fourteenth, about a coffee she had saved me and drunk for me, with a kiss after it, and I hadn't answered, and none had come since. At work that week she said "morning" to me each day, and meant morning. She took her coffee to her own desk. On Thursday we scrubbed at the sinks a basin apart, and she did not hum, and then she stripped her gloves off inside out, the way I taught her, and dropped them in the bin, and went. Those are the findings.

The tablet was propped against the fruit bowl, which had no fruit in it, and the War Room was on with the sound low. On a Sunday the War Room is a skeleton staff and the urn. Counsel stood at the glass with the city behind him and his phone to his ear, on a call about what he called the Bureau's records request. It was the second such call that week, by my count. At the far end of the table a young woman said "Reyes" to the boy beside her, in the voice people use for a diagnosis in somebody else's family. The request was still stalled. Counsel had written *Hold until after the third* at the top of the email that quoted her letter, and the third was nine days off. I have signed enough certificates to know that stalls end. That is the whole of my job, more or less: writing down how something that had been holding stopped.

The page with my name on it was face-down on the writing desk, where I had left it eleven days before. I had not turned it over since. I took my keys from the counter; the brass key lives on the ring with my car key. I unlocked the drawer, and it came out on its runners with its long wooden sigh. The third page was on top, still unpinned. I didn't take it out. I slid the fourth in under it, face-down, without turning it over. On the fourteenth I had refused to put it in with the syllabus, because it is not part of the syllabus. Now it was in with the syllabus. I locked the drawer, and put the keys in my pocket.

The work was the only thing I still wanted. I wanted it the way you want to finish a post-mortem you have stopped learning anything from: because the body is already open, and you have come this far, and the report is the only part of it you haven't written.

On the board by the toaster lay half a slice of toast from noon. The butter had set opaque on it in the cold, a waxy skin the colour of tallow, and the crumb under it had gone hard. I eat standing up when I'm planning. I had been standing up all week. This is not the same as eating. I picked the half up, and looked at it, and put it down.

At four o'clock the phone rang on the counter and said HANNAH.

I registered the hour before I read the name. Sunday at four is when Diane Pell called her mother, and on the thirteenth of September she hadn't.

On the first ring I pressed the scar through my jumper with the fingertips of my left hand. Then I put both hands flat on the counter on either side of the phone, and left them there. The laminate was cold. I could feel the heat going out of me through the heels of my hands, steadily, the way it goes out of a body into a steel table. Second ring. Third. Fourth. Then the small click of the call being taken somewhere else, by something that would listen to her when I wouldn't, and the screen said she had called, and went dark.

I knew what came next. A voicemail of any length is somebody talking into a quiet room. I stood in mine and watched the dark glass for a minute and a quarter, which is a long time to watch glass.

It lit. *Voicemail. Hannah. 1:14.* A name and a duration and no more, because I turned off transcription years ago. I don't like a machine reading my messages before I do.

I picked the phone up. My thumb went to the play arrow and stopped above it, a few millimetres off the glass, and stayed there. I hadn't decided anything. The thumb had.

I found that I couldn't listen to voicemails any more. There were two others, in the archive folder, thirty-four seconds and forty-seven, and I had played them both, and I knew to the second what each had cost. This one had gone into the same column before I could stop it. I put the phone back on the counter, face-up, with the message on it.

It is still there. I have never played it.

* * *

The laptop fan was the loudest thing in the flat.

That evening I stood at the writing desk with the clean laptop open on it and the desk lamp on, and every other light off. I plan standing. Sound first, for once, because there was so little else to go on: the fan's thin, continuous note, the radiator under the window not ticking, and the trackpad, which clicked under my finger like a tongue against the teeth.

The papers had come off the card from the night before: the advance team's run-of-show for Monday the second of November, a security annex behind it, and the floor plans behind that. I read the run-of-show first, top to bottom, the way I read a referral, without skipping to the interesting part, because in a referral the interesting part is wherever the person writing it thought it wasn't. The Calloway. The grand ballroom. Doors at six. The Senator's remarks at nine. At about ten to nine, the principal to the green room, a converted meeting suite off the ballroom corridor.

Then the annex, which was the fortress described from the inside by the people who had built it. A Secret Service perimeter around the building. Magnetometers at the ballroom doors. Hotel staff vetted, listed and badged. A press pool escorted from the riser to a holding room and back, like a patient between departments, and with about as much say. Layer under layer, each one there to stop the next one being reached: skin, then fascia, then muscle, then the peritoneum shining under all of it, and inside, the thing it was all for. Whoever designed it had either studied the peritoneum or been very frightened of something.

At the foot of the annex was its distribution list. Names, titles, a line each, and among them: *SA E. Reyes, FBI (campaign protection liaison).* I read it twice, as I had read her name in counsel's covering email, in my car, on a Thursday at ten past one. She would be in the building. There was still no page for Reyes. Now there wasn't going to be one.

I mapped it for the third time. The first map I drew two years ago, in the abstract, before there was a venue: a man in a room somewhere, with a door. The second was in the week after Brandt's suite, from the floor plans the hotel publishes for people planning weddings. This was the third, with the real run-of-show and the plans from the card, in pencil, on squared paper set square to the edge of the desk. My desk red stayed capped in the pen tray. I worked from the outside in, perimeter, magnetometer, badge, escort, and I tried every approach I had against each layer in turn, and every one of them ended at a man with an earpiece. I used the plans for the layers and for no more than that. Each layer held. I graded the security sound, and the grade gave me about as much pleasure as signing for a parcel.

By ten the side of my right hand was silver with graphite from the wrist to the little finger, where it had dragged across the paper. The only warmth in the room came up out of the laptop into the heels of my hands, and I kept them there.

I checked myself the way I check everything. Two fingers of my right hand on my left wrist, out of habit, and the radial pulse under them was fifty-eight, resting. My mouth was dry, the tongue tacky against the palate. The last time I had planned a body my mouth had watered for hours. I waited for the metal, the coin held too long at the back of the tongue, which had come every time the old injury surfaced. It didn't come.

For the first time in two years of planning, I could find nothing. Not dread. Not hunger. Paperwork.

There were two rallies. I had gone through every recording of him from the autumn that anybody had left uncut, station by station, looking for one angle, a door seen from the outside, and I found it twice. The first was Thursday the eighth of October, at a convention centre: the pool camera's raw feed, which a regional news site had posted whole, three hours of it, with the camera parked on a corridor while the crew waited, so that it caught the detail closing a door and taking up its position outside. The second was Thursday the twenty-second, at a university field house: a local station's raw livestream, which had started early and left its camera on a holding-area door for anyone with the patience to watch a door. I have never been to one of his rallies. I have only ever watched them on a screen. I had the patience.

I scrubbed both, frame by frame. The door shut. A hand flat on it from outside, then an agent's back to it. Then the player's counter ran, and its numbers were the only thing on the screen that moved. On the livestream, behind the door and the wall and the agent, I could hear the field house filling, a sound like surf with a PA over it, and over that the station's own microphone picking up somebody's cough. Sixty seconds, give or take a frame. The door opened and he came out already smiling. Then the other rally, and the same minute, as near as a frame lets you say. He had never gone over.

When the second door opened I touched the scar through my jumper.

Detail out, door shut, sixty seconds. Brandt's gift, confirmed.

I thought it in a whole sentence, with a subject and a verb and an object, the way he would have said it.

I went to the bathroom and pulled the cord, and the light over the mirror came on hard and white. I hadn't looked into that mirror since the morning of the fourteenth, when I washed my face with my eyes on the plughole. I looked now. I said the sentence aloud to the woman in it, to find out whether it sounded like a joke.

"He told me the truth while I was inside him."

It didn't.

* * *

I stayed where I was. The tube over the basin is a strip of flat white light like the one over intake at work, and it does to everyone what intake does: it lays them out, takes the colour out of the lips, and finds every shadow under the eyes and makes a note of it.

Smell first. The bathroom smelled of unscented soap and, under the soap, faintly, of chlorhexidine and cold iron, the smell of work, which lives in my skin and which no shower has ever removed. Then of something else, close, and new. I cupped my hand over my mouth and nose and breathed out into it and breathed in, and there it was. Pear drops. Nail varnish. The sweetish solvent note of acetone, coming off my own breath. I know it from a hundred decedents, the diabetics and the starved, rising off them when the bag is unzipped. Ketosis. My body had run out of what I was giving it and had started on its own stores. In my professional experience, that is how you know a body has started on the furniture.

I looked for a long time. The woman from August was not there: Hannah's sister, who ate two sandwiches in a park and wanted the third, and Mara's mentor, whom Mara watched the way people watch a chef through the pass. I am not being dramatic. I am describing a change of tenant. The person in the mirror was a replacement.

I took off my jumper, and then the rest, and put them on the lid of the laundry basket. The tiles were cold under my bare feet, and the cold came up through the soles into the bones of the ankle. Gooseflesh rose along my upper arms as the arrector pili took hold of each fine hair and stood it up, a reflex older than clothes, doing its best, in its small furred way, to keep me warm with a coat I haven't had for a long time.

I drew breath to dictate, in the voice I use for the dictaphone, and the sentence was there already, the one I have said over nearly every body I have ever examined: *the body is that of a well-nourished adult female*. I got as far as the *well*, in my head, and had to amend the report.

"The body is that of an adequately nourished adult female, consistent with the stated age." I said the whole sentence, and it held.

The clavicles stood proud, two struts with a shadow under each, and the suprasternal notch between them had deepened into a pit you could have pooled a teaspoon of water in. The anterior superior iliac spines showed at the front of the pelvis, a knuckle of bone either side, where for years there had been a soft margin. On Thursday the twenty-second I had put a new hole in my belt with the point of a scalpel blade. I'd like to say it was the only tool to hand. It was the only tool I trusted. About four kilos since the start of October, I put it, by the belt and the clavicles. I have not stood on a scale. I didn't need to. The skin over my knuckles was dry and cracked from the nitrile, fine fissures across each joint like the glaze on an old plate.

The scar is on the left flank, below the tip of the twelfth rib. It was a little over three weeks old: pink, still raised, a line of three point two centimetres with seven pairs of small marks, one of each pair either side of it, where the nylon had gone in and come out, not yet silvered. With the fat gone from around it, it sat closer to the rib than it used to. I put my fingertips on it. It was the only part of me that hadn't changed its opinion of me.

Periorbital darkening, the skin under each eye gone to slate. I pulled down my left lower lid with a fingertip. The conjunctiva should be the pink of the inside of a cheek; mine was nearer bone than rose. Haemoglobin down, or on its way: the flank's three hundred millilitres, and not much put back since. I put out my tongue, and it had a white coat on it, thicker towards the back. I have never written *very tired* on anybody's form. It isn't a finding, it's an opinion. I wrote it on mine.

Then I held both hands out flat in front of me, palms down, fingers spread, the way I would ask a patient to if I had ever had one who could. No tremor. Not in the fingertips, not at the wrist. They were as steady as they have been since my second year of residency.

I looked at her the way somebody else would have to. Not a pathologist. A witness, being asked by a woman with a notebook, because paper is what Reyes has. Height: somebody's guess. Build: slight, and slighter than last month. The jaw. The widow's peak and the parting, which a stranger had once been chosen for. The hands. I read it back to myself, and it would have fitted on half a page, with room for the signature.

The replacement was competent, very tired, and had lost weight. I had graded my lies, my plans, my exits and most of the people I had ever stood in a room with. I had written her an Evaluation once, on a page now face-down in a drawer. I had never given her a mark. Competent. It is the mark you give a resident who has done everything right and ought to be sent home.

Then I checked the replacement for flaws the way I would check a body on the table: the scalp, the face, the neck, the chest, the abdomen, the flanks, the limbs, the backs of the hands and the soles of the feet, region by region, in order, omitting nothing but the back, which nobody can examine for herself. I found them. A split at the left angle of the mouth, which is what a body does when it has been going short for a while. A bruise on the right shin that I could not account for. I didn't look away.

Dressed again, in the kitchen, I watched the phone on the counter light once, Hannah's voicemail still waiting under her name, and I turned it face-down. Then I opened the fridge and stood in its light, with the chicken on the bottom shelf under its plate and the cold coming out over my bare feet, and closed it.
