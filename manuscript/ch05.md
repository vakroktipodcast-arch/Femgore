# Chapter 5

I had been standing at the gap in the door since ten past five, and by eight the station had got into everything I had brought with me. Wet concrete first, the mineral cold of it, a smell like the underside of a paving slab. Iron gone to rust along the pipework, the smell of a cast-iron pan nobody has bothered to oil. From the grating over the dry wet-well came the old sump breath of oil and water that had stood a long time and then left. And through the gap, a hand's width of it, the reservoir: weed, wet stone, the green, faintly metallic breath that open water gives off at first light before anything has warmed it. I stood in all of it and breathed it, and I waited for her.

The dark had stopped being dark hours before. Twenty minutes is what the rods need: the rhodopsin rebuilds, the cones stand down, and a black room comes back to you in no colour at all, grainy at first and then particular, like a print coming up in a developing tray. I knew where everything was. The sheeting, taped at its edges. The pump housing on its concrete plinth, and at its base the iron bracket I had hung my weight from on Saturday, with two zip-ties already threaded through it and laid out long across the floor, cut to a length I had measured on the bench at home. The kit in its row along the far wall, squared to the brick. At my feet the bag with the rest: two more ties for her ankles; the head-torch with its filter, switched off; the page in its clear sleeve; the zip bag I am not going to describe yet. I had liked the dark on Saturday. I liked it better with something to wait for in it.

I had suited up by feel at a quarter past five: white coverall, hair cover, overshoes, two pairs of nitrile, the hood down on my shoulders for now. Under the hair cover an earpiece ran on its wire to the phone in my hand, one of a pair of prepaids. The other I had put out at ten to five, face-down in the grass beside the door at the top of the sumac spur, forty metres up from the trail marker post. The post was bare. I had taken the recorder off it on Saturday, after the third clap. The door opened inward on hinges I had greased the same afternoon; my padlock hung open on the hasp outside, where anyone could have seen it and nobody would look; a rubber wedge held the door off the frame; the moving blanket was tied back to the hinge side, clear of the gap; and a loop of cord on the inside was how I would shut it.

The station holds at about eleven degrees whatever the morning is doing, and it was in me. It came up through the soles of my overshoes from the concrete, and it had settled into the backs of my hands inside both layers of glove, and into the hinge of my jaw, which I had to remind to unclench. It was not a cold I had chosen. I stood in it anyway. I had moved perhaps a handful of times in three hours, each one a weight shift from heel to heel, and I recorded the stillness the way I would record anyone's: female, upright, hands at rest, breathing slow, mouth dry.

The mouth was dry because I had drunk nothing since midnight. I have drained the bladders of enough people who died of cold to know what three hours on cold concrete does to one, the blood pulled in off the skin and the kidneys reading the extra volume as a surplus and getting rid of it, and I had no intention of leaving my DNA in a pump station for the sake of comfort. My tongue sat against my palate like a stamp that wouldn't take. I had eaten nothing since the oxtail in the car. I said nothing to myself about that.

I checked my hands. Since Desmond's plaster, I check. They were steady. I checked them again, which is not the same thing as doubting them. From eight o'clock I kept them working, fist and spread, twenty at a time, the way you tell a patient with a new cannula, and between sets I tucked them under my arms, gloves and all, where the blood still was. Cold fingers are slow fingers, and nothing that was going to happen at half past eight could be done slowly.

At about ten to six the gap had gone from black to slate. Then a thin bone colour came up off the water, and a mist lay on the reservoir the way fat lies on the top of cold stock, and a heron was standing in the shallows on one leg as if it had been put there to scale the view. I would enter it in the logbook later. I keep the logbook honestly. By seven the sky had gone over to ash and stayed there. Nobody was on the trail. Three years of her Sundays sat in the app, public by default, and nobody at the campaign had ever thought to change the setting.

I had her numbers. The app had given me a hundred and forty-one Sundays and I had given it two of my own. Out of the south car park between 7:31 and 7:48. Clockwise. The bench at 3.1 kilometres by five or ten past eight; eleven minutes with the paperback; up at twenty past; on round the second half under the dam to the post at the foot of my spur at 8:33, give or take nine. I walked the loop in my head, at her pace, and graded it. A sensible route. Flat, open, well used, nothing on it that could reach her. In a hundred and forty-one Sundays she had never once left the loop, and that was the only consistency I needed her to have. Distinction.

The last time I had stood on this morning she had come out of the trees with her daughter, and I had capped the lens and gone home. I had checked since. Monday night, the train: I'd heard her say it to him myself, through a bezel, eating a peach. Olivia was back at college. I listened for two sets of footsteps anyway.

There was one. Gravel, coming on from the bench side, brisk, short in the stride. Through the gap I watched her come along the trail towards the post below me: the olive waxed-cotton jacket, the slate collar, hands in her pockets. She walked like a woman who had been told walking was good for her by someone she paid, and believed it because she'd paid. Six minutes early, inside the window. I had built the window. I liked her. I had her liver at fifteen hundred grams. I could not have told you, watching her come on along the gravel, which of those two facts was the warmer. It was the last time I saw her from the outside.

I put my hood up. At 8:27 I rang the phone in the grass.

It went off up the spur beside my feet, loud and cheap and insistent, and down at the post Diane stopped. *She answers. She always answers.* I had written it in her margin. I watched it be true. She looked up the spur, which she had never once looked up, and then she left the loop. The whole of her followed the one sound, the way a body on the table follows when you lift a single limb: the shoulder comes, then the hip, then everything. She climbed the forty metres with her short stride gone long. On the fourth ring she was beside the door. On the fifth she bent, and picked it up, and straightened with her back to me, an arm's length from the gap, looking down the spur the way she had come.

"Olivia?"

I heard it twice. Once through the gap, in the open air, small and level. Then, a fraction of a second behind it, in my own ear, through the earpiece, closer than anyone had spoken to me in years. It is the name a mother says first to a ringing phone, before anyone has spoken. It was the only name she had.

In the dark, I almost stopped. I didn't. I noted that I almost had, the way you note a reading off a scale.

* * *

I ended the call and had the door open before the tone died. "Hello?" she said to the dead line, and looked down the spur for whoever had left a phone in the grass, and did not turn round. I was already behind her. A strip of pewter industrial tape, torn in advance with one end folded under, hung across the back of my left glove, exactly as it had on the kitchen floor. My right hand went over her mouth from behind, my left forearm went across her collarbones, and my weight went down through my knee into the back of her right knee, into the popliteal fossa, where the leg has no opinion, and the leg folded. Coffee came through the glove against my palm, her breakfast on a hot breath, and I took it as the first sample.

Backwards over the threshold, into the dark. There is no dignified way to do it and I did not look for one. Her left hand came up and found my right wrist and dragged at the glove, and the glove held; her right hand still had the phone in it and knocked once against the frame going past, and kept it. A body holds on to whatever it was holding. Two steps inside I turned her and put her down, and she went down face-first, and her left cheekbone met the concrete, grit and all, which is a courtesy to the forensic examiner and none at all to the zygoma. I heard it. I logged it the way I would at the table: abrasion over the left zygoma, about two centimetres. Rushed. The tape went across her mouth from the back of my glove in under four seconds, as rehearsed, and I left the nose clear. I needed her breathing.

A knee between the scapulae. I took the prepaid out of her right fist one finger at a time and dropped it in the bag. Her wrists behind her, one into each of the loops waiting at the bracket, and the ratchets went with that small rapid sound, twice. Her ankles, the two ties from the bag and then a band of industrial tape round both. Then up, the wedge kicked clear, the blanket let down, the cord, and the door drawn shut, my heel against the weatherstrip and my shoulder against the blanket to seat it, and I heard the room go deaf. I had tested that with my hands on Saturday. It was better with her in it. Then back down, my knee between her shoulders again. She breathed through her nose, fast and wet. My own breath was the louder of the two.

It felt good. It felt good the way the clean stroke through the brain feels good, the door that closes without being slammed, except that it was not in my hand this time, it was everywhere: in the big muscles of my thighs and my back where her weight had been, in my lungs, in the heat that had come into my face, warm, actually warm, and the cold gone out of my forearms for the first time since ten past five. I was breathing hard and happy, as I had been with the rug. The rug had been more cooperative. It had also been less motivated, which in fairness to the rug was the point of the rug. My pulse was up and I let it be up. I filed it where it belonged. Rehearsal, execution, under four seconds. Pass.

Her phone buzzed. I felt it through her, in my knee: one buzz from the left side of her chest, where the jacket's inside pocket lay between her and the floor, and then nothing.

I rolled her left shoulder up on my knee and took it out. The screen lit the room, the blue of a phone at the wrong hour, and showed me a stack of notifications. The staff channel. A polling alert. The comms director, twice. Twenty-three of them. The campaign had sent her twenty-three things that morning, every one of them sure she would answer, and her daughter had sent one, and her daughter's was on top.

*are you on your walk? call me after, it's the roommate thing again.*

I didn't know about the roommate. A nineteen-year-old awake before nine on a Sunday is in trouble, or in love, or in a row, and it is usually only one of them. I didn't grade the daughter.

I held the buttons down on the side with the message open on the glass, and the slider came up over it and dimmed it, and I drew it across with a gloved knuckle. It asked me for nothing. The text was still on the screen, dimmed and legible, when it went dark. It did not go first and leave the light behind it; the words and the light went out together. Then the phone went into the foil sleeve, and I folded the sleeve shut.

* * *

A minute later I had her up. I took her under the arms and sat her with her back against the pump housing, and the wrist ties rode the bracket as I had measured them to, with enough slack for her to sit and none for anything else. I did it without a word. While I was that close I peeled the tape from her mouth. It came away with the fine vellus hair of her upper lip in it and left a dull line of adhesive behind, and I folded the strip sticky side to sticky side and put it in the bag. Nothing of mine was staying in that room unless I had decided it would.

Then I sat on the plinth of the old pump, a metre and a half from her, out of reach of a kick, and switched on the head-torch and angled it down at the page, and the station came back in oxblood: her, the housing, the sheeting, the slick of the pipework. With the hood up and the beam above and ahead of my face, and her eyes still full of the morning she had been taken out of, my face stayed black. What she had of me was a light and two gloved hands. The foil was on the concrete between us, where I had put it down.

She did not scream. She had already read the room: the shut door, the way her own breathing came straight back at her off the brick. She had understood what it had been built for. That is an administrator's skill, knowing which rooms are worth raising your voice in, and I gave her credit for it. She swallowed. For the moment she asked for nothing.

I took the page out of its sleeve and read it to her. All of it, from the subject line down, in the order I had typed it.

I had read it aloud once before, to a flat with nobody in it, and heard what it sounded like; what I had not known, because a flat cannot tell you, was what it would do when it reached the person it had been written for, and it reached her: it went out across a metre and a half of cold air in a room I had made deaf, and nothing in that room took any of it away from her, not the brick and not the blanket on the door and not the iron, so that every word arrived at the weight I had typed it, and I watched them arrive in the spill of the torch, in her face and in the set of her shoulders and in the small adjustments of a woman sitting very straight on a floor, the way you watch a weight settle on a scale until the needle stops, and the reading is the reading, and with each word that landed the warmth came back up the insides of my forearms, as it had at the door, and stayed there while I read. It was not the voice I use for the dictaphone. It was not the one I use for Hannah. It was the voice the page was written in. It was mine, and I had never heard it arrive.

*You will be evaluated accordingly.*

I lowered the page.

She was fluent. I want that on the record, because I had the dossier and I knew what she had beaten, and I was listening for it. Not one of the pauses her staff took for thought. On a floor, with her hands tied to iron, that is its own kind of discipline.

"Whatever this is," she said, "there is a process. I can walk you through it."

She led with process.

"I didn't run the press," she said. "Brandt did. I can give you Brandt."

She said it easily, like a woman who had opened with Brandt before, in other rooms, and found that it usually bought her the rest of the meeting. I already had Brandt. He was in the drawer at home, under her.

"There's a list. I can tell you who else signed."

She offered me a list the way the family offers the pathologist a history: helpfully, and some time after it could have made a difference. I heard what I expected to hear. Counsel. The witness line. Whoever had countersigned me into the cabinet and initialled the drawer shut. I didn't ask which list.

There was one name she had said that morning, once, to a ringing phone. She did not say it again. I noticed that as well.

I graded the negotiation in my head while she waited for me to choose from it. A strong opening. Poorly sourced. Wrong currency. Everything she had laid out on the concrete between us was information, because information was what a gatekeeper had; she had spent her life deciding who could have which piece of it and when, and it had never once failed to buy her something. She was sure there was a price. She was sitting in a pump station with her wrists on a bracket and a graze on her face, and she was still sure, and the certainty sat on her as neatly as her collar. I could have pinned it to the whole campaign.

At four o'clock her mother's Sunday call was not going to come. I mention it as a fact about the schedule.

The cold had begun to come back into my hands. Peripheral vasoconstriction: the body withdrawing its funding from the extremities to protect the core, which is the oldest policy there is, and the one the campaign ran on. I could hear her waiting.

"I don't want information," I said. "I want the grade."

She went quiet. I watched her understand it in the oxblood: not the words, which were simple, but the shape of the room around them. There was nothing she had that I would take. A woman who had spent her life inside negotiations was sitting on a concrete floor finding out what it is like to be outside one.

And I had been waiting two years to say that sentence out loud. Since the night the page went into the drawer. I had said it to nobody, and I had said it now, to her, and it had come out at exactly the weight I had always meant it to have. I report that the way I reported the almost, at the phone. My mouth was no longer dry.

The phone lay on the concrete between us in its foil, a small silver brick with her daughter's last words sealed inside it, and my pulse was in my teeth.
