# Chapter 12

It was a little after ten, the side door to the loading bay was twelve metres ahead of me, and in the inside pocket of my jacket was a sliver of plastic smaller than a fingernail with a day of Brandt's traffic on it. I had chosen a Friday because he was at a donor dinner, being photographed with a fork in his hand by a room full of people who would swear to it. I was pleased with that. It is the kind of thing I am pleased with.

The hall roared. It is a room built to keep something cold that would otherwise spoil, and like every room of that kind it never stops talking about it. It was louder than the morgue, a wall of moving air you could have leaned on, and under the roar the cold aisle breathed up through the grilles set in the painted concrete and into my trouser legs, a long way from the soft early-October night I had walked in through. Ozone. Warm dust from the hot aisles, that smell of the inside of a hairdryer. The racks stood in their rows with their status lights going jade and amber, and the ceiling stayed dark, because I had kept to the route I had mapped the night before, where the motion sensors don't look. I had keyed the midline into the panel on the way in, as before.

I graded the exit while I walked it, because I cannot walk anywhere without marking it. Card out, new card in, tap left seated on the uplink, no light disturbed. Route clean. Time well under budget. I gave myself the mark I give a resident who closes without being reminded where the skin goes.

The bay smelled wrong before it looked wrong. Cigarette smoke, recent, stubbed out half-smoked by someone who had been told to stop. A menthol lozenge over it, sucked to hide the cigarette. And over both, cheap cologne gone flat on warm skin, the smell of a man who had put it on hours ago for a short job and had been waiting ever since. Then the van: white, panelled, backed up to the roller door with its side door open and the yard's sodium lamp coming through the gap, the colour of weak tea on the concrete. Nobody in the cab. It had not been there on the way in.

They came out of the dark at either side of the door. Not police. Police announce themselves; it is the one courtesy the job requires, and these two were working hard at not making a sound, which in that room was a waste of effort. The older one was heavy and bearded and stood with his weight forward and his knees soft, as if he had done this before and been paid properly for it. The younger one was quick, light on the balls of his feet, with a lock-knife clipped to his belt and his hand a careful distance from it.

I read them the way I read a tray laid out before a procedure. Zip-ties, a loose bundle looped at each man's belt. A black cloth hood in the young one's left hand, hanging like a dead bird. No firearm drawn, none printing through either jacket. The van open behind them. Nobody brings a hood to a killing; the dead are never in any danger of seeing where they're going. It was all on the tray. Take her. Don't damage her. Find out what she has and whom she's told. It was the third of his walls, the one about no clean kills, and he had put it on two strangers like a pair of coveralls and sent it to stand in his own loading bay.

I had thought he would come himself. I had got as far as wondering what he would wear. He had sent hands instead and stayed at his dinner, and I felt it the way you feel being stood up, briefly and low in the stomach, and then as information. I had not risen to Tessa Quinn, so he had stopped fishing and sent men in to wade. And the rectangle of black tape I had left squared on the operator's desk had told him I would come back, when I had meant it to tell him only that I had been. So he had kept two men parked nearby since morning, and when my cloned card turned his reader green, it had rung their phone. The syllabus had taught; the student had learned. I gave him the mark for that, too.

Two of them, one of me. They could not afford to hurt me badly, because a woman who cannot talk is no use to a man who needs to know what she knows. I could afford anything at all. I liked that asymmetry very much. I could have called it an advantage. I didn't bother. I felt my pulse come up and my face go warm, and I wrote those down as findings. My hands were in nitrile, and steady. The scalpel was sheathed in the inner sleeve pocket of my jacket, against my left forearm. I did not touch it. I noted that I was not touching it.

The bearded one put his hands up, palms out, the gesture men use on horses and on women in car parks. He took one step towards me.

"Easy," he said.

And then he said my name. Not Dr. Vale, which is how the wire stories say it, and the paperwork, and every stranger who has ever been handed a file with me in it. He said, "Iris." It is the name a man uses when someone has given it to him on purpose. Brandt had chosen it, and put it in a stranger's mouth, in the dark, across the city from his dinner.

* * *

My own sweat, coming sharp out of my collar under the cold air. Then nothing for a while that I had time to smell.

The young one came in from my left with the hood held open in both hands. The bearded one was behind me before I had finished turning, quick for his weight, and took me in a bear hug that pinned my upper arms to my sides and lifted me off my heels. His breath was menthol. His forearms were a bar across my chest. My feet were off the concrete and somebody had told him I would scream.

I didn't. I let my whole weight go dead, which is a thing I know a great deal about, and when my heels came back to the floor I drove the right one down onto the top of his foot, onto the first metatarsal, where the bone runs long and thin and close under the skin, and his weight came off that foot. Then I threw the back of my skull into his face and felt the nasal bones go, a crunch like stepping on a snail in the dark, and his arms opened.

I am not a fighter. I have never been in a fight. I am someone who knows where everything is.

The scalpel was in my right hand before I had decided it should be. The young one was inside my reach with the hood still in his left fist, and he had done the thing his brief told him not to do. The lock-knife was open in his right.

He put it into my left flank as I turned, and he pulled the thrust, because somebody had told him not to kill me, and I had known he would. I narrated it as it went in, the way I would write it up afterwards. Skin. Subcutaneous fat, which offers nothing. External oblique, its fibres running down and forward like hands into pockets, cut across their grain. Internal oblique under it, fibres the other way. Then the bite of transversus abdominis, and the blade stopped. I guessed which layer it had stopped in. At home I found I was right. Below the tip of the twelfth rib, forward of the kidney, a layer short of the colon. A knife in the left flank has four things to worry about: the kidney, the colon, the spleen and your opinion of yourself. He got none of them. *I'll live,* I thought, in the dictation voice, and I was delighted.

I have read fourteen hundred wounds after the fact. I had never had one read to me as it happened, by the nerve endings, in the first person.

I cut across the volar surface of his right wrist, one stroke, from just inside the radial artery to just short of flexor carpi ulnaris, through the superficial and deep flexor tendons and the median nerve, and his hand forgot how to hold anything. The knife rang on the concrete like a dropped spoon. I took the tendons and left him both arteries, stopping the blade short of each on purpose. It is the most considerate thing I have ever done for a man who had just stabbed me, and I would like it noted.

We had come off the bay and onto the floor between the first racks, and the sensors found us. The lights came on overhead with a clack, one row and then the next and the next, cold white LED light on white-painted concrete and steel and the three of us, and a man on the floor holding his wrist. I knew that kind of room. I have spent fifteen years in rooms like it. It felt, the whole time, like the morgue with the lights on. It felt like work, and I have always loved my work, and it was the most fun I had had in three years. I am telling you that the way I would tell you the time.

The bearded one came back with his face streaming and the brief gone out of him. He was angry, and anger is heavy and slow and announces itself. Blood from his nose had soaked his beard into a single oxblood tuft. He came in low, the sweat of the effort standing on his forehead, and got his arms round my waist, and we went down together onto the painted concrete, and my flank tore as I landed, a hot bright rip that I filed under *later*. He was heavier. I was better informed. I let his weight carry us over, because that was where it wanted to go, and came up on top of him with my knee in the soft angle under his ribs and my left hand flat on his chest through his shirt and the scalpel in my right.

The young one was against a rack upright with his good hand clamped round his wrist and his eyes on the lights. The bearded one was pinned and breathing wet through the wreck of his nose, his heart going under my left palm through the shirt like someone knocking to be let out. The van had brought two men, and both of them were here. My knee told me he could not get up. My palm told me he was not going to try.

I held it for one breath.

Then I opened the side of his neck. Anterior triangle, at the front edge of sternocleidomastoid, one deep stroke through the common carotid and the jugular beside it, the arterial wall resisting the blade for an instant like a wet tendon and then not. Under the fans, close enough to hear it, his breath changed: a click, a gargle. The spray came up warm across my face and down my front before I had my hand back, in jets, one to each beat of his heart, and under the jets the jugular's liver-coloured welling. It got into my mouth: salt, and warm, and the taste of the inside of my own cheek when I bite it. Iron, all at once, over the iron from his nose. My own pulse was going faster than his jets and my face was hot under his blood, and I liked it. That is the whole finding. I stepped back off him and took both hands away. I counted. Unconscious inside fifteen seconds. Gone in about three minutes. I enjoyed the counting, all of it, and the fans went on as if they had not noticed, which they hadn't.

Only then did I look. He lay on his back under the white light, his beard soaked to the jaw, the blood fanned across the white-painted concrete to the nearest grille and running into it, and I stood over him with my face wet and wanted to keep looking.

There was no page for him. I had not planned him. Nothing to pin, no role to circle in red. He was the first thing I had done that was not on the syllabus, and the first thing I had ever done for no reason except that I could.

* * *

The card was still in my inside pocket. I checked it before I checked myself.

I zip-tied the young one to the rack upright with his own ties, one round his good wrist and one round the post, and he let me. He was alive, bleeding brisk and venous and survivable, and he would be there when Brandt sent someone to collect him. I took the dead man's wallet from his back pocket with two gloved fingers, which was the only time I touched him after, and took his driving licence out of it, and did not read it then. I put it in my pocket with the card. I picked up the hood they had brought for me, folded it into a pad, and pressed it under my jacket against my flank, which was the first good use anyone had made of it all night.

My blood was on his floor, and my DNA is on the office's elimination register, like every pathologist's, so that we can be written out of our own cases. Anyone who ran it would find me. But this was a floor belonging to a man who could not let anything have happened on it, in a building owned three companies deep with Diane Pell as its officer of record. He would clean it better than I could. I gave the job to the Architect, keyed the midline into his panel on my way out, and went through the bay into the sodium and walked the two streets to my car with the hood held to my side like a hot-water bottle. Before I touched the wheel I did my face, neck, hairline and wrists by feel with the isopropyl wipes from the door pocket, and bagged the wipes with my gloves. It was not enough, and I knew it, and I drove home at the limit with my jacket zipped to the chin.

Home at about eleven. I didn't turn on the overhead light. I brought the desk lamp into the bathroom on its long flex and sat on the edge of the bath, where the tile was cool under my thighs, and cut my shirt off with the kitchen scissors. Blood, his over mine. I looked down and twisted my torso into the lamp until the wound came into the light.

Single-edged blade: the posterior end squared, where the spine had sat, the anterior end acute. Three point two centimetres at the skin, longer than the blade was wide, because I had been turning and the skin had been pulling the other way. A track of about four centimetres, running upward and medially. I went through the absences one by one. No guarding. No rebound. No pain at the tip of the shoulder, which is what blood under the diaphragm tells you about. Urine clear, which I checked. Peritoneum intact. Kidney untouched. Blood loss perhaps three hundred millilitres, a generous donation, most of it now in a cloth hood that had been meant for my head.

*The skin tells you about the blade,* Mara said in my head. *The muscle tells you about the hand.* She had said it at the projector last spring, to detective trainees at the police academy, in the lecture on wound geometry we gave together. I had taught it to her. The muscle told me about his hand. It had been holding back.

I made saline in the kitchen: nine grams of salt to a litre of boiled water, left to cool. It is about a third of the strength I brine a chicken in, which tells you what I think of chickens. I irrigated the track until it ran clear into the bath. My residency suture kit lives in the drawer under the sink. Lidocaine lives in a cabinet at work. The dead never ask for it, and it had never occurred to anyone to issue me any for the living.

*Close it along the lines it wants to keep.*

I closed it. Three buried interrupted sutures of the braided absorbable through the external oblique and its fascia, knots turned down out of the way, so that the deep layer would hold when I bent; then seven simple interrupted stitches of the nylon in the skin, the needle going in at right angles and out at right angles, the forceps' teeth biting the skin edge, which is my skin edge and has opinions, the drag of the nylon through my own dermis like a thread through felt, each edge everted a little so it would heal flat, each one an instrument tie, square knot, the tails left long enough to find again on the tenth day, and the pain arriving at every pass exact and bright and local and reported, and beside it in the same breath the deep plain pleasure of doing a thing properly with my own hands, which were steady. They were steady the whole time. I put the needle down. I dressed it, and taped the dressing with the same micropore I use on specimen labels at work.

I was going to be very hungry. I had known since the floor.

At a quarter past twelve I sat at the counter, my left arm held against my side, and made tartare from a fillet tail I had bought for something else. I chopped it by hand, with a single-edged knife; I noticed that. Shallot, very fine. Capers, cornichons, a spoon of Dijon, a few drops of Worcestershire, a yolk broken over the top. I toasted the sourdough and ate, the beef cold and mineral and soft, the yolk coating everything, the capers going off like small sharp lights, the toast cracking under it. Haemorrhage wants iron and protein replaced, and I replaced them. I ate all of it.

I was not tired. Across the city Brandt would be home from his dinner by now, being told about his floor. I dressed carefully over the dressing, dark and high-waisted, and went out at twenty to one to a late bar in a business hotel by the river. There was a man. I didn't ask his name. At his flat, which smelled of someone else's laundry powder, I kept my shirt on and fucked him sitting up, fast, and came hard enough to feel every one of the seven stitches. I checked his hands. The fingertips were soft. Nobody had ever made him practise anything. I noted it, and did not tell myself what I had been checking for. He had a drawer of spare toothbrushes still in their cellophane, which told me more about him than anything he said, and he said a great deal. I used one and took it home in a tissue, because it had me on it.

Home at four. Asleep by ten past, and nothing, no dream, until twenty to seven: two and a half hours, gone under the way a patient goes under, counting backwards from ten and never reaching seven. I dressed for the Saturday rota, badge, keys, the wound under my shirt, and went to the sink to brush my teeth.

I had a mouthful of foam when I reached for his grade. I have graded everyone I have met since August, including a donor's dog. I reached, and there was nothing there. He had no page, no subject and no function, and I stood at the sink and could not think what he had been examined on. My fingertips had gone to the dressing. I hadn't sent them. And I had woken rested. I had slept better than I had in years.

On the sink edge, parallel to the tap, the needle. In the cup beside mine, a second toothbrush, still damp. I threw it away and went to work.
