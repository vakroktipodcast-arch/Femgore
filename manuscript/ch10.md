# Chapter 10

The locker room at twenty to seven smelled of floor disinfectant, pine and ammonia, and of the clean-laundry steam that comes off fresh scrubs when you shake them out of the fold, and of my coffee in its paper cup on the bench, going bitter at its own pace. I was half into the scrubs, trousers on, the top still folded on my knee, when I opened *The Cloakroom* on my phone.

I have read it every morning for three years. It is the anonymous political gossip site where the blind items about me ran, and I read it the way you press on an old fracture when the weather turns. Not because it helps. Because you want to know it's still there.

That Monday there was a new item, posted at six, and by its second sentence I knew whose hand it was.

A former campaign volunteer, it said, let go by Diane Pell last spring for "erratic conduct", had been posting about Pell for weeks under an anonymous account. Then "a source close to the investigation" said that whoever had killed her "knew anatomy". I know his sentences. The ones he wrote about me are clipped at the back of his file in my drawer: the passive verb that lets someone be let go without anyone doing the letting, the single phrase in quotation marks, put there to be repeated. He never tells you what a thing is. He sets it beside something else and lets you do the arithmetic, and the answer is his.

*Knew anatomy* was Mara's word. It was *practised*, carried out of the building in somebody's pocket and come out shorter and worse. Somebody had the file, and somebody had handed it to him. It was the first time anything in that file had been used against anybody, and it had not been used against me.

Under the item, a small photograph of a woman's face, and a link to the account, run through a shortener. A shortened link is a door with somebody standing behind it, counting. The item had two jobs. It gave the investigation and the internet a person to run after. And it was bait. Anybody who knew the volunteer was false, and had a private reason to look closely, would look closely, and the looking was what he was counting.

My thumb went to the link and stopped a few millimetres above the glass, which was warm from my palm. I watched it the way I'd watch a thumb on a table, flexor pollicis longus holding the distal phalanx bent over nothing, and noted it, and took it away. I put the phone in my locker with the link unopened and carried it through the day like a sweet in a pocket. I knew what the deferring was. I am a woman who eats the crust first.

The air conditioning had been turned down for autumn, and autumn hadn't come, and the back of my neck was damp before I had my hair pinned. My case was a man in his seventies who had come off a ladder clearing his gutters. A subdural haematoma, a crescent of clot the colour of blackcurrant jam lying between the dura and the brain like a letter slipped under a door. The gutters, according to the report, were clear. He had finished. The tablet was in my bag in the locker, but I don't open the feed inside a county building, and I spent the afternoon wishing I could, which I note.

The flat that evening had every window open, and the night came in smelling of tarmac giving back the day's heat and of somebody's barbecue two streets over, charcoal and fat and something sticky, and I stood tearing bread off a loaf and salting a tomato over the sink and eating it out of my hand like an apple. I eat standing up when I plan.

I scrubbed back to the lunch hour and watched the War Room find the item. They were delighted. A crank volunteer is a gift five weeks out; somebody actually clapped. Comms was drafting *we do not comment on online speculation* aloud, trying the words in different orders, as if one order might say something. Brandt stood at the glass with his back to the city and said nothing. Surprise is a small piece of work on a face: frontalis lifts the brows, the jaw drops a few millimetres. Every face in that room did it once. His didn't.

Then the account, on the clean laptop, through a cached archive copy, never the live page. Its first post was dated Thursday 17 September, the day after he had stood at my desk and read my drawer. It was eleven days old, and he had been growing it the way you grow a sourdough starter: fed at the same hours, kept somewhere warm, and to be thrown out the moment it had done its job. The misspellings were placed like seasoning, sparingly, never in a name. The grievance began small, how Diane Pell had once spoken to her, and grew louder in a curve I have seen in real files, who Diane was really working for, what they were all hiding, until it was the voice of a woman nobody would believe about anything. Nothing in it about paper. Nothing about anyone else. One woman, one grudge, one dead boss. That was the craft, and it was his old craft. It was what he had done to me. He had built a woman whose accusations sounded like symptoms.

I gave it an A. I don't give A's to fiction.

I will tell you plainly what I felt. I was enjoying it. I want to be exact about where. Heat in the face, and along the forearms where they lay bare on the window ledge, and a quickening under the sternum that was not anxiety. I know what mine feels like. Being hunted by somebody good was the most interesting thing that had happened to me in three years. He had read me, and now he had written back.

Then the photograph. At first I took it for a composite, the kind of face a careful man uses because it belongs to nobody. Then I looked at it the way I look at faces for a living. The pores across the nose, open and real. A slight asymmetry at the alae, the left nostril a fraction higher. And through the lateral third of the left eyebrow a healed laceration, a fine pale line, with the hair growing back white along it. Nobody puts a scar like that on a composite. Composites are drawn by people who want you to remember them, and scars are given you by people who don't.

I ran a reverse image search on the clean machine, and it came back in seconds, which told me he had wanted it to. A public profile. A veterinary technician. A greyhound the colour of wet slate, asleep on a sofa with its legs folded like a deckchair. A sister's wedding. A birthday cake with candles reading 31.

The profile had a real face. It was a woman who looked like me: my jaw, my widow's peak, the parting on the same side, a few years younger. He had chosen her for that. Her name was Tessa Quinn.

* * *

By nine on Tuesday morning the internet had her name. He had left the image findable, and it had been found, and it moved the way infection moves through a ward, along the hands. I followed it between cases on my phone, from the public side only, where I was one of thousands of people looking and therefore no one.

I'll give you the list as I'd give it in a report. Inside forty-eight hours: her home address, posted. Northgate Animal Hospital, where she worked, its phones flooded until it closed for a day. A man livestreaming from the clinic car park, narrating the cars. *KILLER*, sprayed across her garage door. The police, asking her where she had been that Sunday. And a thread of strangers analysing her hands in a photograph of her holding a spaniel puppy, with arrows. It was a woman with her palm under a puppy's chest and its hindquarters along her forearm, the way you are taught to carry anything that might wriggle. They had found a killer's grip in a photograph of a woman holding a spaniel, which is a thing you can do if you have never held a scalpel or a spaniel. I corrected their anatomy in my head, all of it, and could not stop.

Mid-morning the office's public information officer came down with a sheet of paper and a pen. The Chief wanted Mara kept out of it, and I had been senior reviewer on Pell, so it was mine to approve. One line. The office *does not comment on active investigations*. It answered the whole of that day by saying nothing at all, and I signed it. By the afternoon a wire story was quoting Dr. Iris Vale, the senior pathologist who reviewed the autopsy. The office does not release photographs of its pathologists. My name was in the news and my face was not. I was in the news for a sentence that said nothing, which is the most accurate thing ever printed about me.

That evening I braised a lamb shoulder. I browned it hard until the fat on its back went the colour of toffee and spat at me, then a head of garlic split into cloves in their skins, rosemary, half a bottle of wine, the lid on, the oven low, and the flat filling slowly with wine and resin and sweet lamb fat until it was in the curtains and my hair. While it went I chopped the salsa verde on the board, parsley and capers and anchovies and too much lemon, and the tablet stood against the fruit bowl with the War Room running live.

Later Theo Akers turned his laptop round to show the table that the volunteer was trending. Somebody cheered. Brandt said, "Don't share it." He said it without looking up, the way you'd tell a dog to leave something, and then he took out his own phone and watched it for a long time with nothing on his face at all. I couldn't read his screen at that resolution. I didn't need to.

I narrated it for him, because somebody should. The bait only works if nobody cuts the line. If a woman is drowning in public and everyone believes she deserves it, the only person who will swim out to her is the person who knows she doesn't, and that person is the one he wants. He wasn't watching Tessa Quinn. Nobody fishing watches the worm. He was watching the water around her, for a ring. Correct. Full marks.

And mine, since I owe you it. The tactical reason first: any contact, a call, a message, a word to the investigator, would be the rise to his fly, and I am not a trout. That is true. It isn't the reason. The reason is that Tessa Quinn is not on the syllabus. There are three names in my drawer. There is no fourth. I am telling you that the way I told it to myself, with the lamb going in the oven and the lemon on my fingers, and it sounded very clean.

On Wednesday morning Mara stopped at my office door with her hair still in its cap and her arms folded across her scrubs.

"They're saying a vet tech," she said.

"I saw."

"It's my word. They've got my word." She came half a step in. "Iris, the adhesive alone..."

"Not in the corridor."

She stopped. She looked at me, and I let her look.

"It's still in the file," I said. "Where you put it. Go and wash up."

She went. I listened to her down the corridor until the doors took her.

I was in the suite until noon. At twenty past twelve I sat down at my desk with my lunch in its glass container, the cold lamb and the salsa verde in two compartments, and the message light on the desk phone was going. One new message. Left at 11:38 a.m. I played it on the handset with the receiver against my ear and the container's lid in my other hand.

Forty-seven seconds. A woman's voice, close and compressed by the cheap line, with a breath taken before each sentence the way people breathe before they jump.

"Hi. My name's Tessa Quinn. I'm sorry. I'm sorry to call the office like this." She was looking for the pathologist in the news, the woman, because she hadn't written the name down. She had never met Diane Pell. She had never been to the reservoir. The police had asked her where she was that Sunday. There were men outside her house. She had thought that someone who actually knew what had happened could just say so. "I'd just like to talk to someone who knows. If you can." She said a number, slowly. And behind her the whole time, on a hard floor, a dog shifting its weight, the claws ticking as it moved and settled and moved again.

I pressed 7. I watched my finger do it. It was steady. The phone gave its small electronic chirp, and the automated voice told me, in the pleasant tone the county pays for, that the message had been moved to the archive. Everybody in county service knows that nothing in a public office is ever deleted. It is only moved to a folder nobody opens. I have never opened mine. I did not write the number down.

I listened to it once. I deleted it. I ate lunch. The lamb was cold and dense, and the fat had set white in its seams overnight and melted on my tongue as it warmed, slow and sweet and faintly of rosemary, and the salsa verde cut through it, sharp and green and briny with caper, the anchovy coming up underneath, salt and rot, and too much lemon, so that my jaw ached at the hinge with it. I took the fork from my desk drawer and ate all of it, sitting down. It was as good as it had been the night before.

* * *

By ten that night every window in the flat was open, and the heat had settled into the rooms like a guest who won't take the hint. Somebody down the street had a stereo on, music with a lot of horns in it that I couldn't name and didn't try to. I stood at the counter with the heel of the loaf and the end of the salsa verde in its jar, and I didn't eat them. I was thinking about machinery.

An account that regular is not run from a man's phone at a conference table. It is run from somewhere that keeps hours. Nine days earlier I had followed his car out of the hotel garage at eleven at night to a building in the warehouse district, and I knew where it stood and nothing about what was in it, and I would have liked very much to see inside it. I didn't go that night. I put the bread back in its bag. I want to be exact about this. It was ten at night, I had eaten nothing since lunch, and I wasn't hungry. I am reporting it.

Detachment is the first principle of my trade, and the syllabus took it whole. You meet the body before you meet the name. You do the findings, and only then do you read the history, because a history is a story somebody else wants you to believe, and the body has never once tried to win me over. Read the history first and you find what it told you to find. The syllabus is the same rule, widened. Three names. Everybody else is history that isn't available.

I kept to it, mostly. I did not search for Tessa Quinn that night. There was a tab still open on the laptop from Monday, and I closed it without looking at it. I will admit that I looked long enough to know what it was. It was the greyhound.

But I can tell you what I knew, because I hadn't tried to know it, and it was there anyway, the way findings are. The scar through the lateral third of the left eyebrow, and the hairs growing back white along it. The name of the clinic. Her age. The greyhound's name, which was Pilot. What colour her garage door had been under the paint. I knew the dog's name. I hadn't asked for it. Nobody ever asks me for anything, and I know everything anyway. That is not care. That is fifteen years of being trained to see what is in front of me and write it down. A pathologist cannot unsee a finding.

There is something here. I am going to be honest with you about that much. I can see the outline of it the way you see a shadow on a scan before anyone has put a calliper to it, a difference in density, a place where bone-white goes a shade towards ash, and I am not going to measure it. I am telling you that I am not. Watch me not.

I unlocked the drawer. The brass key turned with its small oiled click and the drawer came out on its long wooden sigh, and the lamp made its pool on the walnut, and there was his dossier on top, still a quarter-inch to the left of the knot, where he had put it and I had left it. I lifted the three files out as a block, fingers spread under the bottom one and thumb flat on the top, the way he had, and set them on the desk without letting his slide. I slid the sleeve out from under the photographs and my agreement and took out Lesson Two.

The paper had gone soft at the corners from two years of handling. *Excellent.* sat in the margin beside the subject line, in red, in my hand, where I had written it a fortnight before. The red pen stayed capped in the tray. I took the black pen from the tray and uncapped it, and it smelled of what it was, ink and a little solvent, faint, and under that, on my own fingers, lamb fat. I had washed my hands twice since lunch. I could still smell it.

Below *Excellent.* I wrote *Tessa Quinn*, in the small upright hand I use for case numbers, so that it could be read by anyone and mistaken by no one. The reason was administrative. A name that isn't written down goes on following you about. Once it is written, it is filed.

Somewhere a car alarm started up a couple of streets away and went on for a while, and stopped on its own, as they do, without anybody coming.

I drew his dossier to the edge of the page and laid my left palm flat on it, on his name, and held it down. I set the nib at the start of her name and drew it along the manila edge to the end, one stroke, the nib dragging very slightly where the card had softened, straight and level, without lifting. My hands were steadier than they had been all week. I had never struck through a name before. The line was the crack.

I capped the pen. I put Lesson Two back in the sleeve, squared to its own edge, and the sleeve back under the photographs and my agreement, and I lowered the files onto the stack as a block, and set his a quarter-inch left of the knot, and checked it against my fingernail. Then I closed the drawer and locked it.

I wasn't tired. I went to bed anyway and lay on top of the sheet with the windows open and the heat on my skin, full still from lunch, and did not sleep nine hours, or anything like it. Late, the stereo down the street stopped. In the quiet it left I found I had something by heart that I had never tried to learn. Wednesday 30 September. 11:38 a.m. Forty-seven seconds. I had deleted the message and kept the time.
