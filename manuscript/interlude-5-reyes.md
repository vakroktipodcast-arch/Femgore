# Interlude — Reyes

The notebook was in the inside pocket of her jacket, and she could feel its spiral through the lining as the service lift went down. Eleven names in block capitals, alphabetical. Dates against the first eight. The Sunday ones ticked, and this morning's. VALE against Wednesday, ten o'clock. Two question marks.

The first was a woman who had left the country. Her signatory record gave a forwarding address abroad, and campaign counsel held her contact details and called them privileged. The second was the eleventh name, the one after Vale: a disconnected phone number and an address she had left within a year of signing. Every database she could reach had ended where the paper did. Neither of her two voicemails to counsel about them today had been returned.

She was here as campaign-protection liaison, on page four of the run of show. Her visitor lanyard had turned itself round again. She was in flat shoes, and she had not eaten since a sandwich at four.

A staffer upstairs had told her counsel was at the bottom of the service lift, taking a call where there was signal, so she had come down to ask in person. The doors opened on the lift lobby, and past it on the dock-level corridor. Painted cinderblock under a strip light. A laundry cart and a catering trolley against the wall. It smelled of fryer oil and bleach, and cigarette smoke drifting in from the bay. A cold draught came down from the dock, and warm wet air came out of the kitchen to meet it. The ballroom was two floors up. She felt its drums through the soles of her shoes more than she heard them.

Counsel was round the corner of the lobby, at the far end, with a coat over his shoulders and his phone to his ear. He saw her and lowered the phone.

"After the election, Agent Reyes."

He said it pleasantly. It was the answer he had given her to everything since the records request, except what a judge had made him give. She had it in writing three times, in longer words.

She opened her mouth.

The radios went. Every radio in earshot at once, and none of them hers. A banquet captain's, through the kitchen door. And far up the corridor, the set in the glass booth of the dock office, half a beat behind, so the same words came from two places. "Lockdown, lockdown. Stairs and lifts are locked. Hold all staff where they are." Then a second voice, faster, giving a room. The Alder Suite. Third floor.

Counsel still had the phone in his hand. He was still smiling. The smile had not caught up with the news.

Her right hand was on the holster before she had decided anything. Twenty years of that. She turned from him and went up the corridor towards the dock office and whoever in it could open the stair, fast, a stride short of a run, because the corridor had people in it. She did not look back at him.

People flattened against the walls. A porter. Two servers holding their trays at chest height. Up in the booth the security man had his back to the corridor and was shouting the room into the radio. Everyone in the corridor was stopping, or turning towards the noise. Reyes had stood in enough corridors to know there was a third thing, and that it was rare.

One person was walking the other way, towards the vendor gate. A catering jacket. A black bag on her left shoulder. She was not hurrying and she was not stopping.

They passed close.

Reyes took her in the order she always did. The jacket first: the caterer's black, the same as the servers'. Then the hands. The woman was already looking at Reyes's hands, before her face, the way police do, and for the length of a stride Reyes read hers back, the left one lying on the strap of the bag, open and still, while the trays shifted against the wall behind it, and the right one flat on the jacket over the left side of her chest, holding something there through the cloth. Flat. Fingers together. Not a grip. They were the only steady hands in the corridor.

Then the face. A woman in her thirties. Thin. No make-up. Pale under the strip light, shadowed under the eyes, the hair pulled back. No expression. It was not a blank face. It was the face of someone at work.

She was already putting it in the notebook in her head. Wrong hands. Wrong direction. Then the booth radio said the room again, and she was the Bureau's person in this building on somebody else's schedule. The woman was behind her. Reyes broke into a run. Her thumb lay on the holster strap and did not open it. The notebook knocked against her ribs. Her breath went short, and her shoes were loud on the concrete.

The hand came up the corridor with her. Flat on the chest, on Election Eve, in a building full of flags. Like a woman saying the pledge.

She would spend the rest of her career remembering that face. That night she didn't stop.
