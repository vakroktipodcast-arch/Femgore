# Interlude — Reyes

The notebook was in the inside pocket of her jacket, and she could feel its spiral through the lining as the service lift went down. Eleven names in block capitals, alphabetical. Dates against the eight before VALE, the Sunday ones and this morning's ticked. VALE against Wednesday, ten o'clock. Two question marks.

The first was a woman who had left the country. Her line gave a forwarding address abroad, and campaign counsel held her contact details and called them privileged. The second was the eleventh name, the one after Vale. Her agreement gave a disconnected phone number and an address she had left within a year of signing. Reyes had left counsel two voicemails about them today. Neither had been returned. Counsel was in the building tonight, so she had come to ask in person.

She was here as campaign-protection liaison, on page four of the run of show. She had asked for anything but protection. Protection was where they put you when they wanted you somewhere you could be seen. Her visitor lanyard had turned itself round again. She had not eaten since a sandwich at four.

A staffer upstairs had told her counsel was downstairs, at the bottom of the service lift, taking a call where there was signal.

The doors opened on the lift lobby, and past it on the dock-level corridor. Painted cinderblock under a strip light. A laundry cart and a catering trolley against the wall. It smelled of fryer oil and bleach, and cigarette smoke drifting in from the bay. A cold draught came down from the dock, and warm wet air came out of the kitchen to meet it. The ballroom was two floors up. She felt its drums through the soles of her shoes more than she heard them.

Counsel was at the lobby end, with a coat over his shoulders and his phone to his ear. He saw her and lowered the phone.

"After the election, Agent Reyes."

He said it pleasantly. It was the only answer he had given her since the records request. She had it in writing three times.

She opened her mouth.

The radios went. Every radio in earshot at once, and none of them hers. The hotel security man's. A kitchen porter's, on his apron. And far up the corridor, the set in the glass booth of the dock office, half a beat behind the others, so the same words came from three places. "Lockdown, lockdown. Stairs and lifts are locked. Hold all staff where they are." Then a second voice, faster, giving a room. The Alder Suite. Third floor.

Counsel still had the phone at his ear. He was still smiling. The smile had not caught up with the news.

Her right hand was on the holster before she had decided anything. Twenty years of that. She turned from him and went up the corridor towards the dock office and the stair door beyond it, fast, a stride short of a run, because the corridor had people in it and she did not yet know what she was running to. She did not look back at him.

People flattened against the walls. A porter. Two servers holding their trays at chest height. The security man was shouting the room into his radio. Everyone in the corridor was doing one of two things. They were stopping, or they were turning towards the noise. Reyes had stood in enough corridors to know there was a third thing, and that it was rare.

One person was walking the other way, towards the vendor gate. A catering jacket. A black bag on her left shoulder. She was not hurrying and she was not stopping.

They passed close.

Reyes took her in the order she always did. The jacket first: the caterer's black, the same as the servers'. Then the hands. The woman looked at Reyes's hands before her face, the way police do. Reyes looked at hers. The left hand lay on the strap of the bag, open and still. The right was flat on the jacket over the left side of her chest, holding something there through the cloth. They were the only steady hands in the corridor.

Then the face. A woman in her thirties. Thin. No make-up. Pale under the strip light, shadowed under the eyes, the hair pulled back. No expression. It was not a blank face. It was the face of someone at work.

Reyes had no words for it. She noticed that she had none.

Something at the back of her head had started writing it down anyway. Wrong hands. Wrong direction. Then the security man's radio said the room again, and she was the Bureau's person in this building on somebody else's schedule, and twenty years had taught her which way to run. The woman was behind her. Reyes broke into a run. The flat shoes were why she could. The notebook knocked against her ribs. Her breath went short, and her shoes were loud on the concrete.

The hand came up the corridor with her. Flat on the chest, the fingers together, on Election Eve, in a building full of flags. Like a woman saying the pledge.

She would spend the rest of her career remembering that face. That night she didn't stop.
