# Chapter 20

The weak point was not the Secret Service. I want that down first, because it took me three maps to see it, and when I did see it I was standing at my own kitchen table at ten on a Friday night with a cup of coffee gone cold at my elbow. The Service guards the man. That is the whole of its brief, and it is very good at it. Nobody guards the paperwork about the people who stand near him.

The plans had come off the printer at the desk still warm, and I had carried them through against my chest and laid them out edge to edge under the one bulb, the way you lay films on a lightbox, so that the floors met at the stairs. They smelled of hot toner, dry and scorched and faintly sweet, the smell of a library photocopier at closing time. They were the only warm thing in the flat. The heating had been off since the night of the suite. I put my palm flat on the top sheet before I read it, and took it off again when the warmth went, which was quickly.

Everything on the table had come off the card I brought out of Lantern the Saturday before, after dark, on the cloned badge and the midline. The tap was still on the uplink as well, sending me its thin summary of the campaign's mail, near enough live. The abuse desk had restored the line after its review, and the farm had gone back to humming in its warehouse, copying the campaign to itself for a dead man under the signature of a dead woman. Holt had locked Brandt out of everything Brandt had built for him. Nobody had switched off the farm, because nobody but Brandt had known it was there, and nobody but me knew about the tap.

The credentials first. The Service screens bodies and bags. It does not make badges. Back-of-house press and volunteer credentials were the campaign's own business, printed on the campaign's own badge printer in the volunteer office, from one template, and checking who had been issued what against who ought to have been was an audit, and the audit was Compliance's job. I opened the file. *Last modified by D. Pell.* Friday 11 September, the Friday before the reservoir. Nobody had changed a cell of it since. In the War Room they said Compliance had been dead two months. That was rounding up. It had been seven weeks. I graded the audit anyway, out of habit: thorough, current to the day it stopped, and stopped. Fail, for reasons beyond the candidate's control.

The template was in the card's copy of the volunteer drive, and so was the roster. I went down it slowly, the way I go down a list of medications, looking for the one nobody is taking. Ruth Calder. Signed up in July, worked one phone bank, made eleven calls, never came back. Still marked active, because marking people inactive had also been Compliance. Ruth had made eleven calls and gone home. I liked her. She knew when to stop.

I changed nothing in their database. I didn't need to write anything; I only needed to print. The card printer had been secondhand and paid for in cash, and the stock in its hopper was the same white PVC, from the supplier whose name was on the campaign's own invoice. The printer took a blank with a small ratchet of rollers and pulled it through and gave it back to me warm: the navy-and-gold header, the gold band along the foot that means back of house, and three lines. RUTH CALDER. VOLUNTEER. PRESS ASSISTANT. No photograph. Hers was a real name on a real roster, and it would already be on any list they printed for the door.

At eleven the tablet gave me counsel's email to the War Room, sent that afternoon. The subpoena fight was lost. The signatory list was to be produced on Saturday. Special Agent E. Reyes would have eleven names by the weekend. I read it twice and gave it a B: clear, short, and dishonest only in the word *regrettably*, which a man uses when he means expensive. I found my fingertips on my left flank, through the jumper, on the ridge of the scar, and left them there while I did the only arithmetic there was. Ruth Calder had made eleven calls. Numbers do that. They don't mean anything by it. I didn't think about the order the names would be read in. I thought about Monday.

The Calloway files floor plans for its ballroom level, on three, and the advance team had a copy. I read it the way I read a body. Hotels are built like people: a face for the guests, and behind it the plumbing. I have spent my whole career in the plumbing. In front, the grand ballroom, the foyer, the press riser. Behind, the corridors nobody photographs. Stair C, a service stair beside the green-room corridor, its door about ten paces from the green-room door. A housekeeping closet one floor down, on two. A loading dock at street level on the cross street, with a vendor gate beside the shutter that fire code requires to open from inside on a button, and a note in the hotel's small capitals: 19 S. I traced the route with the red from the pen tray, capped, using the cap and not the tip, the way you point at an X-ray without touching the film.

Then the minute. I wrote it down as a protocol, standing, in pencil. Sixty seconds, from the footage. The door closes. The detail turns its back, a hand flat on the wood. Allow five seconds for him to sit down with the photograph. Count to five. After that I wrote nothing, because what came after was the lesson, and the lesson had been written for two years.

The green room was the Alder Suite. The Service's advance plan showed it with one door. The hotel's plan, drawn for banquet staff and not for anybody with an earpiece, showed two: the corridor door, and a narrow door from the suite's pantry onto the back corridor, so that the coffee could come in without the guests seeing who carried it. I went to the security annex and found the walk-through photographs, and found that corridor, and that door, and enlarged it to four hundred per cent until the grain of the paint came up like skin under a hand lens.

A white label ran across the gap between the door and the frame at shoulder height, corridor side.

I knew it before I could read it. It is the same make of tamper-evident seal I put across the mouth of every evidence bag at work, initialled across the join, the kind that leaves VOID standing in the paint if anyone so much as lifts a corner. I have sealed hundreds. For a moment the old click came, the one a finding makes when it seats, the small sound of a thing going exactly where it belongs. Then it was gone. I noted that it had gone, and I noted the time.

The green room was a converted meeting suite with a service door the Service had sealed but the hotel floor plan still showed. The seal was a sticker.

* * *

The only paper in the flat was the stock I had printed the syllabus on. Heavy, cream, a little toothy, bought two years ago in a single ream because I had not wanted to be seen buying it twice. I noticed that, sitting at the desk on Saturday night with the pen uncapped, and I used it anyway. There wasn't anything else.

It took ink slower than writing paper does. The nib dragged, and the line sat wet on the surface for a second before the fibres took it in, so that each word stayed shining behind the pen for the length of the next. I had never written on it by hand. The syllabus had been typed. Everything I had put on that paper had been put there by a machine, at my instruction, and now it was going to be my hand, at nobody's.

The phone was face-down at my elbow. Hannah's voicemail was on it, a week old now, one minute and fourteen seconds, unplayed. I was writing back to a voicemail I hadn't played. It is a thing people do at the end, I'm told. I had always assumed it was the bereaved.

It was Halloween. Down the corridor somebody had a lantern carved on the landing, and the smell of scorched pumpkin flesh came under my door with the cold. Children went up and down the building in twos and threes, knocking. The knocks came along the corridor, a door, a door, a shriek, a door, and reached mine, and waited, and went on. There was nothing in the flat to give them. Hannah had sent a photograph in the afternoon. Astrid in a cape she was too old for and knew it, the hem held up with clear tape, every seam doubled. June as a skeleton, the bones printed white on black jersey. I counted the ribs. Ten on the left, twelve on the right. Twelve pairs, I thought, and it didn't come out funny. It came out as a number. I turned the phone back over.

I have taken hundreds of letters out of coat pockets on my table. I know what they say and the order they say it in. The practical things, then the apology, then the memory, then the apology again, longer. I have read more of these than anyone I know. Most of them mention the cat. As a form it is underpowered and overworked, and I would mark it down for structure, and I could feel mine falling into it as I wrote, line by line, the way a body falls into the shape of the table, and I couldn't write it out of me.

So I wrote what had to be there. The spare key, and who has the other one. The lease, and the clause about notice. The bank, and the drawer of the kitchen where the statements are. Then a sentence I wrote and read and left: that if anyone tried to hand her my things in a clear plastic bag with a label on it, she was to say no, and make them bring them some other way. I know what those bags look like. I have filled them. And one memory, set down plainly and not underlined. The crusts at the park in August, the ones June wouldn't eat, and how Hannah ate them without noticing she was doing it, as if she were tidying. I didn't say why I remembered it. I didn't know how to put that on paper without making it the point.

What I left out, I can give you exactly, because I went down it like a list.

The agreement, eight pages and a signature page in blue ink. Holt, whom Hannah likes, and the veterans thing, which she believes is real, and which is. June's sticker, and my thumb pressing it down until it had decided to stay. Diane. Brandt. The two voicemails in the archive at work, thirty-four seconds and forty-seven, and the third, face-down at my elbow, a minute and fourteen. Any sentence that could be read as sorry. I wasn't, and I wasn't going to start in ink.

I wrote it to be read twice. First by whoever came through the door if I didn't, with gloves on and a camera, who would read it for what it gave away. Then by Hannah, later, at a table, for what it didn't. So I redacted as I went, the way I had redacted the syllabus, a word lifted here, a clause left short there, and I heard the voice it was doing it in. It was the same one. It was good at this.

I squared it on the desk above the drawer, where it would be found. Then I thought about who would open the drawer after that, and what they would find in it, and in what order, and I stopped.

I signed it with the name Hannah used for me. It was probably somewhere in the voicemail; I wouldn't know. After the third letter my hand stopped on its own, with the nib still on the paper and the ink spreading into the tooth of it, and I had to put the pen down.

* * *

The clocks went back that night. The country gave everybody an hour. I used mine to be early.

My body woke me at what was now four, and lay there knowing it was five. The flat smelled of cold radiators and the coffee I hadn't drunk on Friday. I made a fresh pot and drank it black, standing, and ate the heel of a loaf that had gone hard at the cut end, tearing it, chewing it a long time, because the crust was the only thing in the kitchen that asked for any effort. It tasted of flour and of the fridge. That was Sunday morning.

On set-up day the Calloway brings its volunteers in through the loading dock, because the lobby is being dressed and nobody wants a woman with a lanyard walking across a freshly laid carpet. I came in at eight on Ruth Calder's. The dock smelled of wet cardboard and diesel from a truck idling at the bay, and of chafing-fuel gel, sweetish and blue, from somewhere further in. A receiving supervisor sat in the glass booth with a breakfast sandwich in its paper and a hotel radio in its cradle on the desk beside him, and he looked at the lanyard, not at the badge, and not at me. Nobody held it against a list. A lanyard means somebody else has already checked. I walked the corridor in, past the service-lift lobby and on towards the stair door, and I knew as I walked it that I was walking it backwards.

They gave me cable mats. Long black rubber runners, ridged, heavy enough to want two hands, to be laid along the front of the press riser so that the networks' cables could run under them and nobody would trip on a senator's last night. I laid them square and taped the seams down with gaffer, tearing it by hand off the roll, pressing each strip with the heel of my palm until it had decided to stay. Gaffer tape is the only adhesive more trusted than a lanyard. I was good at it. A young man with a clipboard said I was a lifesaver, and I said, "Thanks," and taped the next one.

At eleven I took my break. Stair C, from the ballroom level down one floor: concrete treads, a painted steel rail, a cage of emergency lighting at the landing. I went at a working walk, the pace of a woman sent for more tape, and counted. The wound pulled on the turn of the landing, lightly, a thread drawn through cloth, and let go. From the stair door on three to the closet door on two, thirty-one seconds.

The closet was unlocked; it was set-up day. A par sheet was taped inside the door, the restock schedule by shelf and by day, and I read it the way I read a chart at the end of a bed, for what was due and what wasn't. Round ivory banquet cloths, bottom shelf, not wanted until a wedding on the Wednesday. The closet smelled of starch, and of bleach under the starch, and of warm lint breathing out of the laundry chute in the wall. I knelt and lifted the stack and put the catering jacket at the bottom of it: black, with a slate collar and the outside caterer's wordmark stitched on the breast, bought from the uniform supplier for cash because of the wordmark. Folded over a plain black tote. The kit in its inside pocket. All of it went there that day, because on Monday nothing would pass the magnetometer but me, a badge, a bag, and a page. I set the cloths back down square, and looked at the stack from the door, and it was a stack of tablecloths.

At three I went out by the dock. The vendor gate is beside the shutter, and you can't see either from the booth. I pressed the green button with a knuckle, and the lock dropped, and I didn't go through. I stood inside and counted it back to locked. Nineteen. Nobody watched it close. Then I pressed it again and went out into the cross street, where the November air came off the river and found every gap in my coat.

That night the chicken went down the chute. Three weeks in brine. When I lifted the lid the brine had gone cloudy as dishwater, and what came up out of it was sour and sweetish and close, the smell of a thing that has been waiting too long for a reason. I bagged it with its plate and tied the bag and carried it down the corridor at arm's length, and heard it go a long way down. After that, tea, and water.

On Monday I had taken the day as leave. At noon I began, and I did it in the same order as before, move for move, the way you do a procedure you have done before. Shower, short; the bathroom cold enough that the steam stood in the air. Hair washed and pinned up tight. Nails short and square over the sink. No lipstick. On the bed I laid out what I would carry, and it didn't take long: the lanyard, the bag with the press folder and the charger and the tin of lip balm, the printed run-of-show. There was nothing to lay out beside them. Everything else was under a stack of tablecloths on the second floor.

The belt went to its last hole and was loose on it. I took the trussing needle out of the kitchen drawer and pushed a new hole through the leather an inch further on, and turned the needle to open it. It was the first thing I'd used it for in weeks. The navy blazer, the press assistant's jacket, went on over a shirt and sat away from me at the shoulders, looser than it had in the shop.

"You don't eat before," I said, out loud, in the kitchen, to nobody. It had been a rule about wanting. Now it was a rule. I didn't eat.

I unlocked the drawer with the brass key. The third page was where it had been, a clean print, never handled. Under it, face-down, was the page in my own handwriting, and I didn't turn it over. I took the third page and nothing else, and folded it in thirds without reading it, and put it behind the run-of-show, and put them both in the inside breast pocket on the left. The other page stayed. I locked the drawer and switched the phone off and left it face-down where it was. My hand went to the pocket. The paper was there.

On the way out, the hall mirror.

This time I didn't look like someone going on a date. I looked like someone going to work. I preferred the date.

Behind me, the letter squared to the edge of the desk. The drawer beneath it locked. The third page in my jacket, where my hand kept finding it.
