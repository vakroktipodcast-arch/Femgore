# Chapter 21

The loading dock smelled of diesel. November diesel, which is not the summer kind; it hangs lower and gets into wool. At twenty to five on Monday, Election Eve, I was standing in a line of staff and vendors that ran from the dock office along the wall under the shutter, and I could smell, in order, a catering van idling at the kerb, somebody's hairspray, the coffee on three or four people's breath, the sizing in the navy blazer I had bought for this, and last, because I am always last to my own body, a sweetish note on my own breath, like pear drops left in a warm pocket.

Ketones. I had eaten nothing since a black coffee and a heel of bread on Sunday morning, and the liver had done what the liver does when nobody feeds it, which is start burning the furniture. My stomach closed once, hard, a fist on nothing, and I logged that too, and didn't want anything. It is the smell we're taught to check for on a collapsed man's breath, before anyone in the room decides what kind of body he is. Nobody in that line was going to check mine.

The badge on my lanyard said RUTH CALDER, and under it, VOLUNTEER, and under that, PRESS ASSISTANT, on white card with the navy-and-gold MARCHETTI header and a gold band along the foot that meant back of house. No photograph. I had printed it myself on a secondhand card printer I'd paid cash for. Ruth was real. She had signed up in July, worked one phone bank, made eleven calls and gone home, and nobody had marked her inactive, because marking people inactive had been Compliance. I hadn't written a record. I'd borrowed one.

I read the line while it moved, because I can't not, because fifteen years of intake have left me unable to stand behind a man without taking his history: the sound tech two ahead of me with the square outline of a nicotine patch showing through his shirtsleeve when he hoisted his flight case; the field organiser beside him, whose hands lay on his clipboard too still for the hour and the day, a stillness somebody had prescribed for exactly this kind of evening, propranolol, I'd have said, swallowed with a glass of water at teatime; a woman of about twenty-five on a staffer's lanyard, pale at the lips, sipping ginger ale the way you sip when you are holding something down, her free hand going to her lower belly and away again before anyone could see it go, who had not told anyone yet; and at the arch ahead the agent on the post, with a dent at the base of his ring finger, the skin there narrower and paler, where a ring had been worn for years and had stopped being worn recently enough for the finger to remember it.

It was the only pleasure I had left, and I knew what it was. A habit. From the inside a habit can pass for an appetite, if you don't look at it closely.

The young women I read twice. I found I was checking each of them for one more thing, a thing there is no sign for on a body: whether anyone had already put a page in front of her, and a pen beside it, laid clip-away.

The Service had the first screen: an arch, a table, a bag check. My bag held a press-kit folder, a phone charger with no phone, and a tin of unscented lip balm. My own phone was at home on the counter, switched off. Everything else I would need tonight had been in a closet on the second floor since Sunday, because none of it could have walked through this arch, and the only thing on me that I could not have left behind was folded in thirds in my inside breast pocket, behind a printed run of show.

The arch said nothing. The wand said something at my belt. I had put a new hole in that belt at noon with a trussing needle, and the buckle sat further round my waist than its maker had meant it to.

"Arms out for me," the officer said. She had short nails and the patience of somebody a long way into a long shift. Her hands went across my shoulders and down each arm to the wrist. Over the left outer pocket, where the pale nitrile was folded small, and on; half the building had gloves about them for the food. Down my back. Then down my left side, under the ribs, and there she stopped, and pressed flat.

It was a month old. Three buried stitches, seven out on day ten, a scar I could find in the dark. Under the heel of her hand it pulled, a hard bright pull right through the wall, and the flinch started in the external oblique before I had decided anything at all. I felt the first fibres take up.

I have stood still for fifteen years while things were done to bodies. I let my breath out the way I tell families to in the viewing room, all the way, through the mouth, and let the muscle go soft under her palm, and kept my face where it was, which was pleasant and slightly bored. If she felt anything, it felt like a woman who was ticklish. She was thorough. She'd have made a good mortuary technician, if she could have learned to be thorough about the people who didn't mind.

Her hand moved on, up, over the left breast of the blazer, and the paper in the inside pocket crackled under it.

"Run of show," I said, before she asked.

"Everybody's got one." She waved me through.

On the far side of the arch I put two fingers of my right hand on my left wrist, the way I've always done it, and counted to the second hand of my watch. Sixty-four. My resting rate, to the beat. I noted it. I didn't yet know what kind of finding it was.

Past the Service line the campaign had its own desk, at the head of the back-of-house corridor. There had been no desk like it at the fundraiser, where a lanyard had meant that someone else had already checked. Somebody had learned something since August, and put a girl at a folding table to prove it.

She was nineteen. I'd have put money on it. I've put it on certificates. The skin along her jaw still had that faint hormonal unevenness that settles by the early twenties, and she had told the man in front of me that it was her first time voting tomorrow. She had a laminated list, sorted by surname, and a dry-wipe marker with its cap posted on the end.

"Name?"

"Calder. Ruth."

She ran her finger down the C's, properly, skipping nothing, and found CALDER, RUTH, and ticked it in marker, and looked up at me with total sincerity and said, "You're all set."

Diligence, full marks. Authority, none. The list checked names, and the name was real, and nobody in that corridor was checking a face against a badge that didn't have one. Matching badges to the people who had actually been issued them was the credential audit's job. The credential audit was Compliance's. The last person to modify it had done so on a Friday in September, two days before I put the paper in her mouth.

I passed because the checker was a campaign volunteer, nineteen, with a laminated list. The hole was exactly where I'd said it would be.

* * *

Radios. Every few metres a radio on somebody's shoulder said something short and broke off in a squelch, and another answered it, a building talking to itself in a language it had invented that afternoon. Under the radios, the hum of the service corridors, which is the hum of every hospital basement I have walked through. A room-service trolley went by me with a flat spot on one wheel, and I heard it all the way to the corner, tick, tick, tick, like a clock with a limp.

I knew the corridors. The plans had come off the card I took from the server farm on the twenty-fourth, and I had read them on my kitchen table like a body: the public rooms in front, the plumbing behind. Service level. Ballroom level, on three. The green-room corridor. Stair C beside it.

I walked them carrying a box of press folders from the press table, because nobody stops a woman with a box. I could have carried a body through that hotel if I'd thought to put it in one. I'd lifted it by its outer flaps with my sleeves pulled down over my hands, and I carried it that way the whole walk, the cardboard edges biting through the cuffs into the heels of my palms. Nothing of mine was going to be on that box.

Doors opened to the public at six. I heard them open, a long way off, the way you hear the sea from a car park. At a quarter past I went into Stair C and stopped on the half-landing, wedged the box against the wall with my hip, and took out the run of show to check my times.

Page four was security contacts. USSS shift lead. Venue security. Hotel engineering. And at the bottom, FBI LIAISON: SA E. REYES.

I had first read that name in October, at the foot of a letter quoted in a lawyer's email. Since then it had been a rank, an initial and a surname, and it was still a rank, an initial and a surname. I folded the page back in front of the other one.

Somebody else in this building read paper for a living.

Down one floor. Thirty-one seconds from the stair door on three to the closet door on two, the same as Sunday. The box made me take the stairs at an angle, and on the turn of the second flight the scar pulled again, a long draw from the flank round towards the back, like a seam being tested by somebody who wanted to know if it would hold. It held.

The closet smelled of starch and bleach and the warm lint that comes up out of a laundry chute. The par sheet was still taped inside the door. I propped the box on my thigh against the shelf edge, got the pale nitrile out of my left outer pocket one-handed and on, left and then right, and the snap of each cuff was small and flat in there among the linen. Then the box went down squared beside the cloth stack, and my bag beside it.

The round ivory cloths weren't due out until a Wednesday wedding. The catering jacket was where I had put it, folded at the bottom, over the black tote. I slid it out and found the kit in its inside pocket with my fingers before I looked at it, and spread it through the blazer, each thing where my hand already knew it would be. The hard case, the marker and the pin, into the left inside breast pocket, beside the page. The roll of black vinyl into the right outer pocket. The long zip-tie flat along the lining. The scalpel, No. 4 handle, guard on, into the right inside pocket, for the seal and for nothing else. The black nitrile, the second pair, into the right outer pocket with the tape.

The jacket went back over the tote and the tote went back under the cloths. Then I stripped the pale pair off inside out and folded them small and put them back in the left outer pocket. A press assistant in surgical gloves is a story somebody tells afterwards.

My hands were cold. The closet was the warmest room I had been in for three weeks, and my hands were cold in it.

Up again. The Service post at the Stair C landing took my gold band at a glance, and I went past him and looked, the way anyone would look, down the short service passage that ran behind the green room. Eight paces of painted block wall and a plain hotel door at the end of it. Across the gap between the door and the jamb, at shoulder height, a white tamper-evident sticker with the Service's legend printed on it, and under the print, invisible until somebody lifted it, the word VOID.

I have taken tape off more faces than anyone in that building. I knew what it would do. It would say so.

I counted them every time I went past. One on the landing. Two at the green-room door. The shift lead walking through every so often, a word with each post, a handoff you could set a watch by, and I did. They were competent. They were not omniscient. Nobody is.

His first event was the volunteer thank-you in the mezzanine ballroom, the room where I had first watched Diane work a crowd in ninety-second loops. His minute before it was in the green room. At seven nineteen I was on the stair below the landing when the radios said the principal was moving. I heard the post's shoes go out through the stair door, and I went up the last flight and into the passage. When the detail arrives, the stair post steps forward into the corridor to widen the cordon at the door, and while he is out there the landing has nobody on it. I had come up to see that.

At seven twenty-one I stood with my ear near the gap of the sealed door, not touching the label. Through it I heard the main door of the green room on its closer, the long hydraulic sigh and the click of the latch, and I began to count.

My heart went along under the count, slow. My stomach had gone quiet some time in the afternoon. I noted that, and didn't like it.

The latch went. Voices. He was out.

I was down the stairs before the post's shoes came back to the landing, and on the half-landing I counted it through again in my head, from the sigh to the latch, and got the same number. I didn't know why he had cut it, whether it was the schedule, or the night, or nothing. I wasn't going to get to ask.

The rhythm was tighter than the footage. The minute alone had become forty seconds. My plan was built for sixty.

* * *

The carpet in the green-room corridor gave nothing back. It was the dense, low hotel kind that takes a footstep and keeps it, and the air conditioning came down out of a grille somewhere above me and lay across the back of my neck like a cold hand that had forgotten it was there. The rally speech was at nine, in the grand ballroom. At ten to nine I was standing near the door to Stair C with my badge out and a clipboard against my chest, wearing the face of a woman waiting to be told where to stand. I ran my thumb along the edge of the badge. The PVC was slick and faintly warm from my body. It was the only warm thing I had on me.

Ten paces from the stair door to the green-room door. Behind the stair door, eight more paces of passage to the sticker. Twelve seconds, door to sticker, at a working walk. I had written that figure down on Friday night and not used it, because on Friday night I'd had sixty seconds and could afford to walk from anywhere. I had forty now. So I would already be at the stair door when he went in, and when the latch went I would have only the eight paces left.

I did the sum again. It still worked. I noticed that I had decided it worked the way you decide which queue to stand in at a supermarket, and that I felt nothing about that either way.

At eight fifty-two the radios changed tone.

They came down the corridor together, in the shape I had counted all evening: the shift lead in front, two agents, an aide with a tablet talking up at an angle, and Holt in the middle of them with his wife at his elbow. Sloane Marchetti had her hand on his sleeve. Her jaw was calm. Under the angle of it, where the collar of her dress stood off the skin, her carotid was going fast enough for me to count from where I stood, if I had wanted to.

He passed within a few metres of me. It was the nearest I had been to him in three years, if a corridor counts. In those three years he had been footage, a face on a tablet propped against a fruit bowl, a man at a rope line in somebody else's video. He didn't look at me. He was listening to the aide, head inclined, giving the aide the whole of his attention, and even sideways, even walking, even with his back half to me, you could feel it come off him, the warmth he kept for whoever was nearest. Cedar went by in his wake.

I kept my eyes on the clipboard. He was the only man in the building who didn't read my badge. He'd have known my name if I had been his staff. That was the whole problem, in a sentence.

At the door Sloane's hand left his sleeve. She went on down the corridor towards the ballroom with a staffer at her shoulder, and she didn't look back. A woman who had known enough, for a long time, not to know any more.

The cordon tightened at the door exactly as I had counted it. The stair door opened beside me, and the post came out through it and took two steps up the corridor and turned his back to me, which is what he was for. He was watching for anyone coming towards the principal. I was behind. I put my hand on the push bar of the stair door. It was cold steel, and I felt the cold of it through my palm into the bones of my wrist.

The detail stopped at the door. He went in alone. The door began to close on its closer.

In the gap before it shut: a framed photograph of a Marine on the dressing table, already turned to face the chair. From ten paces, side-on, I had a shoulder in dress blues, the white of a cover, the edge of a plain black frame on a cardboard easel, angled to where he would sit. Somebody had set it out for him. The dressing table was a banquet table with a skirt and a lamp. There was no mirror over it. A breath of warm air came out of the room into the corridor, smelling of the lamp, and then the door met its frame.

The latch.

I was already counting. I was through the stair door while the post's back was turned, and it closed behind me on its own weight. The scar pulled as I turned into the passage, one hard draw, and I let it. Eight paces. The pale nitrile came out of my left pocket and went on by feel as I walked, left and then right, and I didn't snap the cuffs. Somewhere on the other side of the wall he would be sitting down with his brother, and the detail would be looking at the corridor, which was the plan.

I counted to five and put my hand flat on the sticker. My hand was steady. It had been steady in the suite too, and then it had been happy. I wished, briefly, that it weren't steady now.
