# Chapter 27

The leaf mould came in on his trouser cuffs. Everything else in the suite was the old smell, cold iron and chlorhexidine and the faint sweetness a fresh case gives off in its first hours, before the room has made it its own, and I had stopped noticing any of it years ago. The leaf mould was different. It was wet and loamy and it had come from outside, from under a hedge, from a lawn somebody had been keeping, and it was the only thing in the room that had been anywhere.

It was twenty to eleven on a Monday, a week after, and I had a case open. He was seventy-four. He had gone down in his own garden with a rake in his hands, and his palms agreed with the history: calloused across the heads of the metacarpals, the skin of the right thumb web polished by a wooden handle. I had the heart out and on the board, and I was cross-cutting the coronaries every few millimetres with a fresh blade, and in the left anterior descending artery, not far from its origin, I found it: a fresh thrombus, dark as the inside of a plum, seated on a plaque that had split. The widow-maker. I said it into the dictaphone by its proper name and nothing else. I weighed the heart and wrote the number down. I worked well. I would like that on the record.

Mara was at the next table. When she came round to my side for the saw she stopped farther from me than she used to, and I heard the place she stopped: the soft squeak of her clogs on the wet floor, a full stride short.

When I went out to the anteroom for fresh gloves, the break-room television was on through the office door, the same set, on mute, the captions crawling under a picture of a podium. INVESTIGATORS BELIEVE SENATOR'S ATTACKER ACTED ALONE. Then: SOURCES DESCRIBE A LONE EXTREMIST. NO EVIDENCE OF WIDER PLOT. NO DESCRIPTION HAS BEEN RELEASED. At *acted alone* I put my wrist to my left side, over the scar, through the gown and the apron, which I should not have done, and took it away again.

There had been no page on him, because I had not pinned one. Two of the lessons had been found with paper pinned to their chests, and the third had not, and a story that is handed a body with nothing pinned to it will file it wherever it likes. It had liked a lone extremist. It had one.

The result had come in on the Tuesday within a point of the last poll, with a dead man's name still on the ballot. On Friday the party named a replacement. I read his name on the captions twice to keep it, and I haven't kept it. The machine was intact. Only the man was gone.

The same day a single lawyer put out a statement for three women, unnamed, former campaign staff. Three. I counted them the way I count towels at the end of a case. I was not one of them.

I closed the gardener. Surgical tape on the specimen labels, my initials, the date. Mara passed me the needle holder across the table, handle first, at the full length of her arm. She had not hummed since October. When my back was turned she looked at my hands. I knew by the clogs. They stopped, and the hose in her hand went on running over the same few inches of steel.

I let her. I didn't ask about her weekend. I didn't make the joke that would have made it easier. She was watching exactly the right thing, in exactly the right proportion, and she was afraid of precisely what there was to be afraid of. Full marks.

My coffee was on the office desk where I had put it at eight. It had a skin on it. I didn't drink it, and I wasn't going to go to lunch, and my belt was on the hole I had made with the trussing needle and was loose on that.

The Chief had done the Senator himself, on the Tuesday, with two federal observers in the room and Mara assisting. He wanted, I assume, a face that had never been near politics; Mara's had now been over all three of its bodies. I was not asked, and I did not ask for the report. My hands had written most of it.

At the sinks we scrubbed side by side, a basin apart. She told me the way I had taught her to report a finding: flat, in order, nothing in front of the fact.

"The FBI rang at nine," she said. "Special Agent Reyes. The Chief's on the Senator's file, and I've got the rota this month, so it came to me."

"What did she want?"

"Your shift pattern this week. I said days." She turned off her tap with her elbow. "She said you were meant to see her last Wednesday. Before."

The water ran over my wrists at the temperature I had set it, which was no colder than my flat.

"Did you know?" Mara said.

"No."

It was true. It was one of very few true things I said that day.

I had a face for the voice she was reporting. Flat shoes. A trouser suit cut a little long on the right. Short unpainted nails, and a biro mark on the middle finger of the right hand, and a corridor half a second long. E. Reyes. I didn't tell Mara that.

I didn't ask what else the agent had said. Mara told me anyway, drying her hands on a paper towel one finger at a time, because it was in the order and she hadn't reached the end of it.

"She was explaining the lost week," she said. "She's going down a list. In order, she said. Eight of them done by election night. Then on Friday one came in through a lawyer, and she was the last name on it. I don't know what the list is for. She didn't say. So that's nine of the eleven. There are two left. A woman who's left the country." She put the towel in the bin. "And Dr. Vale. Postponed by Election Eve. Rescheduled for Thursday."

* * *

I had bought a chicken on the walk home. I remember the paper it came in, and the weight of it in the crook of my arm, about a kilo and a half, and the man at the counter saying something about the cold, and that I answered him. It was on the kitchen counter in its paper.

The heating was still off. I was standing at the writing desk with my coat on when the phone lit up on the wood and said HANNAH. Under her name the notification was still there where it had been for two weeks: voicemail, 1:14.

I answered.

Her kitchen came down the line before she did, warm and ordinary and a little compressed, the way everything is through a phone: the dishwasher in its long churn, a tap running and stopping, and somewhere farther off June singing something from school, the same four words over and over with total conviction. My flat had nothing to set against it. The fridge. My own breath. The phone was warm against my ear from the battery, and it was the only warm thing in the room; the hand I had put down flat on the desk had gone cold through the palm, and I left it there.

"There you are," Hannah said. Not unkindly.

"I've been busy."

I have said that to Hannah a great many times. I have never once said it and been lying in quite that way.

"Did you get my message?"

"I did. Sorry."

I had not played it. It was against my cheek while I said so. She let it go, because Hannah always lets it go; she has been letting things go for me since she was old enough to hold them.

So I asked about the girls, and I am going to tell you how I did it, because it was done cleanly and I want you to see the work. I asked about Astrid's reading book by name, the one she had been reading on her own, not to anyone, and when Hannah told me I could hear that she was pleased to be asked, the small lift at the start of her sentence, and I asked the second question, the one that proves you listened to the first answer. That is the one people remember. Anybody can ask the first. I asked whether June had lost her first tooth yet. "Only you," Hannah said, and laughed, and told me. I asked because at six it is the lower central incisors that go first, and I know children by their teeth the way other people know them by their birthdays. I laughed a half-beat after Hannah laughed, which is where a laugh sounds most like it was surprised out of you. I kept my voice low and slightly slow, the voice for families, the one that leaves room after each thing it says so the other person can put something down in it. I didn't fill any of the room myself. I can describe it the way I would describe a colleague closing: even bites, even tension, every knot laid down the same way. My left hand was flat on the desk a few inches from the letter, which was still squared to the edge, still unsent, and I was saying the ordinary things to my sister with the other things written out and lying beside my hand. I didn't look down at it.

"She loved it, by the way," Hannah said. "The microscope. She's been looking at everything. Her own hair. A dead fly off the windowsill."

I had known it was a microscope, from the photograph, and that it was signed *from Iris* in Hannah's hand, and had gone out in more clear tape than paper. I hadn't known what June would put under it. Hannah had chosen it. She had chosen something in my register, for a six-year-old, without asking me, and she had been right.

"I'm glad," I said.

The tap ran again. "Isn't it awful," she said. "About Marchetti."

She said it the way people say it about weather, because everyone was saying it, at bus stops and in queues and over the heads of children, and it was a thing you said now, like the nights drawing in.

"Mm."

"June asked me if the man on her sticker was dead. I didn't know what to say to her. She's six."

I told her. I kept it short, because Hannah would be repeating it, and you should always give people something they can repeat.

"Tell her yes," I said. "Tell her his body stopped working and it can't start again. Don't say he's sleeping, or she won't want to go to sleep. Don't say he went away, or she'll wait. Answer what she asks. Only that. She'll ask the rest when she's ready for it, and then you answer that."

I have said it to a great many families. Nobody has ever complained about the wording.

"That's good," Hannah said. "That's really good. Thank you."

June had stopped singing. The dishwasher went into its rinse.

"You sound tired," Hannah said.

"I am."

It was the only thing I said all evening that wasn't technique.

We began to say goodbye, which in our family takes a while and has its own order, and she got to the end of it the way she has for years.

"Call me Sunday?"

I began to say Sunday. I had the shape of it ready, the way you have your keys out before you reach the door. And then there was no day to put there. I didn't know when I would be able to call her back. The week had a Thursday in it now that belonged to somebody else's diary, and I could not see past it to any Sunday. There was a gap where the day should have been, and I do not leave gaps with Hannah; I have spent years making sure there are none she could fall into. My tongue had dried against my palate, and I had to peel it away to speak.

"Soon," I said.

She was quiet for two seconds, which is one more than Hannah has ever needed. I heard the tap, and the fridge in my own kitchen, and nothing else.

"Okay," she said. And then, "Love you."

She had heard it.

* * *

The kitchen table was under its one bulb, and at twenty past nine I was sitting at it, at my own table, the way I had told myself in October I would.

The page was on the wood where I had put it down when I came in from the walk a week before, folded once, the thirds still in it. It had not been opened since. Beside it was a plate.

I had roasted the chicken after the call. I hadn't decided to do that either. I had found myself at the counter with the bird out of its paper and patted dry, and the salt in my palm and then on the skin, and the oven as hot as it goes, and I had watched my hands do it as if I had come into the room late and they had started without me. No brine. When it came out I had set it on the board to rest and stood over it far longer than any bird needs resting, and then I had carved one side off it myself, leg and wing and breast, and left the oyster on the frame, and that was on the plate with bread beside it for the juices. The rest of the bird was in the tin, back in the oven, which I had turned off. The oven had been on for an hour and a half, and the kitchen was warm. I could feel it on my face and on the backs of my hands. It was the first heat in the flat since the night I came home from the suite. I noted the temperature. I didn't note anything else.

Smell first. Rendered fat, salt, the brown-sugar smell of the crust where the skin had caught and gone the colour of a violin, and under it the clean, faintly sweet steam that comes off the breast when you cut into it. Nothing happened in my mouth.

I sat with it. My hands were in my lap. The steam stopped coming off the plate, and the juices round the thigh went from moving to still, and the skin lost its shine and began to look like skin. My tongue was dry against my teeth. I didn't touch anything.

When the skin had gone dull all over, I picked up the page instead.

I didn't unfold it. I carried it across to the bin and put my foot on the pedal, and the lid came up, and my hand was over the opening with the page in it, held by one corner between my finger and thumb. All my hand had to do was open. It didn't open. It didn't shake, either. It stayed where it was, over the bin, the way it had stayed over a man's chest a week ago, holding a sheet of paper it would neither put down nor put away. I stood there until the hinge of the lid creaked under its own weight, and I took my foot off the pedal, and the lid came down.

I took it to the desk. On the way there was the letter, squared to the edge where it had been for nine days, and against the side of the desk, sleeve to the room, a record, still in its cellophane. I turned the brass key and opened the drawer.

The fourth page was where I had put it, face-down, on top of the working masters and the dossiers. I slid the third page in on top of it, still folded, and the two of them lay together, the one I couldn't pin on the one I hadn't. I closed the drawer. The key stayed in the lock.

I came back to the table and sat down. And I ate.

I took the leg in my fingers and the skin broke under my thumb like the roof of a blister and the salt was on my tongue before the meat was, coarse and sharp and then gone into the fat, and the fat was warm still at the bone, where the heat keeps longest, and ran down the inside of my wrist, and I found the knee with my thumb, felt for the place, and it gave the way they always give when you know where, and the thigh came away from the drumstick dark and loose and close-grained, with the faint iron at the bone that is the only honest thing about a chicken, and I put it in my mouth and my mouth flooded so fast it ached under the jaw, the parotids going like taps, and I ate it with my eyes shut, and then the drumstick, the tendons in it like wet string, and then the wing, which is all skin and fuss and which I have never once left on a plate, and then the breast in long torn pieces, wiped through the juices and through the salt on the plate, and I didn't stop to put any of it down.

I ate the bread through what was left on the plate until the plate shone. Then I got up and took the tin out of the oven with a cloth and stood over it for a moment on the hob, and pulled the other leg off at the hip, standing, and then I took the whole tin back to the table and sat down with it, because I sit after. I ate that half too. All of it.

There are two small muscles on the back of a chicken, one in a shallow dip on either side of the spine just above the thighs, and every cook who knows about them eats them before the plate leaves the kitchen. I was the cook. Nobody was waiting. I turned the carcass over and dug them out with my thumb, both of them, and ate them one after the other.

The bones went onto the rim of the plate as they came clean. I wiped the tin with the last of the bread. My stomach, after a week of tea and water, sat high and tight under my belt, hard as a fist, distended, and I noted it the way I would have dictated it. I knew the word for what a meal like that does to a body that has been living on tea, and I had known it at the first mouthful. The kitchen was warm. The window over the sink was black, and beyond it the sodium of the street.

The drawer didn't lock. I didn't lock it. Thursday was in three days, and I had just eaten for the first time in a week.

The fat on my fingers went pale and stiff as it cooled, in the creases of the knuckles first. My hands were on the table either side of the plate, and they were steady. I looked at them for a long time.
