# Chapter 11

At twenty to eight on a Thursday morning I was eating a char siu bun in my car, across a service road from a building that did not want to be looked at, and I was looking at it.

It was single-storey, brick to the height of a man's hip and ribbed steel above that, with no sign over the door and a leasing agent's board wired to the chain-link fence, which is how a building tells you it belongs to nobody. I had the window down two inches. Smell first. The condensers on the roof were breathing out across the road, a dry, steady exhalation of hot dust and the faint ozone that electronics give off when they have been working all night, the smell of the inside of a hairdryer. Under that, diesel, from the generator in its fenced cage at the side of the yard. Under that, wet cardboard, from the bins of the unit next door, which had been rained on some time in September and had never got over it. The condensers roared. They had roared at eleven at night ten days before, and they had roared on the morning of the twenty-fourth, and they would be roaring when I was dead, because whatever was in that room was never allowed to sleep, and was kept from spoiling the way you keep soup from boiling over, by blowing on it, for ever.

The bun was from the bakery on the arterial corner. I want to be exact about it, because I always am about what I eat on a job. The dough had been steamed so soft that my thumb left a print in it that stayed, faintly sweet, with the yeasty breath of something that had been proving since four; and inside, the pork was cut small and lacquered in its own glaze, honey and soy and five-spice gone dark and sticky as varnish, so that every bite pulled a thread of it across my lip. I had told the office I had a morning appointment and would be in by ten. It was true. This was the appointment.

I had been doing to him what he did to me. Ten days earlier the feed had shown him take his keys off the table at a quarter to eleven, and I had been waiting in the hotel garage when his car came down the ramp, and I had followed it to this street, and watched him badge in at the side door and stay forty minutes. I came back on the twenty-fourth, by daylight, and watched from here. After that it was paperwork, which is my medium. The leasing board named only the managing agent. But a man can hide a great many things from a great many people, and nobody on earth can hide a diesel generator from a permit office. The city's register of permits named the occupier: Lantern Data Services LLC.

I wiped my fingers on the napkin, one at a time, before I touched the keys. The clean laptop was tethered to a prepaid hotspot; my own phone was at home in its foil sleeve, where it had spent more nights that month than I had. Lantern Data Services was wholly owned by Ostler Holdings LLC. Ostler Holdings was owned by Brightwater Row Partners LLC. Brightwater Row was owned by Fenwick Lane Holdings LLC. Three holding companies deep. Three layers, like the meninges: the dura, which is tough and which everybody respects, the arachnoid, which is cobweb and which nobody notices, and the pia, which sticks to the brain so closely that you cannot lift it off without taking a little of the brain with it. None of them is difficult if you know where the seams are. I gave the structure a good mark. It was the work of a man who had read the anatomy and thought the body was the only one who had.

The badge was already in my tote. At twenty past seven I had stood in the bakery queue behind a man in a hi-vis vest with a lanyard, as I had stood behind him on the twenty-fourth, which was the morning I learned he came in for coffee before his first call, and while he paid, the reader in my tote took his card's number the way a swab takes a cheek. He bought two coffees. He bought the second one for someone. I noted it and let it go.

At five to eight the operator arrived, mid-thirties, backpack, no breakfast, and badged in at the side door, as he had on the twenty-fourth. No guard. No second car. A camera over the door and, presumably, something inside, and not one living person watching either. Brandt had built a room that watched itself, and I admired it the way you admire a well-made bone saw: there is not much to say about it except that it does exactly one thing.

At five past eight the climate contractor's white van pulled in at the kerb. The laminated service card zip-tied to the generator cage, which I had read through the binoculars on the twenty-fourth, was initialled on every Thursday, all the way down. My man with the two coffees got out, went to the side door, touched his badge to the reader, and in the glass vestibule beyond it keyed a code into a panel on the wall. The binoculars are the ones I bought for herons. They do not resolve digits through two panes of glass and a road. They resolve a hand. His went straight down the middle of the keypad, top to bottom, four presses and done: the midline. People choose incisions the way I would, if you let them. The line of least resistance. The one that heals.

I went back to the register for the top of the chain. Fenwick Lane Holdings had filed its annual report eleven months before. One officer. A signature at the foot of the form, and I saw the signature before I read the name, because I look at the hand before I look at anything a hand has written. It had no hesitation in it. It was a signature that had never once in its life wondered whether it was allowed. I knew it. I had watched that hand for forty minutes across a desk three years ago, and I had a copy of it in my drawer.

Officer of record: Diane Pell.

The Gatekeeper had kept the Architect's door. She was the pia. She was the one that sticks.

I laughed out loud, alone, in a parked car, with my mouth full of pork bun. It is very hard to laugh with a mouthful of pork bun, and I recommend it. It came up from the diaphragm in hard involuntary contractions, the way a hiccup does, and sprayed crumbs on the dashboard, and my eyes ran, and I went on laughing until I had to lift the laptop off my knees and put it on the passenger seat so as not to drop it.

Eighteen days dead, Diane Pell was the officer of record of four companies, which is more than most people manage alive. The register had not been told. I knew which of those two facts was on file where.

* * *

I left the office at five, and was back across the service road by ten to six, and at three minutes past six the operator came out with his backpack and the building went on roaring to nobody. Then I moved the car two streets away and waited for it to be dark enough.

At five past nine I tethered the tablet and opened the feed. The War Room, thirty-one floors up across the city: Brandt at the glass corner table with his jacket on and his phone out, the posture of a man who is not going anywhere yet. I watched him for longer than I needed to. Once I was through that door I would not be able to see him, and that is the only part of a job like this that I dislike, leaving the man I am working on in the car.

I had budgeted forty minutes. I laid out the kit on the passenger seat the way I lay out a tray. Workwear from a trade-supply shop, matching the contractor's: a hi-vis vest and a cap, with no logo I would have had to explain. Nitrile gloves. The cloned card. A foil survival blanket, folded flat. The head torch with its oxblood filter. A tension wrench and a pick. A blade and a roll of black vinyl. Glassine envelopes. The tap, in a padded pouch. The tablet stayed in the car. My phone was still at home in its sleeve.

At twenty to ten I walked in with my head down and the brim of the cap to the door camera, which was the only camera that could have seen my face. To the camera I was a cap with a vest under it, which is all a contractor ever is to anybody. The cloned card turned the reader green on the first try. In the vestibule I keyed the midline into the panel, two, five, eight, zero, and the panel gave a small polite tone and accepted me. Breaking and entering is mostly entering. The breaking is for amateurs.

The hall hit me in the face. Hot, close, dry air, pushed out of the backs of the racks: warm metal, a burnt-dust note like a radiator switched on for the first time in autumn, and the sweetish plastic of new cable. Then the sound, which was not a sound but a condition, fans in their hundreds, a wall of white noise so dense you could have leaned a ladder on it. It was a room you could scream in all night and be filed as a cooling fault. I noted that professionally and moved on. In the hot aisle it was near thirty degrees and inside a minute I was sweating under the vest, at the collar and down the groove of my spine, and I did not mind. It was the warmest room I had ever been in where I was not allowed to be, and I liked it.

The ceiling lights were the kind that wake when something moves. In the oxblood of the torch I could see the sensor lenses up among the cable trays, small and pale and domed, like the eyes of something blind that had been very carefully placed, and I read them as a floor plan: every lens a cone pointed down, every cone a place I would not stand. I walked the gaps between them, left, straight, left, along the backs of the racks. The rows of status lights went by on either side, green and amber, blinking at nothing. The ceiling stayed dark.

I was enjoying myself. I want that on the record in so many words, without any help from adrenaline, which I have blamed for a great deal and which was not, that night, involved. The last time I had enjoyed a room this much there was a liver in my hands.

At the end of the hall a glass-fronted office looked back over the racks: the room the operator ran. Above its doorway, separate from everything the panel in the vestibule had covered, a second sensor was mounted on the ceiling, a white box with its own small light. A second lock on a second door. The room inside the room. I gave Brandt very high marks for that, and stepped into the doorway, and the little light flickered amber, once.

I stopped mid-stride, with my weight on the ball of my right foot and the heel not yet down. I held it. Gastrocnemius and soleus took the ankle in plantar flexion and kept it there, which they are built to do for about as long as it takes to climb a stair and not for a second more, and the calf began to burn, and I counted, the way I count a pulse. My own was up, and I filed it as a finding. The flicker stopped. Nothing sounded.

A sensor like that does not see you. It sees change: something warm moving quickly across something cooler. So I gave it no change. I let the heel down a millimetre at a time and, very slowly, shook out the foil blanket, the phone sleeve's big sister, and its crackle was the loudest thing I did all night, and the fans ate it. I held it up in front of me at arm's length, the way we hold the sheet at a viewing, and I crossed the office at the speed of the slowest thing I know, which is a body cooling. I have timed a great many people losing a degree an hour. I could go slower than that. It took me four minutes to cross three metres. The little light stayed dark.

Sight last. One desk, one chair. Two monitors, dark, giving back the racks' green and amber pinpricks and nothing else. A keyboard. A mug with a ring of cold coffee drying in the bottom, for which I docked the operator. And against the wall a gunmetal four-drawer steel filing cabinet, with a wafer lock of the kind they put on cabinets so that nobody feels foolish for not locking them. The wrench and the pick had it open in under a minute.

I opened every drawer, because I am thorough. The top three held the shell company's life: incorporation papers; annual reports, Diane's signature at the foot of each one, the same hand on every page; equipment warranties and service contracts. The bottom drawer held the building's own paper, the lease, the insurer's schedule, the fire certificate, and, hung between the lease and the fire certificate, one manila folder with a name typed on its tab.

Mine. A printed file, three years old: the campaign's dossier on Iris Vale. Clipped inside the front cover, a colour photograph taken with a long lens. My face, outside my own building, younger.

* * *

I closed the folder without reading further. The job came first. I put the file on the desk, squared to the edge, and I was a little proud of that, I admit, in the way you are proud of not eating the second biscuit until the work is done.

Back out under the foil, at a degree an hour, and along the gaps between the cones to the rack where the building's outside line came in. The tap was the size of a pack of cards. It went inline on the uplink behind the cable management, where nobody reaches unless something has gone wrong, and its lead went into a USB port on the rack's power strip, and its light came on and went off again, as the manual said it would. It has no radio. It keeps everything that passes through it on a card, which must be collected by hand, and sends out only a thin encrypted summary and a heartbeat, over the building's own uplink, inside the building's own traffic, like a pulse taken through somebody else's wrist. I had bought it, and read its manual the way I read a toxicology report, and fitted it three times on my kitchen table.

He would know someone had been here, because I was about to tell him. He would sweep the place for a transmitter and find nothing that transmits. And he could not call the police about a break-in at a building owned, three companies up, by a woman eighteen days dead, whose autopsy I had signed as reviewer, in which he kept whatever he kept. He could not tell Holt without explaining the building. The three walls were still standing, and I was standing behind them.

Back through the doorway under the foil. At the desk I cut a single rectangle off the black vinyl, the length of a pair of eyes, with one clean snick of the blade, and laid it on the desk in front of the keyboard, squared to the edge. No page. No marker. A redaction bar with nothing under it. I meant it to say only that I had been. It is what a student leaves, when she has read the teacher's file.

Then I graded the file, because I cannot not. My address, the same flat. My salary band. My height, two centimetres out, and everything else right, which is a better mark than most of my residents got. A pencilled column of my movements over two weeks, home, work, the butcher, home, by a hand that knew how to wait. A printout of the blind item that had ended my credibility, with edits in the margin in Brandt's handwriting, which I knew from his dossier the way you know a colleague's suturing. A typed page headed with a summary of my complaint, which I turned over without reading. I know how it ends. I was there for the ending. And a routing slip, in Diane's hand: *Reviewed. Contained. D.P.* I read it twice and gave it a pass. It was accurate. I had been filed between the lease and the fire certificate. I had been assessed as a fire risk. I had been assessed correctly.

The photograph last, because I look after I know. A six-by-four print, long lens, a little soft. A woman coming out of my building's front door with a paper bag of groceries in the crook of one arm, celery leaves over the top of it, her hair longer than I have worn it since, in a coat I gave away. On the back, in pencil, a date in October three years ago, and a reference number. The paperwork had a number for my face.

She looked more like Tessa Quinn than I look now. He hadn't matched Tessa to me. He had matched her to this. I am telling you that I saw it, and I am telling you I am not going to think about it, and I didn't, for the rest of the night.

I unclipped the photograph and slid it into a glassine envelope from my pocket and left the rest. The paperclip I left on the folder. The file went back into the bottom drawer, between the lease and the fire certificate; the drawer shut; the wafer lock re-pinned. Out under the foil, through the gaps between the cones, the midline keyed into the panel to arm it, and the side door closed behind me at twenty-one minutes past ten. Forty minutes and change.

Two streets away, in the car, I didn't start the engine. On the tablet Brandt was still at the War Room table with his jacket on, knowing nothing yet. I watched him for a minute. Then I turned the screen face-down on the passenger seat. The sweat was drying on my back under the vest, a long slow evaporation, and the condensers were a faint roar two streets off and then I stopped hearing them. I stripped the gloves off inside out. I was hungry, in the old, frank way, in the stomach and the back of the jaw, and I report it as I found it: a woman who had eaten a pork bun at twenty to eight that morning and been working since.

I took the photograph out of the envelope bare-handed, by the edges, the way you hold evidence, which it was. The satin of it under my fingertips was the first thing I had touched skin to skin since the bun. The street lamp was sodium, and it made the colours of three years ago go to tea and ash. I looked at her for a long time. I knew the hour was passing, and I let it.

I didn't recognise her either.

In the bottom drawer of a dead woman's cabinet, under a paperclip that was holding nothing, the space where my face had been.
