# Chapter 11

At twenty to eight on a Thursday morning I was eating a char siu bun in my car, across a service road from a building that did not want to be looked at, and I was looking at it.

It was single-storey, brick to the height of a man's hip and ribbed steel above that, with no sign over the door and a leasing agent's board wired to the chain-link fence, which is how a building tells you it belongs to nobody. I had the window down two inches. Smell first. The condensers on the roof were breathing out across the road, a dry, steady exhalation of hot dust and the faint ozone that electronics give off when they have been working all night, the smell of the inside of a hairdryer. Under that, diesel, from the generator cage. Under that, wet cardboard, from the bins of the unit next door, which had been rained on some time in September and had never got over it. The condensers roared, and they would be roaring when I was dead, because whatever was in that room was never allowed to sleep, and was kept cool the way you cool soup, by blowing on it, for ever.

The bun was from the bakery on the arterial corner. I want to be exact about it, because I always am about what I eat on a job. The dough had been steamed so soft that my thumb left a print in it that stayed, faintly sweet, with the yeasty breath of something that had been proving since four; and inside, the pork was cut small and lacquered in its own glaze, honey and soy and five-spice gone dark and sticky as varnish, so that every bite pulled a thread of it across my lip. I had told the office I had a morning appointment and would be in by ten. It was true. This was the appointment.

I had been doing to him what he did to me. Ten days earlier I had followed his car out of the hotel garage at eleven at night to this street, and watched him badge in at the side door and stay forty minutes. I came back on the twenty-fourth, by daylight, and watched from here. That morning it was paperwork, which is my medium.

I wiped my fingers on the napkin, one at a time, before I touched the keys. The clean laptop was tethered to a prepaid hotspot; my own phone was at home in its foil sleeve, where it would stay until the night's work was done, and where it had spent more nights that month than I had. The leasing board named only the managing agent. But a man can hide a great many things from a great many people, and nobody on earth can hide a diesel generator from a permit office. The city's register of permits named the occupier: Lantern Data Services LLC. The state's register of companies did the rest. Lantern Data Services was wholly owned by Ostler Holdings LLC. Ostler Holdings was owned by Brightwater Row Partners LLC. Brightwater Row was owned by Fenwick Lane Holdings LLC. Three holding companies deep. Three layers, like the meninges: the dura, which is tough and which everybody respects, the arachnoid, which is cobweb and which nobody notices, and the pia, which sticks to the brain so closely that you cannot lift it off without taking a little of the brain with it. None of them is difficult if you know where the seams are. I gave the structure a good mark. It was the work of a man who had read the anatomy and assumed nobody else had.

The badge was already in my tote. At twenty past seven, at the pickup end of the bakery counter, I had stood beside a man in a hi-vis vest with his badge on a reel clip at his hip, level with my tote, as I had on the twenty-fourth, and while he waited the reader in the tote took his card's number the way a swab takes a cheek. He bought two coffees. He bought the second one for someone. I noted it and let it go.

At five past eight the climate contractor's white van pulled in at the kerb, as it does every Thursday, and my man with the two coffees went to the side door, touched his badge to the reader, and in the glass vestibule beyond it keyed a code into a panel on the wall. The binoculars are the ones I bought for herons. They do not resolve digits through two panes of glass and a road. They resolve a hand. His went straight down the middle of the keypad, top to bottom, four presses and done: the midline. People choose incisions the way I would, if you let them. The line of least resistance. The one that heals.

At a quarter past eight the operator arrived, mid-thirties, backpack, no breakfast, and badged in at the side door, as he had on the twenty-fourth. No guard. No second car. A camera over the door and, presumably, something inside, and not one living person watching either. Brandt had built a room that watched itself, and I admired it the way you admire a well-made bone saw: there is not much to say about it except that it does exactly one thing.

I went back to the register for the top of the chain. Fenwick Lane Holdings had filed its annual report eleven months before. One officer. A signature at the foot of the form, and I saw the signature before I read the name, because I look at the hand before I look at anything a hand has written. It had no hesitation in it. It was a signature that had never once in its life wondered whether it was allowed. I knew it. I had sat across a desk from that hand for forty minutes three years ago while it watched mine, and it had countersigned the agreement in my drawer.

Officer of record: Diane Pell.

The Gatekeeper had kept the Architect's door. She was the pia. She was the one that sticks.

I laughed out loud, alone, in a parked car, with my mouth full of pork bun. It is very hard to laugh with a mouthful of pork bun, and I recommend it. It came up from the diaphragm in hard involuntary contractions, the way a hiccup does, and sprayed crumbs on the dashboard, and my eyes ran, and I went on laughing until I had to put the laptop on the passenger seat so as not to drop it.

I checked the other three while I was still wiping my eyes. Eighteen days dead, Diane Pell was the officer of record of four companies, which is more than most people manage alive. The register had not been told. I knew which of those two facts was on file where.

* * *

I left the office at five, and was back across the service road by ten to six, and at three minutes past six the operator came out with his backpack and the building went on roaring to nobody. Then I moved the car two streets away and waited for it to be dark enough.

At five past nine I tethered the tablet and opened the feed. The War Room, thirty-one floors up across the city: Brandt at the glass corner table with his jacket on and his phone out, the posture of a man who is not going anywhere yet. I watched him for longer than I needed to. Once I was through that door I would not be able to see him, and that is the only part of a job like this that I dislike, leaving the man I am working on in the car.

I had budgeted forty minutes. I laid out the kit on the passenger seat the way I lay out a tray. Workwear from a trade-supply shop, matching the contractor's: a hi-vis vest and a cap, with no logo I would have had to explain. Nitrile gloves. The cloned card. A foil survival blanket, folded flat. The head torch with its oxblood filter. A tension wrench and a pick. A blade and a roll of black vinyl. Glassine envelopes. The tap, in a padded pouch. The tablet stayed in the car.

At twenty to ten I walked in with my head down and the brim of the cap to the door camera, which was the only camera I knew of. To the camera I was a cap with a vest under it, which is all a contractor ever is to anybody. The cloned card turned the reader green on the first try. In the vestibule I keyed the midline into the panel, two, five, eight, zero, and the panel gave a small polite tone and accepted me. Breaking and entering is mostly entering. In my trade the breaking is done by the saw, and nobody has ever thanked the saw.

Heat came out of the hall to meet me, the way it comes out of an oven you have opened to baste something: close, dry air pushed out of the backs of the racks, warm metal, a burnt-dust note like a radiator switched on for the first time in autumn, and the sweetish plastic of new cable. Then the sound, which was not a sound but a condition, fans in their hundreds, a wall of white noise so dense you could have leaned a ladder on it. It was a room you could scream in all night and be filed as a cooling fault. I noted that and moved on. In the hot aisle it was near thirty degrees and inside a minute I was sweating under the vest, at the collar and down the groove of my spine. It was the warmest room I had ever been in where I was not allowed to be, and I liked it.

The ceiling lights were the kind that wake when something moves. In the oxblood of the torch I could see the sensor lenses up among the cable trays, small and pale and domed, like the eyes of something blind that had been very carefully placed, and I read them as a floor plan: every lens a cone pointed down, every cone a place I would not stand. I read the trays for cameras too, the smoked domes that live at the ends of rows, and found none. He had paid for a room that noticed movement and not one that remembered faces. I walked the gaps between the cones, left, straight, left, along the backs of the racks. The ceiling stayed dark.

I was enjoying myself. I want that on the record in so many words, without any help from adrenaline, which I have blamed for a great deal and which was not, that night, involved. The last time I had enjoyed a room this much there was a liver in my hands.

At the end of the hall a glass-fronted office looked back over the racks: the room the operator ran. Above its doorway a second sensor was mounted on the ceiling, a white box with its own small light, its lens cut to hang a curtain down over the threshold and the first three metres of floor. A second lock on a second door. The room inside the room. I gave Brandt very high marks for that. I knew the kind. It counts: one pulse is a maybe, and it wants a second inside a few seconds before it believes anything. There was no way in but through its curtain, so I stepped into the doorway and let it have its maybe, and the little light flickered amber, once.

I stopped mid-stride, with my weight on the ball of my right foot and the heel not yet down. I held it. Soleus, the slow postural one, will hold an ankle in plantar flexion for a long while; gastrocnemius, which is built for sprinting and showing off, will not, and it was gastrocnemius that began to burn, and I counted, the way I count a pulse. My own was up, and I filed it as a finding, and the finding was appetite, not fear. The flicker stopped. Nothing sounded.

A sensor like that does not see a body. It sees change: something warm crossing something cooler. So I gave it no change. I let the heel down a millimetre at a time and, very slowly, shook out the foil blanket, the phone sleeve's big sister, and its crackle was the loudest thing I did all night, and the fans ate it. I held it up in front of me at arm's length, the way we hold the sheet at a viewing, and I crossed the office at the speed of the slowest thing I know, which is a body cooling. I have timed a great many people losing a degree an hour. I could go slower than that. It took me four minutes to cross three metres. The little light stayed dark.

Sight last, and all of it past the edge of the curtain, in the sensor's blind spot. One desk, one chair. Two monitors, dark, giving back the racks' green and amber pinpricks and nothing else. A keyboard. A mug with a ring of cold coffee drying in the bottom, for which I docked the operator. And against the wall a gunmetal four-drawer steel filing cabinet, with a wafer lock of the kind they put on cabinets so that nobody feels foolish for not locking them. The wrench and the pick had it open in under a minute.

I opened every drawer, because I am thorough. The top three held the shell company's life: incorporation papers; annual reports, Diane's signature at the foot of each; equipment warranties and service contracts. The bottom drawer held the building's own paper, the lease, the insurer's schedule, the fire certificate, and, hung between the lease and the fire certificate, one manila folder with a name typed on its tab.

Mine. A printed file, three years old: the campaign's dossier on Iris Vale. Clipped inside the front cover, a colour photograph taken with a long lens. My face, outside my own building, younger.

* * *

I closed the folder before I got anywhere near the complaint. The job came first. I put the file on the desk, squared to the edge, and I was a little proud of that, in the way you are proud of not eating the second biscuit until the work is done.

Back out under the foil, and along the gaps between the cones to the rack where the building's outside line came in. The tap was the size of a pack of cards. The line in from the street was no use to me; everything on it travels sealed. I went past the box where the seal comes off, to the uplink on its inside, behind the cable management, where the traffic runs bare, the way the vessels do once you are through the capsule. Seating it inline meant cutting the link, and I counted while it was cut: seven seconds. Buildings drop seconds every night and nobody writes them down; I let mine go into the noise. Its lead went into a USB port on the rack's power strip, and its light came on and went off again. It keeps what it hears on a card, for collecting by hand, and sends out only a thin summary and a heartbeat inside the building's own traffic, like a pulse taken through somebody else's wrist. I had read its manual the way I read a toxicology report, and fitted it three times on my kitchen table.

He would know someone had been here, because I was about to tell him. He would sweep the place for a transmitter and find nothing with an aerial. And he could not call the police about a break-in at a building he kept whatever he kept in, owned three companies up by a woman eighteen days dead whose autopsy I had signed as reviewer. He could not tell Holt without explaining the building. The three walls were still standing, and I was standing behind them.

Back through the doorway under the foil. At the desk I cut a single rectangle off the black vinyl, the length of a pair of eyes, with one clean snick of the blade, and laid it on the desk in front of the keyboard, squared to the edge. No page. No marker. A redaction bar with nothing under it. I meant it to say only that I had been. It is what a student leaves, when she has read the teacher's file.

Then I graded the file, because I cannot not. My address, the same flat. My salary band. My height, two centimetres out, and everything else right, which is a better mark than most of my residents got. A pencilled column of my movements over two weeks, home, work, the butcher, home. A printout of the blind item that had ended my credibility, with edits in the margin in Brandt's handwriting, which I knew from his dossier the way you know a colleague's suturing. A typed page headed with a summary of my complaint, which I turned over without reading. I know how it ends. I was there for the ending. And a routing slip, in Diane's hand: *Reviewed. Contained. D.P.* I gave it a pass. It was accurate. I had been filed between the lease and the fire certificate. I had been assessed as a fire risk. I had been assessed correctly.

The photograph last, because I look after I know. A six-by-four print, long lens, a little soft. A woman coming out of my building's front door with a paper bag of groceries in the crook of one arm, celery leaves over the top of it, her hair longer than I have worn it since, in a coat I gave away. On the back, in pencil, a date in October three years ago, and a reference number. The paperwork had a number for my face.

She looked more like Tessa Quinn than I look now. He hadn't matched Tessa to me. He had matched her to this. I am telling you that I saw it, and I am telling you I am not going to think about it.

I unclipped the photograph and slid it into a glassine envelope from my pocket, and left the rest.

The paperclip stayed on the folder, and the folder went back between the lease and the fire certificate, and the plunger of the lock went home. Out under the foil and along the gaps, the midline keyed in to arm the panel, and the side door closed behind me at twenty-one minutes past ten. Forty minutes and change.

Two streets away, in the car, I didn't start the engine. On the tablet Brandt was still at the War Room table with his jacket on, knowing nothing yet. I watched him for a minute. Then I turned the screen face-down on the passenger seat. The sweat was drying on my back under the vest, a long slow evaporation, and the condensers were a faint roar two streets off and then I stopped hearing them. I stripped the gloves off inside out. I was hungry, in the old, frank way, in the stomach and the back of the jaw, and I held it the way I had held the step.

I took the photograph out of the envelope by the edges, the way I hold evidence, and bare-handed, the way I never do. The satin of it under my fingertips was the first thing I had touched without a glove since I pulled the gloves on at twenty to ten. The street lamp was sodium, and it made the colours of three years ago go to tea and ash. I looked at her for a long time. I knew the hour was passing, and I let it.

I didn't recognise her either.

In the bottom drawer of a dead woman's cabinet, under a paperclip that was holding nothing, the space where my face had been.
