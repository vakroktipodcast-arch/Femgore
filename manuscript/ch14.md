# Chapter 14

The card reader on the War Room door made two notes when it refused you, a high one and a lower one. By the time they had come through the bezel unit's microphone and the hotel's network and the tablet's small speaker, they had been thinned into the sound a microwave makes when it has finished with your soup. I heard it twice that Thursday, at 7:51. But you should have the history first. You don't start a chart at the last line.

At twenty past seven the War Room was mostly air handling, a low exhalation the bezel unit had been sending me for six weeks, with the clink of the urn lid over it and a roomful of talk squeezed through the speaker until it sounded like a party heard through a floor. Beside the tablet, against the fruit bowl, a black coffee I had made and was not drinking had stopped steaming and begun to smell of the cup. I wasn't due on the table until ten. My hands were flat on the counter either side of the tablet, the way you rest them at a case conference when it's somebody else's turn to present. I was waiting for Cam Brandt to come in and not sit down.

I had been taking the history for three days, the way you take it from a patient who doesn't know he's giving one. On Monday morning, in front of the room, he told Theo Akers, the boy in digital, that he had leaked the photograph. The boy said he'd deleted it, like he was told, and as he said it a clean pallor went up under his hair as if a blind had been drawn. Brandt went home before eleven. Since then he had stood: at the end of the table, at the window, at the urn, carrying a coffee he didn't drink.

The room had noticed before anyone said anything. A ward knows which bed's cultures have come back before the consultant has read them; the nurses' routes go a step wider round the foot of it, and none of them could tell you they'd done it. The War Room had made the same adjustment. In three days nobody stood within an arm's length of him. I was counting. Diane would have managed it: a side room, a hand at the elbow, one pause of the kind her staff mistook for thoughtfulness, and fixed before lunch. Diane was not available. I knew the reason exactly, and the time. 10:47, by my watch.

On Wednesday evening, at 9:14 by the feed's timestamp, he bent to Holt's ear. Holt stood and put a hand on his shoulder, and the two of them went out of the glass room together and out of the lens. It was the one conversation of the week I wanted, and the camera gave me the doorway, a rectangle of corridor light with nobody in it. Holt came back alone four minutes later and was charming about something on the wall screen. Brandt came back nearly two minutes after him and did not sit.

I have read the file. Holt's dossier, in my drawer, has a section for the men he has stopped knowing, and read in a row they are a clinical course. First the meetings stop including you. Then the doors. Then the systems. At no stage does anybody say the word, any more than a transplanted kidney is told it is being rejected. The host just stops sending it blood, a vessel at a time. Nobody in that building had the authority to dismiss Cam Brandt, and so nobody did. They simply stopped letting him in, which is how a hospital handles a death it would rather not certify.

At 7:42 the huddle started without him. On his way to the head of the table Holt touched the deputy communications director on the shoulder, and the deputy sat down at the end of the table, in Brandt's chair. A hand on a shoulder and a man sitting where he was put. I have seen a bed handed over with more ceremony.

At 7:51 Brandt came into the frame outside the glass, in charcoal, and held his card to the reader. The two notes. The light went the colour of a brake lamp. He looked at the card as if it had said something rude, turned it over, and tried again. The two notes. At the head of the table Holt did not look up. It takes years of practice not to look up through glass. Holt did it with his whole face. A man from IT came along the corridor and let Brandt in with his own card, and smiled at him, and the smile was sorry about something.

Brandt went to the laptop at the end of the table. Somebody was sitting at his place, so he logged in standing, one hand flat on the table. The units can't read a screen; they were bought to watch people, and they are good with faces and useless with fonts. So I read his hands, which are the best thing about him. The password went in once, at the speed of a man who has typed it ten thousand times. A pause. Again, a little harder on the keys. A pause. Then a third time, slowly, a key at a time, each finger lifting clear, the way you type when you have begun to suspect it isn't the keyboard. A man types his password a third time the way the bereaved ring a number they know has been disconnected. Not to get through. To hear it not answer.

His hands stopped on the trackpad, the right over the left, fingers a little flexed. He looked up the table at Holt, and Holt was laughing at something the deputy had said, head back, a bright crackle on the cheap microphone. I watched the rejection run its course in Cam Brandt, a procedure I had only ever read in a dossier, performed now by Holt himself, by the book, without his ever being seen to hold the book. I gave it full marks. Something moved under my sternum, a small warm displacement, and I am not going to call it sympathy, because it wasn't. I filed it as the feeling of watching a colleague receive the diagnosis you would have given him yourself: correct, and in good time, and it should have been you who said it. I didn't let the sentence go any further. My left hand had gone under my shirt to the dressing on my flank, the nylon ends a stiff line under the tape, six days old and itching, and it stayed there.

* * *

At ten past one I was in my car in the ME's car park with the engine off. The canteen is on the far side of the car park, and I had come out the other way. Crossing the tarmac I checked my coat pocket, the way you check for a wallet. There was nothing in it. I'd known there wouldn't be.

The sun had been on the car all morning, and the seat was hot through my scrubs at the backs of my thighs, like a bath someone else has run. Over the warm vinyl was the smell I can't smell in the suite, formalin and the unscented soap that doesn't shift it and, under both, the sweetish note fifteen years of corridors have set into my skin like a watermark; in a hot car it was like being shut in with a colleague.

I took the tablet out of my bag and opened the tap's summary, as I had twice a day since I fitted it. It sends me, over Brandt's own uplink, a thin digest of what goes past it: headers, and the bodies of mail up to a couple of kilobytes, nothing attached. It's like being sent a patient's observations instead of the patient.

Holt had locked Cam Brandt out of the house that morning. He had not locked him out of the plumbing, because officially there was no plumbing. The farm was Brandt's private copy of the campaign, three companies deep, with a dead woman's name on the paperwork, and its copy of the mail ran under her compliance authority, not his login. Holt couldn't close a door he had never been told existed. So counsel's correspondence came to me the way everything in that building eventually comes to a pathologist: through the drains.

At 11:12 counsel had sent an email to Holt's office. Not to Brandt. Brandt had been copied on every email of counsel's I'd read for a week. This one went past him the way the huddle had. Its first line was the instruction: *Hold until after the third.* Under it was counsel's reply to a letter, sent at 11:05, and under that the letter itself, received on Wednesday and quoted in full, because a lawyer will not paraphrase anything he may later be quoted on. The digest cut off a line into the firm's disclaimer, with the letter just inside the limit: its words, and no letterhead. I read a body from the outside before I read it from the inside, and I'd been handed this one already opened.

The letter requested *all agreements entered into under matter LC-0311, together with associated signatory records, in connection with the death of Diane Pell.* It was signed *Special Agent E. Reyes.*

Counsel's reply said the campaign was *reviewing the scope of the request and attendant questions of privilege* and *will respond in due course*. I gave him an A. He had it by heart. I have seen *in due course* on an insurer's enquiry into a cause of death, and both parties understood it to mean after the body was cold. Stalling is the one bodily function a law firm never loses, even on the table.

I checked the matter number. I know LC-0311 the way I know the timestamp on a voicemail I deleted. It sits under a thin rule in the footer of every page of my agreement. I had cut copies of those pages to the size of a fingernail and packed them into Diane's mouth so that the footers would be there for whoever opened it. Her teeth closed at the last on the tip of my glove, and I can still find that pressure in the fingertip when I want it. I had pictured the footers followed as far as counsel, where paper goes to wait, embarrassing a man in a good suit on the way. I had not pictured anyone with a name pushing past him. Someone had. All agreements under the matter: staff, vendors, donors, whoever rents them their buses, and mine in among the bus contracts, eight pages and a signature. Somebody with an initial was asking for the lot.

I searched the name the way I pull a chart: name, rank, office. There was nothing I could use. The Bureau doesn't publish its people, which I respect, and an initial and a surname turned up a hundred strangers. I have a dossier on everyone who matters, in a locked drawer, with string and a button. I knew the boy in digital by his hairline. Reyes had a signature block and an initial, and an initial is not a person. It's a case number on a toe tag, waiting for the family.

Out of habit I opened the War Room. At 1:40 counsel went into the glass room and spoke to Holt with his head inclined, the way a registrar talks to a consultant at a bedside when the patient can hear, and Holt nodded twice. Brandt was at the far end of the table, ten feet away, standing with a coffee, and nobody called him over. He may not have known. For the first time that week I knew something about his campaign before he did, and I waited for it to feel the way it should have, and it felt like reading a colleague's results over his shoulder, and my mouth was dry.

My hand had gone to the dressing through my scrub top, and I was thinking, so it stayed there, the tails a ridge under the tape, the skin round them itching the way healing skin does. The car was warm. I noticed the warmth, and I noticed that it didn't change.

I went back to the letter and read it a second time, all of it, from the signature block up to the first line, the way I read a chart, latest entry first. *Special Agent E. Reyes. In connection with the death of Diane Pell. Associated signatory records. All agreements entered into under matter LC-0311.* There was no page for Reyes. There was no subject line, no function, no Evaluation typed two years ago at my desk. In the warehouse the Friday before, I had done something that was not on the syllabus, but that had been a thing I did. This was a thing I was in. For the first time, something was happening that I had not written.

* * *

On Friday at ten past five in the morning I had the desk lamp on. It ticks as it warms, like a fingernail on a specimen jar. I unlocked the drawer and took out Lesson Two, my master: *Excellent.* in red beside the subject line, *Tessa Quinn* in black beneath it, ruled through once. I didn't read the Evaluation. I know what it says. I did arithmetic.

The plan had the Architect in the third week of October, close enough to the election that the campaign couldn't absorb him. You can bury a Chief of Staff in September with a statement at 5:58. Nobody can bury the man who writes the statements a fortnight from the vote. It was a good plan. I had liked it for two years. I gave it up in the time it takes a kettle to boil.

My reasons, in order. Counsel could stall, and stall well, but paper wins in the end; I work for it. A man Holt has cut loose has nothing left in the building and a reason to walk into a federal office and trade what he knows for himself, and what Brandt knew was my name. Brandt walled off was Brandt with a reason to talk to E. Reyes. Third, which was the second again: inside the week. Nobody revises a syllabus in the middle of term unless the examiner is coming early. I gave the logic full marks. I squared the page to its own edge and put it back and locked the drawer, and while I was squaring it I thought, for no reason I put into words, of a letter with an initial at the foot of it reaching him before I did.

At half past nine, at work, I booked Wednesday the fourteenth off through the leave system. One line. A day booked five days ahead is wallpaper in a personnel file; a sick call on the morning itself is the line an investigator underlines. *Reason for absence* is a box you are allowed to leave blank, and I left it blank. On my side of the building nobody gets to. That was administration.

I ate less that week. I'm telling you because I tell you the facts about lunch, and nothing is also a menu. Thursday: a black coffee, made at seven and left to go cold beside the tablet. No lunch. In the evening, one egg, boiled, eaten out of the cup with a teaspoon at the counter. The yolk had gone past soft to chalk and left sulphur at the back of my tongue. Half a slice of toast; the other half I left on the board. Friday so far: water from the kitchen tap at five, which had stood in the pipes all night and tasted of them, flat and mineral.

The body's version, in its own notation. Appetite reduced, onset Thursday. No nausea. No pain. Afebrile. Weight not taken. Patient reports her mood as good. Noted. Unexplained. Not discarded. The body knew something the plan didn't, and I didn't ask it what.

Mara and I washed up side by side, a basin apart, before the case. She told me about her lunch, which was an egg-and-cress sandwich she'd had in the fridge since eight and plainly meant to eat the minute we were done, and she offered me half across the sink. I knew what it would be by one o'clock: white bread gone soft and sweating in its clingfilm, the egg's sulphur coming up through the mayonnaise, the cress wet and peppery.

"No, thank you, Mara."

She looked at me for about as long as it takes to read a label twice. Then she went back to the soap. I'm telling you what she did. I'm not telling you what it was worth.

The case, at a quarter past eleven, was a man of seventy-one, found in his armchair with the radio on. He had been there about two days. When we unzipped the bag his room came up out of his clothes: dust cooked onto the back of a radio, the sourness of a chair that has held the shape of one man for longer than most marriages, a close, sweet, unaired smell like the inside of a biscuit tin. He died with the radio on. Somebody had been playing to him for two days, which is more than most of my patients get.

The extraction fans in the suite run all day at the pitch of an aeroplane cabin at altitude, so constantly that they stop being a noise and become the air, and anything quieter than a dropped instrument goes into it and doesn't come out unless you are standing close. That's why I didn't notice it at first.

I took the heart out. It came into my gloved hand heavy and firm and cold, four degrees out of the cooler, slick, the left ventricle thick under my fingers like the wall of a cistern. I laid it on the scale and the scale thought about it and said four hundred and eighty grams. That was his whole history in one figure: blood pressure, years of it, unremarked, and then an armchair. I felt it before I heard it, a vibration in my chest behind the sternum, like a phone going in a coat pocket.

I was humming.

It was the Mingus. I'd been hearing it all week through the unit on the top shelf of his record wall, side one, every night since the page, from the chair by the piano. I had first heard it in September through laptop speakers, and turned it off. I had never hummed at work. Not once in fifteen years. I didn't know how long I had been doing it. The fans had been keeping it for me.

Mara had leaned in over the scale to read the weight. Her pen stopped on the form, and she looked up.

"What is that?"

"Mingus," I said. "*The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady.*"

At twenty to ten that night my phone lit up on the kitchen counter. Mara. No message, only a link, bare, the album's small square under it. I didn't reply. I played it. The phone lay face-up beside the bread board, where Thursday's other half-slice had gone stiff and curled at the edges like a page left in a window, and the music came out of the little speaker thin and far away, the whole band squeezed down into something you could have held in one hand, and I stood at the counter and listened. When it came to the place where a needle would have lifted at the end of side one, nothing lifted. A phone has no sides. I reached out and stopped it there myself.
