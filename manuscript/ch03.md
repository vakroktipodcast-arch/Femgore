# Chapter 3

I unlocked the drawer before I took my shoes off. It was a quarter past six on the Friday, and I had come in from work with a paper parcel from the butcher's and put it in the fridge, and then the key, and then the shoes. The Pell dossier went on the desk, and beside it, out of its sleeve, my working master of Lesson One, two years old and corrected in red ever since, its margins grown a second, smaller, crosser text. I had the marker uncapped before I sat down, and the smell came off the felt the way it always does, sweet and sharp, a pear-drop solvent that goes straight past the nose and taps on the backs of the eyes. It was the one from the pen tray. Not the one in the back compartment; that one had a job. I wanted an hour. One hour of Diane Pell's week that belonged to no one: not a driver, not a donor, not the Senator, not her phone. I was going to find it before I went to bed.

The hotel went first. All night, in the lift and the car and the shower, the Calloway had looked like a gift: a building she worked in, a staff corridor nobody checked, doors held open with cases of water. I had come out of it with a blister, and a houseman's plaster over the blister, still on my heel that evening and gone grubby at the edges. At the desk, under the lamp, the hotel was a gift the way a cat's gift on the doormat is a gift. I wrote *the Calloway* in the margin of the grid and ruled one line through it, against the edge of a card so it would be straight, and wrote underneath: *Too many doors. Too much carpet. Everyone she has is in the room with her.*

The grid was a week: a hundred and sixty-eight hours in seven columns, every hour sourced, two years of my own work. Weekdays: the driver at ten to seven, HQ, the War Room, donor dinners, home after eleven. Home was an alarmed two-bedroom townhouse with a neighbour whose dog needed walking at all hours, which is an alarm system on a lead. Her phone was never off. She answered every call. I had seen that one for myself the night before: the phone going in the middle of a donor's sentence and Diane answering on the first ring, and the donor beaming as if he had been promoted. I wrote it across the weekday block and underlined it twice. *She answers. She always answers.* Then I underlined it a third time.

I think better with my hands busy, and my hands think better holding something that was recently alive. Six lamb's kidneys, out of their paper onto the board. The butcher had asked whether I wanted them trimmed, and when I said I'd manage he looked at my hands and said he didn't doubt it, and I liked him more for that than I have liked most people this year.

Raw kidney smells of what it does for a living. Under the lamb, which is a clean, milky, grassy sweetness, there is a thin note of ammonia, a public-lavatory note if I'm honest, and I am. I named it before I liked it. Then I liked it. The capsule peels like the skin on a grape; I pinched it at the convex border and drew it back towards the hilum and it came away whole, translucent, a small glove turned inside out. Then the knife, from the convex border through to the hilum, so that each kidney fell open into two halves like a pair of ears, and there it all was in section: the cortex dark and granular at the rim, the medulla paler and striated beneath it, and at the hilum the renal crest and the pelvis, the white funnel at the core. A sheep's kidney is unipyramidal. One pyramid, one crest where ours have papillae, no calyces at all, a single drain where ours have a dozen. I snipped the white core out of each half with the kitchen scissors. The kidney is the body's compliance department. It sees everything that passes, keeps what is useful, and sends the rest down a tube to be forgotten about. I had six of them on a board, and I had never enjoyed a department more.

I washed my hands with the unscented soap and went back to the desk with them still damp. The ammonia stayed on my fingertips. It came up off the page every time I touched the grid. Saturday was HQ from ten. Sunday afternoon, HQ. Sunday at four, her mother: the dossier had it from a trade-magazine profile, the quotation copied out in my own small capitals: *I call my mother every Sunday at four, no matter what.* Sunday evening, the weekly call sheet. And Sunday morning, in the narrow column between seven and noon, a single word in my hand: *home?* I had written it months ago, with the question mark, and never gone back. I circled the question mark in red.

The butter went into the pan. I let it foam and fall and turn the colour of strong tea and smell of hazelnuts, the second before it turns, and laid the kidneys in cut side down. They seized and arched and spat. Ninety seconds. Then over, and in with a spoon of English mustard, a pinch of cayenne, Worcestershire, a splash of water to bring the pan back into a sauce, and two slices of toast standing by. The cayenne got into the air and went for the back of my throat. The heat came off the pan onto my face and got no further. My forearms stayed cold, the cold I had brought home from the lift.

I ate from the pan at the counter, standing, with the papers on the far side of the flat out of the splash line. When I am planning I eat on my feet. I'll tell you the facts about dinner. All six, and both slices, and I wiped the pan with the last corner of the second one. The kidneys were pink at the centre, with that dense, iron-rich give they have, half liver and half muscle, and the mustard was hot enough to make my eyes run once, and the toast had gone soft under the sauce in the middle and stayed crisp at the edges. It was twenty to eight. I was still hungry when the pan was clean.

I graded the dossier, because it was mine. It was thorough. It was sourced. It was handsomely kept, and it failed. B-minus, for diligence, which is the grade you give the student who has done everything except the thing.

Then I did the sum again, a fingertip on each hour. Every hour of Diane Pell's week was either guarded or attended. A driver, staff, donors, an alarm, a phone she always answered, her mother at four. The campaign had made her the one person on it who was never alone, and not one of those guards was there for her sake; the campaign had never once imagined that anybody might come for the woman who kept its files. Her week was so well defended it had no room left in it for a life, which is the kind of thing you only ever say about other people's diaries. I looked at my left hand on the page, the one from the lift. It was still. I'm telling you so, and moving on.

There was a gap in the dossier, and when I saw it I saw what it was. A hundred and sixty-eight hours and not one of them unsecured is not a description of a woman. It is a description of a file with a hole in it. The hour existed, and I didn't have it. I wrote in the margin, in red: *Where does she go to not be reached?*

* * *

By ten I had moved to the writing desk proper, with the clean laptop open and nothing else on in the flat, so that the screen was the only light, blue-white over the edge of the dossier, turning the red ink black. The pan was soaking in the sink. Through the wall the fridge started and stopped. The question sat in the margin, and I did what I always do, which is go back to the body. If you want to know where a woman goes, look at what the going has done to her.

At the fundraiser she had read one text, and as she turned her wrist to read it the dress watch had slid down, and under it, on the left wrist, there had been a band of paler skin, wider than the dress watch and squarer, the shape of a sports strap. On Tuesday I had read the same kind of band on a woman from a bus shelter, a centimetre wide, where a hospital bracelet had been. Diane's had been put there by something she chose. She wore something that counted her steps, on days when nobody was looking at her, and things that count steps keep the count somewhere.

Behind my signed agreement in the drawer there was a printout, three years old: Diane's cover email, and in its header one stray CC to an address that wasn't the campaign's. Her own. I had clipped it there the day the email came, on purpose, because a private address on a public woman is a key, and I keep the keys I don't yet need.

I made an account on Trailmark from the clean laptop with no real name on it. I had read the privacy policy first, all of it, which makes me one of perhaps four people living; the app does not tell anyone who has looked at them. I put her address into the field that offers to find people you know.

She came up at twenty past ten. *D. Pell.* The profile had never been set to private, because the default is public, and Diane had never read the settings. The Compliance Director of a campaign under ten weeks from victory had accepted the terms and conditions without reading them, and I found this so moving that I had to look away from the screen. The avatar was the reservoir at dawn with no face in it, which was the most honest self-portrait I have seen anyone on a campaign produce. One hundred and forty-one recorded walks. All of them on Sundays.

I read them the way I read a run of bloods, the whole column before any single value. Every one was the same route: a loop of 6.4 kilometres, clockwise, from the south car park. Start times ran from 7:31 to 7:48. The early ones were the rule. The handful after twenty to were short-cut Sundays, or walks begun from the far side of the car park. The first walk was logged on a Sunday, eleven days after the date on my agreement. I am giving you that because it is the date. My mouth tasted of the kidneys, and of nothing else.

Then the pause. At the same coordinates every week, 3.1 kilometres in, the track stopped moving and stayed stopped for eleven minutes, give or take one: there by five past eight or ten past, gone again by twenty past. I knew it was a bench before the satellite view showed me one. Eleven minutes of nothing, every week, logged to the second. The only person I had ever met who kept better records of her own absence was me. Campaign security, which paid for her driver and her alarm and had her private address on her own personnel file: fail.

There were four Sundays when she walked about a third slower: the Sunday after Thanksgiving, two at Christmas, one in late May. Weather, I thought, or a knee. A woman of fifty-two is allowed a knee. I logged them.

Then I laid her track over the satellite map. After the bench the loop came round below the dam, and there, about 1.1 kilometres on, a trail marker post stood where a spur of path went off between sumac. Forty metres up the spur, behind the trees, was a square of dark roof: an old pump station, brick, abandoned, with the sumac grown up to its walls. Up from the bench at twenty past, and on round the water to the post. I did the arithmetic twice. She would pass the foot of the spur at 8:33, give or take nine minutes.

I printed the satellite view by cable, from the clean laptop, and the printer pushed it out warm, and I laid it on the desk and drew an X on the spur in red. The marker dragged a little on the cheap paper. Beside the X I wrote *8:33*.

Then I waited for the feeling, the way you wait for a result from the lab. I put two fingers of my right hand on my left wrist, the grip from the lift, and counted. Sixty-four. My hands were still. I licked my lips before I wrote, and they still tasted of cayenne.

In the margin of the dossier I wrote: *Affect: nothing identifiable.* Directly underneath it, in the same hand and the same red, I wrote: *Lamb's kidneys ×6, devilled. Toast ×2. Standing.* I didn't ask myself why the second line belonged on that page.

* * *

After midnight the pan was washed, the counter wiped, the dossier squared on the desk, and I took the rug out from under the writing desk where it lives. It is a flatweave, oxblood and bone, and I give its length in centimetres, which I never do for furniture, because I was measuring it as something else: about a hundred and seventy, and about eight kilograms. It smelled of wool and the old dust of under-the-desk. I rolled it tight on the kitchen floor and belted it at two points with my own belts, one round the waist and one round the knees, if a rug has knees, and by then it did. I know how this looks. A woman in a T-shirt and socks, at twenty past twelve, squaring up to a carpet. I would grade the exercise C for dignity and A for commitment, and the commitment carries it.

Diane, from the night before, out of her heels: about a hundred and sixty-five centimetres, about seventy kilos. The rug was a lighter woman than Diane by some sixty kilograms, and a great deal more cooperative, and it had never once answered its phone. The rug could teach me the order of my hands. It could not teach me her weight, and I noted that too. Then I gave it a face. A strip of surgical tape across the top end, pressed down with my thumbnail until it had properly decided to stay, and on the tape, in the desk red marker, two dots for the nostrils and a line for the mouth. It was the first time the marker had touched anything standing in for her. It looked faintly surprised, which was fair.

I stood it up against the counter edge, and the face came out about where hers would be. The industrial tape was pewter, the same make as the roll I would take with me on the day, not the roll itself; that one was never going to carry my kitchen on its edges. It came off the roll louder than I'd have liked in a sleeping building, like a sheet being ripped for bandages. I tore it into strips and laid them along the counter edge in a row, and folded one end of each back on itself to make a tab, the way you make a dressing, so that a gloved finger can find the end without looking. Two pairs of gloves, one over the other, as it would be on the day. My phone was propped against the radio in airplane mode, recording, and from the radio, low, came a late call-in show: a man from the suburbs saying it was done, the whole thing, he'd put his house on it. I wondered which room he would hand over first.

She would have a phone at her ear. A woman on a phone has one hand and half her hearing, and you come from the phone side. Hand over the nose and mouth, weight back and down, a strip across the mouth, then the wrists, which a rug can't help you with. I have spent fifteen years at the wrong end of this done badly; the table tells you what a struggle leaves, in the fingernails and the forearms and the backs of the heels, and I was working out how to leave none of it. The strip rode across the back of my left glove, tab towards the fingertips. The right hand did the covering, and came off the mouth only once the left hand had the nose, to find the tab blind and lay the strip across in one pull.

Attempt one. The rug folded at the wrong place, at the waist belt instead of the knees, and the strip stuck to itself, and I ended up on one knee holding a carpet in an embrace. At half speed it played like footage of a stranger. Seven point two seconds, first contact to the tab pressed down. I have sat through a great many teaching videos. None of them showed a consultant pathologist losing a wrestling match to soft furnishings, and the profession is poorer for it. Fail.

Attempt two. I corrected for the fold, took the weight down across my own thigh instead of fighting it, and found the tab first time. Five point one. The weave had caught the inside of my right wrist on the way down, just above the glove cuff, on bare skin, and left a friction abrasion about two centimetres long, superficial, weeping a little, with a few fibres of oxblood wool in it, and I documented it as I would on anyone else. The tiles were cold under my knees through the socks. The retired couple downstairs have complained once in four years, so I lowered the rug the last few inches by hand. If they ever asked, moving furniture at one in the morning was the better of the available explanations.

Closed is not the same as kept closed. On the day the airway would be shut just long enough to get her through the door and down, and then given back, because she would need her voice for what came after. A body without air does not go quiet first. It fights harder first: the diaphragm hauling against nothing, the neck cording, the hands going for whatever is over the face, and only near the edge does it stop arguing, and where the edge is depends on the lungs and the fitness and the fright of whoever is under your hand. Nobody knows it to the second. I would be counting, and I would let go early.

Attempt three. Phone side. Hand. Weight. Tab. Mouth. The rug went down without a sound I hadn't chosen.

On the video, at half speed, it was three point eight seconds. Under four. The airway was closed: my left hand over the dots, the strip across the line. I was sitting back on my heels on the kitchen floor with a carpet in my arms, and I was breathing hard, and I was happy. The kitchen smelled of adhesive and wool and, under them, me. I'll give you the findings. Respiratory rate up. A flush across the chest and the front of the throat, warm to my own hand. Forearms still cold. And the number, which pleased me the way a clean section pleases me, three point eight, a personal best.

I deleted the video, and then deleted it again from the folder where deleted things wait, because I know where people look. I peeled the face off the rug and folded it in on itself, sticky side in, with the used strips, and put the bundle in a zip bag and the bag in the back compartment of the drawer, and turned the key. I unbelted the rug and unrolled it. I carried the tape and the gloves and the marker back to the desk. It was half past one.

I capped the marker and laid it on the printout, parallel to the edge of the page, with its tip a finger's width from the X, so that it pointed. The rug went back under the writing desk, its corners square to the boards, as if nothing had been asked of it.
