# Chapter 9

The deadbolt turned with the weight it always turns with, a quarter of resistance and then the soft, heavy give at the end, like a joint going back into its socket, and I came in sideways with the grocery bag on my hip and the morgue still on my skin, and the flat smelled of itself. It was Wednesday, the third day after Diane, a little after half past six. Chlorine from the sink, where I had rinsed a cloth that morning. The lemons in the fruit bowl, beginning to think about turning. The dry, faintly scorched nothing of a radiator that has not yet been switched on for the year. And under that, at about the height of a standing man's chest, something that was not mine: dry, green, rooty, expensive, like a cellar somebody keeps well. I own unscented soap and nothing else that smells of anything. I have spent fifteen years training my nose, and I am not going to waste it on lavender. I stood in my own hall with the door still open at my back and the street's air going past me into the flat, and by the time I had shut it I knew.

I didn't go to the desk.

I went to the kitchen, and I kept the bag on my hip until I got there, and put it down on the counter. Eggs, a tin of anchovies, butter, a lemon, a loaf. I didn't switch on the overhead. The last of the day was coming through the window over the sink, the colour of weak tea, and it was enough. The knife block first: full, every handle at its own angle, the way I leave them. Then the fridge. I opened it and the seal came away from the frame with that small wet kiss it gives, and I looked. The butter in its paper, the paper folded back at the corner exactly where my thumb had folded it. The half lemon under its cling film, the film tight and unbroken over the cut face. The milk, and I turned the bottle and looked at the ring under the cap, and the ring was whole. The kettle on its base, cold to the back of my hand. Nothing opened. Nothing added. Nothing swapped.

Most women who come home to a burglary check the jewellery. I checked the butter. I'm a pathologist. I know where the jewellery is.

Then the desk. The drawer was locked. I had locked it, and the key had been on my ring, in my coat pocket, all day, in a building where I am surrounded by people who cannot be burgled. He had not needed it, which I took personally. I put the desk lamp on and bent and looked at the brass of the lock face under it, the way I would look at the skin around an entry wound, and there was nothing. No scratches, no bright new crescents where a pick had slipped. Entry, competent. I unlocked it, and it came out on its runners with its long wooden sigh, and everything was there. The three dossiers. Under them, the photographs. Under the photographs, my signed agreement, with Diane's cover email clipped behind it. Under that, the clear sleeve, and in the sleeve the title page and all three pages, the working masters, soft at the corners. I pressed the panel at the back and it came away, and behind it the rest of the black vinyl and the bundle of zip-ties lay as I had left them, and I could not tell whether he had found them or not. It is the one thing in the flat I could not grade. A man who had found that panel would have put it back exactly as it was. So would a man who hadn't.

Here is the tell. The dossier on top I square to a knot in the grain of the drawer's floor, a dark oval like a thumbprint in the walnut, the left edge of the folder on the knot, every time. I have done it every time for two years. Tonight the edge sat a quarter of an inch to the left of it. I measure bodies in centimetres and furniture in inches. Nobody has ever asked me why, and the furniture has never complained.

My hand went out over the folder to square it, and stopped, and stayed there, a little above the manila, the fingers slightly flexed. I noted that. Then I took the hand back.

I said I would tell you which was the tell. The drawer was the evidence. The kitchen was the tell, and it was mine. I had come into a flat a man had been in and I had checked the food before the files. Pathologists know that the cleanest way into a body is through its fridge, and my body had known it before I did, and had gone to the fridge first, and told me in that order exactly what I was afraid of. And the fridge had told me back that he had not come to kill me. Not today.

The dossier on top was his. It had been on top since Monday night, when I read the Lesson Two page for an hour with his file open beside it, and put the file back first because it was the one I was working. The sleeve lies under all three, so a man who wants the pages has to lift the dossiers as a block, and set them down on the desk, and put them back afterwards, and the block had come back a quarter-inch left. A man who opens a drawer with three files in it reads his own first. In his, in the margin beside the photograph of him leaving the Tabard, there is a pencil note in my hand from two years ago. *The only one who'll understand it.* He had read that, in my handwriting, about himself. It is an odd thing, to have a note reach the man it was about, two years late, by burglary.

I worked his visit backwards from the room, which is what I do for a living. He came in daylight, while I was at the table with someone else's lungs in my hands. He read everything. He took nothing, because taking is evidence and he removes evidence for a living. And he put it all back almost exactly, the way the bereaved tidy a room before the police come: lovingly, and a quarter-inch wrong. The quarter-inch was the size of a man whose hands were not quite as steady as he had thought they would be. Entry competent, restoration very nearly excellent, one error. I found the error because I have spent fourteen hundred cases finding the one thing somebody else put back wrong.

I had not eaten since a coffee at ten. Under all of it I was hungry, and I noted it, and left it on the counter with the eggs.

He had read the syllabus. He knew that Lesson One was done, because the whole of the thirty-first floor had seen it by Sunday midnight, and that two lessons were still to be set, and the three dossiers lying over the pages had told him whose names went on them: his, and Holt's. And under the dossiers, in the same drawer, on the campaign's paper, was my signature, in the blue ink they gave me, rounder than it is now.

Mine was a name to him now.

* * *

I cooked standing up, because I was planning. Four eggs, broken into a cold pan with far too much butter and stirred over the lowest flame the hob will hold, the curds coming slowly, the butter browning a little at the edge where the pan was hottest and smelling of hazelnuts. Two anchovies mashed into a spoonful of soft butter with the back of a fork until it was the colour of putty and the smell of it was salt and rot and the sea, which is the smell of every good thing that has ever been preserved. Toast, the anchovy butter going into it while it was too hot to hold. The eggs over that, barely set, trembling. The lemon zested over the lot, so the oil in the zest went up into my face. I ate it off the plate in my hand with the tablet propped against the fruit bowl, where it had stood for the debate, and I was very happy, and I am telling you that because it is a fact about supper.

First I scrubbed back through the day's recording. The bezel unit had the table, wide; the unit under the credenza lip had the urn, from below, close. It took me a quarter of an hour to find the gap. At 12:58 he left the room. At 3:06 he came back, in the same charcoal suit, and went to the urn and drew a coffee and stood holding it, and did not drink it. Two hours and eight minutes. I can do my flat, at a careful walk, in two hours and eight minutes, if I allow for traffic and for reading.

After nine, I watched it live. The room was quieter than it had been on Sunday. Holt had given his statement on Diane in the afternoon, and the staff were drafting a vigil, which is a press release with candles. Brandt sat at the end of the conference table, not working, with his phone face-down by his hand, and I ate, and I watched him decide three times not to do something.

Three phone calls a man could have made, and I watched him not make each of them, which is the most intimate thing I had seen a man do in years.

The first came to him. The security lead put a slip of paper by his elbow and said something I lost under the air system, and I heard *sheriff's office* and *liaison*. Brandt held the slip a long time. Then he folded it in half and put it in his inside pocket and did not dial. You can't call them. To give them a name you would have to tell them how a man gets from a mouthful of shredded paper to a pathologist in three days, and that means telling them about the paper. And the paper is the one thing the campaign cannot be seen to have made, seven weeks out. Correct.

Holt came in at half past ten with his tie loosened, and the cold began in my forearms, on the volar surface, the way it did the night of the debate, and moved into the backs of my hands. I kept eating. I want you to know that was a choice. A junior staffer near the window, a young woman, had been crying on and off all evening, and he went to her first and put his hand on her shoulder and talked to her quietly, and he knew her parents' names. He has a good memory for the first names of people he is about to make into paperwork. He was kinder in that small room than anybody who works for him. Then he stopped by Brandt's chair and asked if there was anything he needed to know.

"No, sir. Statement landed."

Brandt watched him go. You can't tell him either. There is a section in Holt's dossier, in my drawer, for the men he has stopped knowing. I have watched him do it on footage, a man standing at his shoulder one month and gone from the next month's photographs, and never a raised voice. The moment he hears that somebody has been into the old files, the problem is not the somebody. The problem is the man who kept the files. Correct again.

The third was nearly midnight. He picked the phone up and scrolled, a long way, past everyone he liked, and his thumb stopped. I couldn't read the screen at that resolution and I didn't need to. I read the thumb. Then he put the phone down on its face on the table, a small separate tock, and took his hand off it. You don't know what I have. You don't know who I've told. A dead pathologist with a dossier somewhere you can't reach is worse than a live one. Full marks.

I have told no one. I don't own a safe-deposit box. I own a fruit bowl. He couldn't know that, and I found that I wanted to keep it from him the way you keep the end of a book from someone who is enjoying it.

I filled the kettle while I thought, and made toast for the last of the eggs, which had gone cool and firm in the pan the way I like them second time round, and ate that too. It was more than I had meant to eat. It was not a meal after anything. Nobody had died today. It was supper, and I had an appetite for it, and that is all I am going to say.

He would go home, after this, and put on a record. It was in his file. I had nothing to put on.

I enjoyed it. I want to put that plainly. I had been read correctly, from across a city, by a man who had stood in my flat and handled my things, and I had read him back, and got it right, and watched him get me right in real time. Nobody had done that to me since I signed. Nobody had looked at me long enough.

He was going to come for me himself. Not with the police's permission, not with Holt's, not with a stranger's on a clean brief. He had walled himself in, on all three sides, with me. We were the same kind of alone.

The kettle clicked off. I made the tea strong with milk and stood holding the mug in both hands in the blue of the tablet, not drinking it, and something warm went through me, low and brief, and I put it down to the tea.

* * *

After one I put the desk lamp on and nothing else. The flat had gone properly cold. The radiators were still off, and the floorboards came up through my socks, and the lamp made a pool on the walnut the size of a dinner plate with the rest of the room standing back from it.

I unlocked the drawer and lifted the dossiers out the way he had, as a block, my fingers spread under the bottom file and my thumb flat on the top file, and set them on the desk without letting the top one slide. I slid the sleeve out. The paper in it has gone soft at the corners the way paper goes when it has been handled for two years and never used. I took out Lesson Two and laid it under the lamp.

I read it as he would have read it, at this desk, in daylight, while I stood across the city under a fan with my hands in somebody else's chest. The redactions, square and black. The second person, coming at him off the page. *You see every room exactly as it is.* I heard it in his voice. I had only ever heard his voice through a button the size of a fingernail, flattened and a beat late, and I heard it anyway, because now I knew he had heard it in his own.

I asked myself whether today changed the Evaluation. I took the question seriously; when new evidence arrives you go back to the finding, and you don't go back to it hoping. I read the line at the foot of the page twice. It held. If anything it read better for his having read it.

"The Architect," I said to the flat, in the syllabus voice, which is the voice I use for the dictaphone with the warmth taken out, "is hunting the student."

The curriculum had assumed I would set every lesson. It had made no allowance for a subject turning up to sit the exam early, in my flat, uninvited, and reading the marking scheme. Most students wait for the exam. He had broken into the staff room to read the marking scheme, and then put it back nearly where he found it, which in my experience is the difference between a cheat and a scholar.

I noticed I was not afraid.

I went looking for it the way you go looking for an organ that should be there: the spleen, say, which can hide up under the ribs and which you have to put your hand in and feel for. I put my hand in. It wasn't there. What was there instead, in the place where fear should have been, was interest, packed in tight and dense, the way the spleen is when it has been working hard. I am reporting that finding. I am not going to name it.

Under Lesson Two in the sleeve lay the third page. I didn't take it out. I didn't look at it. I only knew, with the lamp on the back of my hands, that he had read it this afternoon, and that he was now the second person alive who had.

The pen tray holds my red pen, the ordinary one, the one I use on the masters. The marker in the kit is for circling people. I uncapped the pen and the smell of it came up, faint, a little bitter, nothing like the solvent of the marker; it smelled of a school desk rather than a whiteboard. The last thing I had circled in red was pinned to a woman's chest. It felt almost wholesome to use red on paper nobody was wearing, the way red was designed to be used, by a teacher, at a desk, late, with a cold cup of tea at her elbow.

My hand was steady. I bent to the margin beside the subject line, and the nib went across the paper with a dry, small scratch, the only sound in the flat, and I wrote one word and a full stop.

*Excellent.*

I capped the pen and laid the page back in the sleeve, squared to its own edge, not to his, and slid the sleeve home and lowered the dossiers onto it as a block. Then I put the edge of his file to the knot, out of two years' habit, and moved it. A quarter of an inch to the left. I checked the distance with a fingernail, the way you check the depth of a cut. I put my hand flat on the manila, on his name, and kept it there, and didn't move the folder at all. The tiredness came up through me all at once, from the cold soles of my feet, the first thing my body had asked me for all day that wasn't food. I took the hand away.

I left his dossier a quarter-inch to the left, where he had put it, and slept nine hours.
