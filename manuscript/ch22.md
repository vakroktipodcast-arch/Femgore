# Chapter 22

My hand was flat on the sticker, and the count was at five.

It was a white label the size of a luggage tag, laid across the gap between the door and the jamb at the height of my shoulder, with the Service's legend printed along it in navy and, under the print, down in the adhesive where nobody could see it yet, a word I knew was there. It was the same make I put on evidence bags at work. I have sealed thousands of them and signed across the seal, and I have never once thought of it as a lock. Tamper-evident is a modest promise. It doesn't say nobody will get in. It says somebody will know. A sticker is what you have left when you can't have a key, and I had looked at this one twice that evening and noticed its honesty.

The plan had been to lift it. A No. 22 blade slid flat under the label along the jamb edge and drawn down in short strokes, the way you lift tape off a decedent's cheek without taking the epidermis with it, so that the label came away whole and could be laid back down afterwards. It would not have been clean. Nothing lifts that make clean; the word stays ghosted in the adhesive for anyone who puts their face to it. But it would have read as untouched to a man glancing down a passage, and a glance was all the passage would get. I had allowed fifteen seconds for that. I had allowed fifteen because I had sixty. The plan had been built in the last week of October on two clips of a closing door, and on neither of them had the minute gone over.

I had forty, and five of them were gone.

I put the clipboard flat down the back of my waistband, under the blazer, out of the way. I thumbed the guard off the scalpel and ran the blade down the gap instead, top to bottom, through the label where it bridged the door and the frame. A cut where there should have been a lift. Then I put my shoulder to the door. It went inward the way pantry doors go, heavy and grudging on its closer. As it moved, the face of the label caught on the door's edge and parted. It made a small dry sound, a zip. It sounded exactly like a plaster coming off a heel. I heard it and kept going. The guard went back on the blade by feel. The blade went into its sheath, and the sheath into the right inside pocket of the blazer. I turned sideways to go through the gap, and the scar on my left flank pulled once, hard. I pressed it through the blazer with the heel of my left hand. It quieted. I was thinking.

The closer brought the door to behind me and it touched my heel as it came home, a soft push, like a nurse's hand in the small of the back. On the suite side there was a thumb-turn, small and plain. The annex had a line on it, under the walk-through photograph: the hotel wanted that door kept off the lock from the inside, for the servers, and couldn't lock it from the corridor at all, so the Service had sealed it. I turned it. It went over with a click I felt in the bones of my thumb more than I heard it, and then nobody could come in behind me, and I had chosen that. Through the wall the ballroom was playing something with a lot of brass in it, warming the room up, and the brass took the click and kept it.

I was behind a screen. Hotels stand one in front of every service door, a folding banquet screen in three hinged panels upholstered in something the colour of weak tea, so that the guests don't have to look at who is bringing the food.

I smelled the room once I had stopped moving. The carpet had been shampooed that afternoon, and the whole floor gave off the cleaner, a synthetic floral that has never grown anywhere, sitting on top of the wet-dog note of nylon pile that hadn't quite dried; under it, in this room only, cedar, and the warm dust coming off the lamp. That was all. I had my own breath too, in a room that still, the faint sweetness of a body that had been burning itself since Sunday morning. Nobody else would have caught it. I caught it.

Then I looked. Through the gap at the hinge I could see a slice of the room. The one upholstered chair. The navy jacket already off and hung on its back, square at the shoulders, the flag pin on the lapel catching the lamp. His shoulder in a white shirt, and the knot of a tie, and the dressing table, which was a banquet table in a skirt, and on it the corner of a plain black frame, turned away from me, towards him. He didn't turn. He hadn't heard anything worth turning for. Across the room the main door was shut on its closer, and its privacy latch was thrown. He had thrown it himself when he came in; it was part of the minute. So both doors were locked, and he had locked one of them, and I had locked the other, and we were, for the moment, the only two people in the building who could say that.

I was hungry. Not in the stomach. The stomach had gone quiet somewhere in the back corridors that afternoon, and I hadn't asked it to start again. This was further up and further forward, behind the face, the hunger you feel in a restaurant when the door to the kitchen swings and you see your own plate on the pass. It was the first thing in me since the bathroom floor that had leaned forward, and it was leaning at the back of a man's head through the hinge of a screen.

I ran the ways out the way I'd run a differential, most likely first. There were two. The main door went through the detail. The service door was clean until forty, when the post would step back to the stair landing and the stair would be watched again. Everything else in the room was ruled out by the walls.

Somewhere in this building a woman had a list with my name on it, and from everything I knew of her, which was a signature and a line on page four, she was the kind of person who reads labels.

I looked down at my right hand. On the tip of the index finger, stuck to the nitrile, there was a tongue of the label's face film the size of a fingernail, curled at one edge, and on it the letters stood up pale where the adhesive had let go. VOID. The rest of the word was on the far side of the door, split down the middle and lifting from the frame, telling anyone who walked down the passage exactly what it had promised to tell them. I peeled the scrap off my glove and put it in my right outer pocket, beside the roll of tape. Evidence control. My hands did it without me.

The sticker was torn. Someone would see it. My clock had become a shorter clock.

* * *

I stepped out from behind the screen.

It was four paces of shampooed carpet to the near side of the dressing table, and the carpet gave a little under each one, damp in the pile, and the lamp came onto the side of my face as I cleared the screen, a small local warmth like standing next to someone who has just come in from running. My fingertips stayed cold inside the gloves. I didn't go round to his side. I stopped where the table was between us, and so the photograph was between us too.

The table had been dressed by somebody who did this every night of the week. A lamp with a pleated shade. A bottle of water with its cap still sealed. A copy of tonight's remarks, printed, clipped at the top left corner with a black bulldog clip. And the photograph of his brother the Marine, in a plain black frame on a cardboard easel, turned to face the chair. From my side it was a rectangle of black felt and a single cardboard leg holding it up. I couldn't see the Marine. He could.

He was sitting forward in the chair with his forearms on his knees and his hands loosely together between them, in shirtsleeves, the tie knotted, looking at his brother. That was all he was doing. I had watched that minute from the outside on two clips, frame by frame, as a door with a man behind it, and I had supposed that inside it he did something: prayed, or spoke, or wept, or rehearsed. He looked at a photograph. His thumbs lay one over the other. His breathing was slow and even and came from low down, from a diaphragm that had been trained to fill ballrooms. I report it because it is what I saw.

Then he looked up from the photograph.

I looked first. I never look first. In every room I have ever worked in I have smelled and listened and touched and weighed before I let my eyes do anything, because the eyes will tell you a story and you have to have the facts in hand before you let them. This was the face I had come for. I looked at it first.

I read it the way I read a face on intake, because I have never been able to look at a face any other way. A startle: the brows went up, frontalis drawing the skin of the forehead into three soft lines and letting it go again, perhaps half a second. His face was turned into the lamp, and the pupils didn't change. They were small and they stayed small. There was no flinch at the eyes, no narrowing, no blanching at the lips or the alae of the nose, and his hands, loose between his knees, did not come apart. It was the ordinary startle, the one a man gives when a door opens that he thought was shut. I had waited two years for a second expression to come in behind that one. I stood there and waited for it.

It didn't come.

He saw me. He did not recognise me.

His eyes went to the lanyard. They went to the white card on it, and the gold back-of-house band, and the navy-and-gold header with his own name across it in block capitals, and the three lines under that, somebody else's name, VOLUNTEER, PRESS ASSISTANT, and they stayed there a moment, reading, and then they came back up to my face with the matter settled. They didn't go to my hands. A woman with a lanyard had appeared in a room he had locked, and his face had filed her the way rooms like this one have always filed women with lanyards: staff, here for something, about to tell him the time. I had imagined this face for two years, in every state but the one it was in, which was pleased to see me.

Two years of rehearsal should have left me a mark ready for this face, and I reached for one the way you reach for a pen you always keep in the same pocket, and nothing came to hand.

Through the wall the ballroom had gone up a notch. The brass had given way to drums, and over the drums a man on a microphone was telling a room full of people to get ready, it wouldn't be long now. Under it my count went on by itself, and behind me, past the torn label, the stair was still unwatched. My hands were at my sides. The left one wanted the inside breast pocket of the blazer, where the page was folded against the hard case and the marker and the pin, and I didn't let it go there. I didn't take my pulse. I have taken my own pulse in most rooms that mattered since August. I didn't take it in this one.

Then he smiled.

It was the smile from the debate: the whole face he had turned on the man at the other lectern in the seconds after he touched his cuff, the whole face the break-room television had shown me on the morning of the debate, smiling at the camera. Zygomaticus major drew the corners of his mouth up and back. Orbicularis oculi came in under it, the ring of muscle round each eye tightening, the skin at the outer canthi gathering into fans. That second muscle is the one most people can't do on purpose. It was a true smile, by every sign I know how to read, and it was for me, and it meant nothing.

No water came, and no heat, and not the coin at the back of the tongue either. I had been hungry for this for two years, and I found there was nothing in my mouth.

* * *

I stood across the table from him and waited. I held still, as if the next line would arrive if I gave it long enough.

Thirteen gone, of forty. The scrap of label in my pocket. The rest of it split on the door. A corridor that was about to be watched again.

I know what recognition looks like on a face. I have watched it arrive through the glass of the viewing room when the sheet is turned back: half a second of a stranger, and then the face falls into the one they knew, and the face at the glass falls with it. Diane had done it on the concrete with a small dry click at the back of her throat, before she had decided to.

The drums through the wall. The low burble of an earpiece radio on the far side of the main door, somebody saying something short and somebody else saying something shorter. The lamp, ticking very faintly as the shade heated. His breathing. In the crease beside his nose there was a fleck of the powder they had put on him for the cameras, the colour of milky tea, sitting on the skin where the brush had missed. On a table I would have noted it. I noted it. I let it go.

He stood up.

It was courtesy. A woman was standing, so he stood. It was in him the way the startle had been, below thought. As he came up out of the chair his right hand crossed his body. His thumb and forefinger took the left cufflink, plain silver, and gave it a quarter-turn. His eyes went down to it. Two seconds. With the jacket off there was nothing over it, only the white cuff and the small silver bar, and I saw all of it from a metre away. Then his eyes came up. His hands came open a little at his sides, the palms half-turned towards me, the way a man holds his hands when he is about to ask if he can help.

"I think there may have been a mix-up," he said. "Are you with the network?"

He said it helpfully. His voice was a baritone at the volume of a small room, unamplified, warm, unhurried, the question turned up at its end the way his palms were, and it was offering me a way out of my own embarrassment before I had shown any. The badge said PRESS ASSISTANT. He had read it, and been kind about it.

There was a joke in it. I could see the whole shape of it. The network. Eleven of us on a list, my name among them. And one more, who had signed what I signed, and who had left me a message on a Sunday afternoon three years ago, from an office, that began *I think we may have... known the same person*. That was a network. I didn't make the joke. I am only showing you where it was.

My left hand went to the blazer. This time I let it. Through the cloth I found the run of show, and behind it the page, folded in thirds, warm from lying against me all evening. I didn't take it out. I know every word on it.

He waited the way he waited at a lectern, with interest.

I had no line for this. The syllabus began with his recognition. I am telling you that. I can hear how small my voice has got.

He stood there in his shirtsleeves. He waited, politely, for an answer about the network.

Between us on the table, the Marine, facing him. All I had of him was the back of the frame.
