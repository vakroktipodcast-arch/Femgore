# Chapter 7

I have told you I left. That was the short version. The long one took the best part of half an hour, and I had written it in my head two years before. Nobody grades leaving, because when it is done well there is nothing left to grade, so I graded it myself, item by item, while forty metres down the sumac spur the trail went on being a public footpath on a Sunday morning.

The station smelled different with her finished. Damp concrete still, and the iron of the pipework, but over them now the toner coming off the wet paper, and under everything the urine, which a body lets go at the end, and which I named and stepped round. Every contact leaves a trace. That is Locard's exchange principle, and it is also true of family Christmas, which nobody has ever successfully cleaned up after either. You do not get to leave no trace. You get to choose which ones.

What went with me, first. The bag was already half full: the industrial tape; the four zip-ties, cut at the bracket and at the ankles; the band of tape from her ankles. Zip-ties carry lot numbers, and a lot number is the loose end of a sleeve that anybody patient can pull. In after them went the prepaid that had rung for her, battery out, the battery zipped into a pocket of its own, because a phone in two pieces is not a phone, it is a paperweight with ambitions. The clear zip bag that had held the paper. The red marker. The glassine envelope with its cap in it.

Her own phone was still in its foil by the lantern. I slid it out. The foil was kit, so the foil came with me; the phone went back where she had kept it, powered off, into the left inside pocket of her jacket, under the waxed cotton. A missing phone writes a robbery. Somebody would read the vacant pocket and build a thief to fit it, and I was not going to have anybody else's story laid over mine. The last thing that screen had shown was her daughter, asking her to call after her walk. I knew that. I didn't turn it on to check.

What stayed I can give you in one breath, because I had built it to be read in one. Diane on the concrete with her back to the pump housing. Two strips of black vinyl over her eyes. The page over the manubrium on its single pin, THE GATEKEEPER circled in red. The paper in her mouth, and the few pieces on her jacket and the floor, gone soft as felt where they were wet and ash-grey at the edges. Nothing else: no tie, no tape, no fibre of mine. Every scrap of that paper carried the number under its thin rule, the campaign's own filing aimed back at the people who did the filing, and I was very pleased with it. Numbers point both ways, though. A matter number tells you which matter, not whose, and that one ran, as it turned out, to a list with me on it, and I should have asked.

Then my own trace. I took myself off just inside the door in the reverse of the order I had put myself on: overshoes; outer gloves, the right one with the print of her incisors in its fingertip; hair cover; the coverall peeled down and rolled in on itself so that its outside would touch nothing again. All of it into a bag, and that bag into another, with the small dry crackle of somebody unwrapping a sweet in church. Under it I was a Sunday walker in a fleece and trainers. I turned each trainer up and read its sole by the lantern. The inner gloves stayed on. They would stay on until the car.

Then the sweep. I laid the lantern on its side on the concrete so that its light went flat across the floor instead of down onto it. Overhead light forgives. Oblique light finds: a fibre stands up and throws a shadow ten times its length, a hair becomes a cable. I have watched scene examiners for a decade, and most of them hold the torch up like a candle at a vigil, pious and useless. I crouched at the threshold and read the floor in strips from where I was. You don't walk back across a floor you have just read. Nothing stood up out of it but dust, and the dust was the station's.

I looked at her once more from the door, the way I look at a body laid out for a viewing before I let the family in. Is the face composed. Is there anything in the room that they will remember instead of her. The page lay flat. The circle was closed. The vinyl was level, temple to temple. Presentation: distinction. I listened a moment longer than I needed to. The drip. The faint whine of the lantern. Nobody beginning anything. I logged the lingering as what it was, a cost to the schedule, forty seconds over, and turned the lantern off and put it in the pack last, and it was twenty-five past eleven.

Outside, I drew the steel door to. My padlock had hung open on the hasp since before dawn; I lifted it off with a small chink and put it in the pack, and swung the hasp over its staple with nothing through it, a flat clack, iron on iron. A shut door with no lock on it. Nobody had looked at that door in years. It was the door I had clapped against on Saturday, three times, to learn what the trail could hear through it. Nothing.

The day outside was overcast and flat after the lantern's blue, the colour of the inside of a sink, and my eyes took a moment with it. Sumac, leaf litter going over, the green breath of the reservoir. The air was cooler than I was and warmer than the station, and the sweat from inside the coverall went cold on my neck. Down the spur to the marker post. The trail was clear both ways, as it had been on every Sunday I watched it, and I took the service track nine hundred metres to the lay-by where my car sat out of sight. Hers was in the south car park.

I walked at the pace of a woman with nowhere to be. That is a skill, and I am good at it. For a woman alone on a trail it is a costume with very few pieces and none of them optional: hands in the pockets, eyes on the middle distance, the gait of someone thinking about lunch. My hands were in my fleece pockets because of what was on them. I was thinking about lunch. I had eaten nothing since Saturday, and my stomach was taking it up with management. I noted it as a fact about the schedule.

I was at the lay-by, the car in sight and the county road loud beyond the hedge, when a bell rang behind me. Two bright notes, the ding-ding of good manners. A rider went by on my left in a flash of hi-vis and a slap of displaced air that I felt on my cheek before I had turned my head, and was gone down the track towards the road, past the MARCHETTI sign at the turn, without looking back. I saw no face. Nobody saw mine. Tyres on packed gravel make almost no sound. The bike had come up behind me from the direction of the pump station, along a track I had believed I had to myself, and I did not know how long it had been there. I took my pulse through the glove with the fleece cuff pulled down over both, and didn't care for the number. I had not been alone out there for as long as I had believed.

* * *

I was home at a quarter past twelve. Procedure first. The bag inside the bag went where it goes, and I am not going to tell you where. The padlock went back to where I keep such things. The trainers went into a bag of their own. The fleece and everything under it went into the machine on the hottest wash it does, which is hot enough to set an egg. I had peeled the inner gloves off at the car, inside out, and they had ridden home in a pocket; they went into the bag last, before the bag went.

Then forty minutes in the shower, as hot as the boiler allows. Unscented soap. I scrubbed the way you scrub for theatre: the nails with the brush, then each finger as if it had four sides, which it does, the webs between, the backs of the hands, up the forearms to the elbow, the water running off the elbow and never back down onto the hands. Forty minutes is how long a surgical scrub would take if the surgeon had nothing to operate on afterwards and simply wanted to be clean. The pump station would not come out of my hair. Damp concrete gets into hair the way a cellar gets into a coat, and I washed it twice and the cellar stayed. The morgue has never come off my skin either, so this was only a new tenant, and I am used to tenants. The mirror over the basin had fogged white by the time I got out. I left it that way.

I checked for tears. I check for them the way I check a decedent for a pulse, which is to say as procedure, not as hope. Two fingers and a count. There were none. I am reporting it, not making it mean anything.

I lay down on the bed in a towel, with my hair wet on the pillow, to let my heart rate come down. I gave it an hour. It was a quarter past one. When I opened my eyes the flat was dark, and the pillow was damp under my cheek, and the clock said twenty to two, and it was the next morning.

I had slept through four o'clock. Diane once told a trade magazine that she called her mother every Sunday at four, no matter what. Somewhere a woman had sat beside a phone at four, and I had been asleep. That is all I am going to say about it.

It was hunger that woke me. That had never happened before, and I noted it as new data. I had been ravenous at the pump-station door, and I had slept straight through ravenous for twelve hours, and that was the first thing all day I could not account for.

I cooked by the lamp in the cooker hood and nothing else. The marrow bones and the onglet were from the butcher's. I had bought them on Saturday. The oven went on as hot as it goes. There were three bones, beef femur, cut lengthwise into canoes by a man with a band saw and opinions, and I salted the cut faces and put them in to roast. The steak came out of the fridge to lose its chill on the board. I sliced shallots thin. I cut the heel off a sourdough loaf and toasted it. I did all of it standing, the way I plan.

At twenty to three I ate the marrow on the stool at the counter, and I am going to take my time over it, because I did. It came out of the oven sizzling at the edges and trembling in the middle, the yellow marrow gone from opaque to nearly clear, which is the adipocytes giving up their fat, cell by cell, in the heat, and I ran the handle of a teaspoon down the medullary cavity of a femur, where I had last seen marrow on a slide, and it came out of the bone in one soft glistening scoop that I laid on the toast and salted and covered with parsley and lemon and put in my mouth, and it was warm salted butter with a mineral note under it, like licking the spoon you have left in the stockpot, the parsley green and bitter against it, the lemon cutting through the fat like a door opened in a hot room. I did all three bones. I scraped the third with the spoon end until it squeaked.

At three I seared the onglet. The pan as hot as it would go, the fat spitting, the fridge humming to itself, and outside the building the particular silence of three in the morning, which is not a silence so much as a city holding its breath to see whether you'll notice. A hard crust on both sides and rare in the middle. The shallots went into the butter left in the pan, and the butter went over the meat, and I sat on the stool and ate it with the rest of the bread to mop. The crust was salt and char. The middle was oxblood, faintly ferrous, with a liverish depth to it that nothing else on a cow has. Sear: distinction.

Onglet is the crura of the diaphragm, the two muscular pillars that anchor it to the spine, the muscle that does the breathing. I had watched one stop the morning before. I salted it. The French call it the butcher's steak, because the butcher kept it for himself.

Then, because I am a doctor, I gave the lecture. Fight or flight: adrenaline, a flood of it, for hours. The liver turns out its glycogen and the muscles burn theirs, and when the emergency ends the body finds its stores gone and asks, quite reasonably, to have them back. Cortisol stays up behind the adrenaline like a creditor, breaking down protein for the liver to make new sugar from, which is gluconeogenesis, which is the body pawning the furniture. No food since Saturday. Hunger is the expected finding. The literature is clear that the post-adrenergic body wants its glycogen back. The literature is silent on whether it wants bone marrow. I have read the literature. Adrenaline also produces a fine tremor of the hands, eight to twelve hertz, which is why people who have just had a fright can't get a key into a lock. All of that is true, and I said all of it to myself in the voice I use for families, and I gave the lecture a pass, which is a lower mark than I gave the steak.

The plate in front of me was clean. I had wiped it with the last of the bread. My fork was in my right hand. My left hand lay on the counter beside the plate, palm down, fingers slightly apart, and it was perfectly still. No eight hertz. No twelve. Nothing at all. I gave it a line in the dictation voice, the way I would a hand on my table. Left hand, at rest. No tremor observed.

* * *

I washed up. I dried the pan and hung it on its hook. The marrow bones went into the pedal bin double-bagged, because that is what we do with bone at work, and I did it at half past three in the morning in my own kitchen out of pure habit, and if you can't find that funny I can't help you. Then I turned the lamp off and sat in the dark with the window black until five, and nothing happened in it worth telling you.

At five I made tea, builder's, with milk, and drank it standing at the window. I had opened the window a hand's width, and September came in through the gap and lay on my wrists. It was still full dark. The tea was too strong and too hot, tannic enough to fur the teeth, and the first mouthful went down over a stomach that was still full and still warm from three o'clock, like pouring hot water onto a hot-water bottle. I was rested. I want that on the record as a finding: rested, for the first time in weeks, with nothing behind the eyes but the eyes.

I knew where she was. It came whether I asked for it or not, the way the answer to a sum does. She would have been brought in overnight, after they found her, and she would be lying now in the cold room at four degrees, in a bag, on a tray, with a tag on her toe in somebody's capitals. The lividity would be fixed by now, settled low in her where gravity had put it, the way it settles in somebody who died sitting up, and no amount of turning her would move it. She was eighteen hours dead and a little more, which put rigor at its height or just past it: the small muscles first, the jaw first of all, so that her jaw would be set round the paper as hard as if she had decided to keep it, and the fingers curled, and the big muscles of the thighs locked like a door in a heatwave. Rigor mortis is the body's last attempt to hold a position, and like most last attempts it lasts about a day and a half and then lets go of everything at once. It was my work, going on without me in a fridge across the city, at exactly the rate I would have predicted, and I stood at the window and enjoyed the accuracy of it the way you enjoy a clock that keeps good time.

The grey came up slowly towards six. Down the street the sodium lights went out block by block, as if somebody were walking along switching them off by hand, and the first bus went by with nobody on it.

My phone buzzed on the counter at two minutes to six, loud on the hard surface as a dropped instrument. A news alert. The campaign had a statement out. *A senseless act. Our hearts are with Olivia and the whole Pell family. We ask for privacy at this time.* I read it twice. As condolence, it was a C-minus: it spelled grief correctly and had nothing in it a person had ever said aloud. As something else, it was very good. They had used her daughter's name in the second sentence. Somebody in that room was very good at their job, and I hoped it was someone I'd met.

I had not opened the War Room feed. It was there, Sunday night in that room, recorded and waiting, and I had not let myself look. I was saving it the way you save the last bite.

At four minutes past six the phone went again. Mara. *You up? Unexpected one in overnight. Woman, 50s, off the reservoir trail. Chief rang me at home.*

I read it twice. The second time I read it for what it left out: no name, no tape, no page, no paper. Whoever had told her what was on the tray had not told her what had come in with it. I knew whose body it was. I had known before the phone made a sound.

At eleven minutes past: *He's given it to me. Says he wants 'a face that's never been near politics'. You're senior reviewer. Hope that's ok??*

So the Chief already knew it was political. Nobody had opened her yet, and the building had already decided what kind of body she was, and chosen accordingly. A face that has never been near politics, which in this city narrows it down to the dead and Mara, and the dead were busy. I read the two question marks at the end of her message and put no mark of my own beside them.

I typed a reply. I deleted it. I typed another, shorter, and deleted that. My thumb on the glass was steady both times, and in the blue of the screen I watched it be steady.

I hadn't been asked. I would be in the room anyway, on the far side of the table, standing where Mara had stood last week, watching somebody else take apart a thing I had made.

I replied: *On my way.* I was already dressed. I had been dressed for an hour.
