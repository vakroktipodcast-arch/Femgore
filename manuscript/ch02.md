# Chapter 2

The service entrance smelled of chafing-dish fuel, that blue, faintly sweet alcohol of a gel burning under a tray, and behind it, from deeper in the building, beef jus reducing in a pan big enough to bath a child in. Then floor cleaner with a pine note, suggesting a forest that has recently been mopped. The linen carts breathed their warm-laundry breath at me as I passed, like cattle in a shed. I came in under the steel lintel a little before seven on the Thursday with a badge lying flat on my sternum that said CLARE WHITLOW / PRESS, and nobody looked up, because I walked in the way you walk into a building you are already late for.

Clare Whitlow is a real freelance writer who files rarely and was, as far as I could establish, out of the country. I had borrowed her name the way you borrow sugar from a neighbour who's away: without asking, and meaning to give it back unopened. On Tuesday night, in the end, I had only walked, with the kit knocking against my hip, until the thing that had got me off the kitchen stool had been walked down into something I could use. The credential took what was left of that night and all of Wednesday's. It was good work. I don't say that about many things I've made at a kitchen table.

The fundraiser was a donor reception for the host committee in the mezzanine ballroom. The Senator was on the road after Tuesday, being his to lose in other cities, and would join by video link; the dossier in my drawer put Diane Pell on the road with him. The War Room was on the thirty-first floor of the same hotel, because campaigns, like the rest of us, prefer to live above the shop. I was there to look at the building. Recon is work, and work was the one thing I had never had to explain to anybody.

At the press table a volunteer with a shoebox of lanyards looked at the one I was already wearing, at its colour and not its name, and pointed me with her pen towards the pen. There was no list. I want that down the way I would dictate a finding, because it will matter later: no list, no check, nobody on the door but a shoebox. Nobody checks a lanyard. A lanyard is a promise that somebody else has already checked. It is the door everyone walks through, because everyone already has a lanyard.

The ballroom's air-conditioning had been set for men in wool, and it lay along my forearms like a hand. I liked it. It was the morgue's cold, the sort you choose. I gave the press pen four minutes, which is about the attention span of a press pen, and left it by the service door at the back with my phone held face-up in front of me, as if it had my instructions on it.

The corridor behind a ballroom is the corridor behind a ward. Steel doors with kick plates, a fluorescent tube flickering at the rate of a slow pulse, carts, and everyone with a lanyard presumed to belong, because nobody in a corridor like that has time to ask. I have walked fifteen years of them at the pace of a woman slightly late, the most invisible pace there is. I rode the service lift once to the thirty-first. The executive corridor's door had a flap of water-case cardboard folded into its strike, so that it swung for anyone and the card reader blinked at no one, and beyond it a young man in shirtsleeves was propping a suite door open on its swing bar with a case of water. He didn't look back. I rode down again.

A server came off the floor with a tray of lamb cutlets, frenched, the bones as white as piano keys, barely touched. Donors don't eat at fundraisers. Eating is what you do when you haven't already paid for the room. I took four in a napkin without breaking stride, and he let me, because I had a lanyard. I ate them in the service stair with my back to the wall.

I want to be exact. The fat at the edge had crisped under the grill and gone soft again under the heat lamps, so that it gave between the teeth like warm wax and then ran, and the eye was still pink and tasted of the animal, of grass and something close to sweat, which is what you eat lamb for. Char at the bone, bitter and good. The salsa verde had skinned over and gone warm, but there was caper in it still, and anchovy, and parsley chopped by somebody tired. I gnawed each bone clean, getting the last of it out of the angle where the shaft meets the eye with my incisors. Four. I had skipped dinner. It was physiology, and I filed it there. My hands, when I wiped them, were steady. By the top of the stair one of Clare Whitlow's shoes had opened a conversation with my heel.

From the mouth of the corridor I read the staff the way I read anything that comes in at work: from the outside, before anyone gives me the history. The deputy finance director had the yellowed sclera and spider naevi of a man whose liver was filing a complaint I could hear from the doorway, and like every complaint in that building, it was being managed. The advance man had bitten his nails past the quick and had a smoker's cough he didn't smoke for. A woman from comms stood with one shoulder higher than the other, a scoliotic tilt nobody had ever told her about, which also seemed a fair description of her job.

I ranked them, out of professional curiosity, the only kind you're allowed in public. The comms woman third; you can see most of a spine from outside. The advance man second, for the lungs. The finance man first by a distance, for the liver, which would have come out the colour of a church candle, and I would have enjoyed telling him. That was funny, and I knew it was funny, and I let myself have it.

It was not the ranking I had come to do.

A staffer came down the corridor fast, twenty-something, earpiece, a clipboard held flat to his chest like the shield on a knight's tomb. He looked at the badge and not at me, the right order for him and the wrong one for his employer, and waved me through to the back of the ballroom.

"Go on, Diane's coming down, she'll want the pen tidy."

Her name sat in his mouth worn smooth. I heard it first as a fact about the schedule, and the fact was wrong: Diane Pell was meant to be on the road. Then I heard it as what it was. She was here tonight, unscheduled, and so was I.

The recon had become an opportunity.

* * *

I heard the change before I saw her. A ballroom at donor pitch has a roar to it, broad and steady, like surf heard from a car park, a few hundred people telling each other things they already know. Under it a string trio sawed through Vivaldi for nobody; over it, the bright clatter of ice in glasses. At twenty past eight the roar dipped on the main stair, the way a ward goes quiet when the consultant comes on, and Diane Pell came down into it.

I had not seen her in three years. Fifty-two. A slate suit, cut well and worn plainly. Low heels, about three centimetres, which I subtracted without being asked. Reading glasses pushed up into an ash-blonde bob. She stood straight from the hips, the way women stand who were once made to, with a swimmer's shoulders gone a little soft under the jacket. I took her height off the donor beside her and her weight off the way the suit sat across her back: about a hundred and sixty-five centimetres out of the heels, about seventy kilos. It was how I would have taken her on the table. I didn't notice, at the time, that I had done it.

She worked the room in loops of ninety seconds, and none that I timed ran over. She remembered which man had given the maximum and which had only said he would, and let the second see that she remembered without letting him see that she minded. She touched no one for longer than a handshake. She handled money the way I handle organs: by weight, with both hands, and with no illusions about where it had been.

Then the screen at the end of the ballroom woke and Holt Marchetti was on it for four minutes, from a hotel in another city that looked exactly like this one, his voice coming through the speakers with a faint hiss half a second behind his mouth, as though he had been dubbed into his own life. I didn't watch him. I had seen the show. I watched Diane watch him from beside a pillar, a glass in her hand she wasn't drinking from, and partway through her lips shaped one of his words a beat before he said it. *Together.* Of course she knew where he was going. She had laid the road.

Earlier I had seen a young staffer at the rope line talking low to a man with a recorder and nodding at that pillar. When her loop brought her to it, the man was waiting with a question about staff turnover in the Senator's office, which was a good question, put well. Diane paused once before she answered: a small stop, a held note at the glottis and then a release, so that her first word arrived a fraction late and very clean. He took it for thoughtfulness. Everybody does. I had read the file. It was a stutter that a girl had beaten, a long time ago and with nobody's help, into a pause, and the pause had been trained so well it passed for judgement. Her answer gave him nothing and made him feel thanked. I watched him write the nothing down, gratefully.

The young staffer's name was Evan. I know because I watched her say it, through the porthole in the service door beside the press pen, the kind they put in kitchen doors so that the people carrying soup don't kill each other. I couldn't hear a word. I read lips about as well as anyone who has spent fifteen years under extraction fans, and I read the rest of him the way I read a body, by what had been done to it. She said his first name once. She said *badge*, and *desk*. His hands went to his lanyard as if it had got tight. Then she put her hand on his arm, a handshake's length, and said, *Go home. Eat something.* She meant the second thing. That was the decent part, and I want you to keep it, because nobody would have marked her down if she hadn't said it. Thirty seconds, door to door. I have seen slower deaths certified as sudden.

In ninety minutes she was never more than an arm's length from a donor, a staffer or her phone. When it rang, she answered on the first ring, in the middle of a donor's sentence, and the donor beamed as if he had been promoted. She read one text, and as she turned her left wrist the dress watch slid towards the heel of her hand, and where it had been was a band of paler skin, wider than the watch and the wrong shape for it, the shape of a sports strap. Her face did something private for about a second. Then the phone went away and the face came back.

I graded her. I didn't decide to. It came out in the voice I use for the dictaphone, which was, I had begun to notice, the voice I use for everything I mean. Subject: the Chief of Staff. Donors: distinction. Press: distinction. Personnel: distinction, with a note for the boy and the sandwich she had more or less told him to buy. Decent in nearly every register audible from the back of a ballroom. The failing mark is not a comment on any of this. The failing mark is for what all of this is for. You will be evaluated accordingly.

Fail.

At about ten to ten she left by the side aisle, past the press pen, and as she went she looked along the row of us the way you look along a shelf for the thing you came in for. She looked at me for exactly as long as she looked at the man beside me. Her eyes passed over me the way you pass over a fire door. Three years ago I had sat across the desk in her own office for forty minutes while I signed, and she had watched my hand the whole time, to make sure it did what it was there to do.

She registered nothing. I was invisible to these people. It was my whole advantage, and it was the original injury, the same fact facing two ways, and I felt both. The first felt like cold air on my forearms. The second came up at the back of my tongue like a coin held there too long, and I didn't swallow it.

* * *

At ten the service lift took me back up to the thirty-first. The cardboard was still in the strike, gone soft with the hours, and the carpet changed underfoot from a pattern designed to hide gravy to a plain charcoal designed to hide nothing, because nothing would ever be spilt on it. The glass-walled corner suite was dark, because everyone who used it was downstairs being thanked. The suite beside it still stood ajar on its case of water. That wasn't chance. It was a campaign that believed nothing was coming, and I had banked on it the way you bank on gravity.

It had been booked as a bedroom and was being used as a cupboard. Cases of water, hip-high. A rack of rope-line stanchions, their belts coiled in the tops like sleeping snakes. Lawn signs in shrink-wrap. A bed nobody had slept in under a drift of tote bags, as if it had been snowed on by merchandise. The air-conditioning up here ran colder than the ballroom's, a dry filtered cold that went straight for the back of my neck.

I laid my things on the desk the way I lay out instruments, left to right in the order of use. Nitrile gloves. Two units, each the size of a coat button, camera and microphone in one, bought for cash. The roll of black vinyl. A small tablet, for pairing. I put the gloves on, left and then right, and the snap of the cuffs went into the carpet and stayed there.

The connecting door had a deadbolt on my side and would have one on theirs. I turned mine and tried the handle, on the theory that nobody bolts a door against themselves. It is the founding principle of every institution I have ever worked for. The handle went down and the door came towards me. Grade for the campaign, on security: the door everyone walks through and no one checks. They had already written it themselves, on the strike plate, in cardboard.

The War Room smelled of coffee stewed on its element since lunch, of carpet shampoo, and of the citrus solvent of a whiteboard marker left uncapped on the ledge. It wasn't my red one. I noticed that I checked. Glass on two sides, and below it the city in sodium, every street a seam of orange laid into the dark. The long table, its chairs pushed back at the angles people leave when they expect to come back. The wall screen, asleep. By the door, a credenza with a coffee urn on it, the element ticking as it cooled, and beside it a bin of paper cups.

I put the first unit behind the bezel of the wall screen, low on the left, facing the length of the table, and tore a short strip of vinyl to hold it. The tape came off the roll with a sound I hadn't heard from that kit before, a dry, rising rasp, like a zip opened in a library. The second unit went under the lip of the credenza beside the urn, angled up and out. One short strip. I pressed each down with the pad of my thumb until it had decided to stay. My knuckle grazed the window on the way back and the cold of the glass came straight through the nitrile. Two minutes. I closed the connecting door without touching anything of theirs, and turned my own bolt home.

I peeled the gloves off inside out into my pocket and sat at the desk with the tablet. The first unit came up and gave me the dark table end-on, like a runway at night. I was pairing the second when the suite door swung in.

A night houseman with a cart. About sixty, by the hands and the hairline. A name tag: DESMOND. He had come in because the door was open, which is what doors are for.

I had my left shoe off. That was true; the heel had blistered on the stair, and I had slipped the shoe off the moment I sat. I turned the tablet face-down on the desk, as if that were simply where I'd put it.

"Sorry," he said. "Door was open."

"Press. They said I could file in here."

"Course." He looked at my foot. "That's a blister."

"Stairs."

It was a good lie, and it had the great advantage of a blister in it. The best lies are mostly true and partly bleeding.

He looked at me then, properly, at my face, the way nobody had all night. He was the only person in that building who checked. He opened the first-aid box on his cart and held a plaster out across the corner of the desk.

I reached for it with my left hand, and the hand trembled, once. A single fine oscillation at the fingertips, there and gone. Then it was still, and I took the plaster and said thank you.

He didn't see it. He was looking round for somewhere to put the backing paper. I saw all of it.

I graded him, out of habit and something nearer gratitude. Full marks for looking. Full marks for the plaster. He looked at me as though I was there. It was the only A I gave all night.

"I'll leave that wider," he said on his way out, and pushed the door back against its stop with his foot. "For the air."

I had noticed the tremor. I didn't know what to do with it, so I am going to describe it to you instead. Left hand, four fingers, the thumb uninvolved. Amplitude: a millimetre, perhaps two, at the tips. One cycle, quicker than I could have made it on purpose. Duration: under a second. Onset as the hand reached for something offered. Resolution spontaneous and complete. Nothing in the right hand. Nothing since. The first since my second year of residency. I told you once that you would know what it meant if they ever shook. I don't. I am only putting it down where you can see it, the way you leave something on a table between two people. You're holding it too, now.

I put the plaster on my heel and pressed its edges down with my thumbnail, and packed the desk right to left, and left the door the way Desmond had, wide, for the air.

In the lift down I held my left hand with my right, two fingers across the wrist the way you hold a patient's to count, until the doors opened. I watched it the whole way, as if it belonged to somebody on my table and I was only there to write down what it did. The walls were brushed steel and gave back nothing but a smear of light. I didn't need them to. I could feel it in the zygomatic muscles, the corners of the mouth drawn up and back and held. I was smiling. I didn't know when that had started.
