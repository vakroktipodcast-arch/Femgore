# Chapter 2

I came in through the hotel's front doors a little before seven on the Thursday, with a badge lying flat on my sternum that said CLARE WHITLOW / PRESS, and nobody looked up, because I walked in the way you walk into a building you are already late for.

Clare Whitlow is a real freelance writer who files rarely and was, as far as I could establish, out of the country. I had borrowed her name the way you borrow sugar from a neighbour who's away: without asking, and meaning to give it back unopened. On Tuesday night, in the end, I had only walked until the cold that had come into my forearms in front of the tablet had settled into something I could carry. The credential took what was left of that night and all of Wednesday's.

The fundraiser was a donor reception for the host committee in the mezzanine ballroom. The Senator was on the road, being his to lose in other cities; the dossier in my drawer put Diane Pell on the road with him. The War Room was on the thirty-first floor of the same hotel, because campaigns, like the rest of us, prefer to live above the shop. I was there to look at the building. Recon is work, and work was the one thing I had never had to explain to anybody.

At the press table a volunteer with a shoebox of lanyards looked at the one I was already wearing, at its colour and not its name, and pointed me with her pen towards the pen. There was no list. I want that down the way I would dictate a finding, because it will matter later: no list, no check, nobody on the door but a shoebox. Nobody checks a lanyard. A lanyard is a promise that somebody else has already checked.

The ballroom's air-conditioning had been set for men in wool, and it lay along my forearms like a hand. It was the morgue's cold, the sort you choose. I gave the press pen four minutes, which is about the attention span of a press pen, and left it by the service door at the back with my phone held face-up in front of me, as if it had my instructions on it.

The corridor behind a ballroom is the corridor behind a ward. It smelled of chafing-dish fuel and of beef jus reducing somewhere, and of floor cleaner with a pine note, suggesting a forest that has recently been mopped. Steel doors, a fluorescent tube flickering at the rate of a slow pulse, linen carts breathing their warm-laundry breath like cattle in a shed, and everyone with a lanyard presumed to belong. I have walked fifteen years of them at the pace of a woman slightly late, the most invisible pace there is.

I rode the service lift once to the thirty-first. There was a camera dome over its doors, as there is over every service lift, and I gave it the crown of my head and the badge. Hotels keep about a month. Nobody asks for a month of a donor reception. Upstairs, the executive corridor's door had a flap of water-case cardboard folded into its strike, so that it swung for anyone and the card reader blinked at no one, and beyond it a young man in shirtsleeves was propping a suite door open on its swing bar with a case of water. He didn't look back. I noted which suite, and rode down again.

A server came off the floor with a tray of lamb cutlets, frenched, the bones as white as piano keys, barely touched. Donors don't eat at fundraisers. Eating is what you do when you haven't already paid for the room. I took four in a napkin without breaking stride, and he let me, because I had a lanyard. I ate them in the service stair with my back to the wall.

I want to be exact. The fat at the edge had crisped under the grill and gone soft again under the heat lamps, so that it gave between the teeth like warm wax and then ran, and the eye was still pink and tasted of the animal, of grass and something close to sweat, which is what you eat lamb for. Char at the bone, bitter and good. The salsa verde had skinned over and gone warm, but there was caper in it still, and anchovy, and parsley chopped by somebody tired. I gnawed each bone clean, getting the last of it out of the angle where the shaft meets the eye with my incisors. Four. I had skipped dinner. It was physiology, and I filed it there. My hands, when I wiped them, were steady. By the top of the stair one of Clare Whitlow's shoes had opened a conversation with my heel.

From the mouth of the corridor I read the staff from the outside, the way I read anything that comes in at work. The deputy finance director had the yellowed sclera and spider naevi of a man whose liver was filing a complaint I could hear from the doorway, and like every complaint in that building, it was being managed. The advance man had bitten his nails past the quick and had a smoker's cough he didn't smoke for. A woman from comms stood with one shoulder higher than the other, a scoliotic tilt nobody had ever told her about, which also seemed a fair description of her job.

I ranked them, out of professional curiosity, the only kind you're allowed in public. The comms woman third; you can see most of a spine from outside. The advance man second, for the lungs. The finance man first by a distance, for the liver, which would have come out the colour of a church candle. Somebody ought to tell him. I would have been glad to.

It was not the ranking I had come to do.

A staffer came down the corridor fast, twenty-something, earpiece, a clipboard held flat to his chest like the shield on a knight's tomb. He looked at the badge and not at me, the right order for him and the wrong one for his employer, and waved me through to the back of the ballroom.

"Go on, Diane's coming down, she'll want the pen tidy."

Her name sat in his mouth worn smooth. I heard it first as a fact about the schedule, and the fact was wrong: Diane Pell was meant to be on the road. Then I heard it as what it was. She was here tonight, unscheduled, and so was I.

I had come to examine the premises. The patient, it turned out, was in.

* * *

I heard the change before I saw her. A ballroom at donor pitch has a roar to it, broad and steady, like surf heard from a car park. Under it a string trio sawed through Vivaldi for nobody; over it, ice in glasses. At twenty past eight the roar dipped on the main stair, the way a ward goes quiet when the consultant comes on, and Diane Pell came down into it.

I had not seen her in three years. Fifty-two. A slate suit, cut well and worn plainly. Low heels, about three centimetres, which I subtracted without being asked. Reading glasses pushed up into an ash-blonde bob. She stood straight from the hips, the way women stand who were once made to, with a swimmer's shoulders gone a little soft under the jacket. I took her height off the donor beside her and her weight off the way the suit sat across her back: about a hundred and sixty-five centimetres out of the heels, about seventy kilos. It was how I would have taken her on the table. I didn't notice, at the time, that I had done it.

She worked the room in loops of ninety seconds. She remembered which man had given the maximum and which had only said he would, and let the second see that she remembered. She touched no one for longer than a handshake. She handled money the way I handle organs: by weight, with both hands, and with no illusions about where it had been.

Then the screen at the end of the ballroom woke and Holt Marchetti was on it for four minutes, his voice a faint hiss half a second behind his mouth, as though he had been dubbed into his own life. I didn't watch him. I had seen the show. I watched Diane watch him from beside a pillar, and partway through her lips shaped one of his words a beat before he said it. *Together.* She had laid the road.

Earlier I had seen a young staffer at the rope line talking low to a man with a recorder and nodding at that pillar. When her loop brought her to it, the man was waiting with a question about staff turnover in the Senator's office. Diane paused once before she answered: a small stop, a held note at the glottis and then a release, so that her first word arrived a fraction late and very clean. He took it for thoughtfulness. Everybody does. I had read the file. It was a stutter that a girl had beaten, a long time ago and with nobody's help, into a pause, and the pause had been trained so well it passed for judgement. Her answer gave him nothing and made him feel thanked. I watched him write the nothing down, gratefully.

The young staffer's name was Evan. I know because I watched her say it, through the porthole in the service door beside the press pen, the kind they put in kitchen doors so that the people carrying soup don't kill each other. I couldn't hear a word. I read lips well enough, and the rest of him the way I read a body, by what had been done to it. She said his first name once. She said *badge*, and *desk*. His hands went to his lanyard as if it had got tight. Then she put her hand on his arm, a handshake's length, and said, *Go home. Eat something.* She meant the second thing. That was the decent part, and I want you to keep it, because nobody would have marked her down if she hadn't said it. Thirty seconds, door to door. I have seen slower deaths certified as sudden.

In ninety minutes she was never more than an arm's length from a donor, a staffer or her phone. Not one of them was security. Nobody in that room thought she needed any. When the phone rang, she answered on the first ring, in the middle of a donor's sentence, and the donor beamed as if he had been promoted. She read one text, and as she turned her left wrist the dress watch slid towards the heel of her hand, and where it had been was a band of paler skin, wider than the watch and the wrong shape for it, the shape of a sports strap. Her face did something private for about a second. Then the phone went away and the face came back.

I graded her. I didn't decide to. It came out in the voice I use for the dictaphone. Subject: the Chief of Staff. Donors: distinction. Press: distinction. Personnel: distinction, with a note for the boy and the sandwich she had more or less told him to buy. Decent in nearly every register audible from the back of a ballroom. The failing mark is not a comment on any of this. The failing mark is for what all of this is for. You will be evaluated accordingly.

Fail.

At about ten to ten she left by the side aisle, past the press pen, and as she went she looked along the row of us. She looked at me for exactly as long as she looked at the man beside me. Her eyes passed over me the way you pass over a fire door. Three years ago I had sat across the desk in her own office for forty minutes while I signed. She had uncapped the pen for me, the blue one, and laid it down with the clip turned away from my hand, so that I would have nothing to do but the one thing, and then she had watched my hand the whole time, to make sure it did it.

She registered nothing. I was invisible to these people. It was my advantage and it was the original injury, and I felt both. The first felt like cold air on my forearms. The second came up at the back of my tongue like a coin held there too long, and I didn't swallow it.

* * *

At ten the service lift took me back up, crown first under the dome. The cardboard in the strike had gone soft with the hours and the door had begun to bite through it; one more staffer letting it swing shut and the floor would have closed with me on it. I folded the flap once more on itself and seated it back with my thumb until the latch had nothing to find. The carpet changed underfoot from a pattern designed to hide gravy to a plain charcoal designed to hide nothing. The glass-walled corner suite was dark; everyone who used it was downstairs being thanked. The suite beside it, the one I had watched being propped on my first pass, still stood ajar on its case of water. A campaign that believed nothing was coming had left it that way, and I had banked on it the way you bank on gravity.

It was a bedroom being used as a cupboard: cases of water, rope-line stanchions, lawn signs in shrink-wrap, a bed nobody had slept in, snowed on by merchandise. The air-conditioning ran colder than the ballroom's and went straight for the back of my neck.

I put the gloves on before anything came out of the bag, left and then right, and the snap of the cuffs went into the carpet and stayed there. Then I laid my things on the desk the way I lay out instruments, in the order of use. Two units, bought for cash, wiped and bagged at home under nitrile: each a lens the size of a coat button on a short lead to a body the size of a matchbox. The roll of black vinyl. A cheap tablet, for pairing.

The connecting door had a deadbolt on my side and would have one on theirs. I drew mine back and pressed my fingertips to the edge of the door. A door with its far bolt thrown sits in its frame like a tooth in its socket. This one rocked on its latch the width of a fingernail. Nobody bolts a door against themselves. It is the founding principle of every institution I have ever worked for. The handle went down and the door came towards me. Grade for the campaign, on security: the door everyone walks through and no one checks. They had already written it themselves, on the corridor door, in cardboard.

The War Room smelled of coffee that had stewed through the afternoon, of carpet shampoo, and of the citrus solvent of a whiteboard marker left uncapped on the ledge. It wasn't my red one. I noticed that I checked. Glass on two sides, and below it the city in sodium. The wall screen, asleep at the end of the long table. By the door, a credenza with a coffee urn on it, switched off at its own timer, and beside it a waste bin and a stack of paper cups.

I put the first unit behind the bezel of the wall screen, low on the left, the lens facing the length of the table, its lead into the service port on the back, which stays live because nobody in a hotel turns a screen off at the wall. The tape came off the roll with a sound I hadn't heard from that kit before, a dry, rising rasp, like a zip opened in a library. The second unit went under the lip of the credenza beside the urn, angled up and out, its lead into the urn's socket strip. One short strip each. I pressed them down with the pad of my thumb until they had decided to stay. They would talk out through the hotel's own network like any guest's laptop; the tablet was only there to introduce us. My knuckle grazed the window, and the cold of the glass came through the nitrile. Two minutes. I closed the connecting door without touching anything of theirs, and drew my own bolt home.

I peeled the gloves off inside out into my pocket and sat at the desk with the tablet. The first unit came up and gave me the dark table end-on, like a runway at night. I was pairing the second when the suite door swung in.

My pulse came up into the side of my neck, where I could have counted it without touching anything. I didn't count it.

A night houseman with a cart. About sixty, by the hands and the hairline. A name tag: DESMOND. He had come in because the door was open, which is what doors are for.

I had my left shoe off, and that was true; the heel had blistered on the stair. I turned the tablet face-down on the desk, as if that were simply where I'd put it. His eyes went to it and came off it.

"Sorry," he said. "Door was open."

"Press. They said I could file in here."

"Who said?"

"Evan."

There is an Evan on every campaign floor, and this one had been sent home to eat something. Nobody was going to ask him anything tonight. Desmond nodded as if he knew him.

"Course." He looked at my foot. "That's a blister."

"Stairs."

It was a good lie, and it had the great advantage of a blister in it. The best lies are mostly true and partly bleeding.

He looked at me then, properly, at my face, the way nobody had all night. He opened the first-aid box on his cart and held a plaster out across the corner of the desk.

I reached for it with my left hand, and the hand trembled, once. A single fine oscillation at the fingertips, there and gone. Then it was still, and I took the plaster and said thank you.

He didn't see it. He was looking round for somewhere to put the backing paper. I saw all of it.

I graded him, out of habit and something nearer gratitude. Full marks for looking. Full marks for the plaster. It was the only pass I gave all night.

"I'll leave that wider," he said on his way out, and pushed the door back against its stop with his foot. "For the air."

I had noticed the tremor. I didn't know what to do with it, so I am going to describe it to you instead. Left hand, four fingers, the thumb uninvolved. Amplitude: a millimetre, perhaps two, at the tips. One cycle, quicker than I could have made it on purpose. One cycle is not, strictly, a tremor. I am calling it one. Duration: under a second. Onset as the hand reached for something offered. Resolution spontaneous and complete. Nothing in the right hand. Nothing since. The first since my second year of residency. I told you once that you would know what it meant if they ever shook. I don't. I am only putting it down where you can see it, the way you leave something on a table between two people. You're holding it too, now.

I put the plaster on my heel and pressed its edges down with my thumbnail, and packed the desk right to left, and left the door the way Desmond had, wide, for the air.

In the lift down I held my left hand with my right, two fingers across the wrist the way you hold a patient's to count, until the doors opened. I watched it the whole way, as if it belonged to somebody on my table and I was only there to write down what it did. Under my fingers the forearm was cold, and not the ballroom's cold: Tuesday's, the kind that comes to you, there all night under the other. The walls were brushed steel and gave back nothing but a smear of light. I didn't need them to. I could feel it in the zygomatic muscles, the corners of the mouth drawn up and back and held. I was smiling. I didn't know when that had started.
