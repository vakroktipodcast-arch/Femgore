# Chapter 24

I took the second pair of gloves out of my jacket, the black ones, and put them on over the pale pair I had worn since the service door, left and then right, and snapped each cuff over the wrist. My fingers were cold inside the first pair. The second pair went on over the cold like a lid on a pan that has gone off the boil. In Brandt's suite a room had taken that sound and given none of it back. This room gave it back. It was a converted meeting suite with one lamp and a carpet somebody had shampooed that afternoon, and the snap came off its walls small and domestic, the sound of a woman in a kitchen getting ready to joint something, and behind it, through the wall, the ballroom was playing something with a great deal of bass in it to a crowd that was being kept warm. He watched me do it. He watched the way he must have watched staff do things for twenty years: politely, his hands loose on the arms of the chair, waiting to be told what it was for.

Lesson Three, as written, was simple. Immobilise him. Keep him conscious. Pin the page. Leave him alive, and let eleven women finish him.

I had written that two years ago at my own desk, and since the bathroom floor I had known the arithmetic was wrong. Eleven, less one who had died suddenly at home. Less the one standing in the room.

I came round the end of the dressing table and took his left wrist the way I take a pulse, two fingers and a thumb, and he let me. He held it out the way men hold out a wrist for a watch. I have taken the pulses of fourteen hundred people who couldn't refuse. He was the first who didn't think to. The zip-tie came out of the lining of the blazer flat, and went round his wrist and round the arm of the chair, and was through its own head and drawn tight before his face had caught up with his arm. From where I stood, at his shoulder, I could see the photograph the way he saw it: his brother in dress blues in a plain black frame, propped on its cardboard easel, looking at the chair.

The hard case was in the left inside breast pocket, where it had been all day against the page. I opened it one-handed, the way you open a spectacle case, and took the syringe out capped. It had come from the office's toxicology lab, out of a locked cabinet with a log on its door, and somewhere in that log there was now a line that did not agree with the shelf. Somebody would count. Counting is what offices are for. The count would go into a file, and the file would find its way to a woman who signed her letters E. Reyes, and that had always been part of the lesson: the eleven names in a federal file, and him alive in a hospital bed to be asked about each one of them, slowly, by somebody with a notebook.

I took the cap off with my thumb.

He saw the needle, and he flinched. It was a real flinch. Orbicularis oculi closed hard around both eyes, the whole ring of it, the skin of the lids puckering, and his head went back into the upholstery as far as the chair would let it go, and the tied arm pulled once against the plastic and stopped. He was frightened. I had watched him on a stage and on a feed and at a rope line and at a funeral in the papers, and he had never once been frightened, and nobody had ever made him be.

And there it was, coming up through everything else in the room, through the aftershave, which was cedar and money and had been chosen for him by somebody whose job it was, through the shampooed carpet and the sweet wet-dog breath an extraction machine leaves in a pile for a day afterwards, through the warm dust off the lamp: the sharp first note of the other sweat, apocrine, rising from his axillae into a shirt that had been ironed for television, the same note I had last smelled rising off Brandt on his desk, fear, recent onset, and my mouth filled with water the way a mouth fills at a door with the kitchen on the other side of it, and it was the first thing I had tasted in five chapters, and I am reporting it.

I put the needle into his left deltoid through the sleeve of his shirt, a hand's width below the point of the shoulder, and the muscle took it the way muscle does, a small resistance and then none. I put my thumb on the plunger and pressed.

I had been counting since the green-room door closed. I had counted through my name and through his not knowing it, through the four sentences and through his kindness, seventy-one, seventy-two, and somewhere after that, with my thumb going down and my mouth wet, there was no number. I have counted things my whole working life. Ribs, towels, grams, the paces of a walk across a dam. I had never lost a count. I did not feel it go. I found it gone.

Nobody opened the door. I looked at it once, the main door with the latch he had thrown himself, and it was only a door, and I went back to my thumb.

He didn't shout. Even then his syntax held. He went to the only move there is in a small room, the one he must have made across a thousand tables with donors and with men like Brandt, and he made it with a needle in his arm.

"All right," he said. "All right. What do you want?"

My thumb stopped on the plunger with the barrel part-given. I drew the needle out, and stepped back round the table to my own side of it, and stood there holding the syringe needle-up between us, my thumb off it.

He had asked what I wanted. Not what I was going to do: what I wanted. It was the first question anyone in the campaign had ever asked me, and I didn't have an answer any more.

* * *

It went into him the way I had known it would, from the shoulder outwards. It takes the strength out of a body and leaves the voice where it is, which was the point of it; the lesson had always needed his voice. His left arm went first, the one it was in. The tied wrist turned in the zip-tie with nothing behind it, the fingers opening on the arm of the chair. He looked at the tie. He looked at me. Then he worked it through, visibly, the way I had watched him work a room on a feed, reading the faces from the back to the front, finding the one person who mattered and going to them, and he arrived, by himself, at the only loss in the campaign he was able to feel.

"Is this about Diane?"

His voice broke on her name. I had heard that voice through a wall of loudspeakers in front of thousands, and through a camera the size of a button under a credenza, and in this room telling me he met a great many people, and it had never broken. It broke now in the middle of the word, the vocal folds letting go of the note as if somebody had cut a string, and what came after it came in pieces.

"She was family. I haven't slept since. Her daughter — Olivia — I was at the —"

He didn't finish it. The word was funeral. I had seen the photograph in the papers, him beside the girl, who was taller than her mother had been, both of them looking at something below the edge of the frame. I didn't say it for him.

He wept. I am qualified to tell you what it was, and I will be exact, because it deserves that much. The face went in the way faces go. Corrugator supercilii drew the brows together and up into that peaked, unmistakable shape nobody has ever produced on purpose; the corners of the mouth were pulled down; the chin puckered over mentalis like the skin on cooling milk. The tears came from both eyes at once and ran from the outer corners, which is where they run when the head is back, and his free hand came up, slow with the agent, and covered his face, and the breath went in and out of him behind it wet and uneven, catching at the top. I have stood on the far side of the glass in the viewing room more times than I can count, and I know what is performed and what is not. This was not. It was grief, and it was real, and I was the one person in the building qualified to sign for it.

Through the wall the ballroom stopped its music and somebody on a microphone told them to practise their applause, and they practised it, a long roar that came through the plaster at exactly the wrong moment, and he wept through it.

He gave her back to me in pieces, and every piece was accurate. He said she stopped before certain words and people thought she was choosing them. He said she had told him once, on a plane, about the stammer when she was a girl, and how she had beaten it out of herself. I had known about the stammer. I had it from a dossier, from a profile, from a scope across a reservoir. He had it because she told him.

He said Olivia was a sophomore. He said Wellesley. He said Diane rang her mother every Sunday at four, no matter what. He knew all of it. So did I, every fact, from a lens and a feed and a trade magazine. We had both done the reading.

I looked for myself in it. I looked the way you look for a name on a list, from the top, and then from the top again. He was grieving the woman who had filed me into a cabinet. There was nothing in it for me, and it was not meanness; it was not even forgetting. There was nothing available. And it came to me then, not as an argument, but the way a finding comes at the bench before you know what it means and you write it down anyway: the thing I had spent two years on had never failed to see women. It had seen Diane down to the stammer. It had chosen which ones.

His wife was in the ballroom, on the other side of the applause. He didn't say her name. He said Diane's again.

My left hand had gone to my side. I didn't send it. It was there, through the blazer, through two pairs of gloves, flat on the scar below the ribs, pressing, and it stayed there while he talked. My right hand held the syringe with the needle up and my thumb off the plunger.

"They still don't know who," he said.

They would. They would know from a list of eleven names, and the list would answer the question in its own order, with nobody's grief attached to any line of it, because a list doesn't stand beside anyone at a funeral. Nora Wade had died suddenly at home, in another county, and nobody from the campaign had stood beside anybody at hers.

The syllabus had assumed he would recognise the lesson. Every page of it began there. He was grieving the Gatekeeper. Something in me began, out of fifteen years' habit, to write it up, the reflex going for the pen the way a hand goes for a light switch in a room it knows, and it found no hand to hold the pen with.

Something was running down my face. I noticed it the way I had noticed Brandt's blood, by temperature first: a line of warmth at thirty-seven degrees, coming from the inner corner of my eye and down beside the nose, and then another, and they were mine. It surprised me that I still ran that warm. I had been cold since the bathroom floor. I had not known there was anything left at thirty-seven degrees to come out.

He took his hand away from his face and looked at mine. His own was wet and swollen at the lids and still kind, and he nodded at me, slowly, the agent in his neck, as if I had said something and he agreed with it.

"You knew her," he said. Not a question.

I understood then that he would never be taught. There was no page that reached him. The syllabus shattered from the inside, and I was crying too, and he thought we were grieving the same woman.

* * *

I abandoned the script.

I am telling you plainly, because there isn't another way to tell it. There was no next step. Lesson Three as written went on from here, and I knew every line of what came next, and none of it was addressed to the man in the chair. It was addressed to a man who knew who I was.

I set the syringe down on the dressing table beside the photograph, uncapped, the rest of the agent still in the barrel, so that my right hand was free. I didn't know what I wanted it free for. The black nitrile made a small dry sound against the wood as I let go.

The tape was still in my right outer pocket. I had not touched it all night. Lesson Three kept his eyes open and his mouth open; it was a lesson about being seen and being heard, and the tape had been for the other two, who had needed to be looked at without looking back. I looked at my hands instead. They were black to the wrist, and clean.

I took the page out of the inside pocket. It had been folded in thirds since the morning, when I put it there, and it came out in thirds, and I opened it along its creases with both hands. The creases held. Good paper keeps a fold the way skin keeps a line. It had the weight good paper has, more than you expect from one sheet, and the glove dragged on it a little as I smoothed it, the way a glove drags on dry skin. Nothing on it was marked. I had not circled anything. I am not going to give it to you. You have had two of them. This one stays where it is.

The pin was in my right hand. I had taken it from the same pocket without telling my hand to, the single steel pin, the same as the other two, and I held it between finger and thumb and held the page in my left. Its point pressed through the black nitrile and the pale beneath it and broke neither, and I felt it in the pad of my thumb, the smallest exact pressure in the room, and I looked at his shirt where it would go. Over the top of the sternum, where the skin lies on the bone with almost nothing between them, where I had put the others. Manubrium, I thought. The word arrived, and stood in me, and went no further than that.

There was one other page I had written since the syllabus. It had my own name on it. It was at home, face-down in the drawer under the place where this one had lain. Neither of them had been pinned to anybody.

My hand put the pin back into the inside pocket, beside the capped red marker and the hard case with nothing in it now. I watched it go in. It was not a decision. I would like to say it was. My hand went in under the lapel and came out without the pin, and there was nothing in it, and I looked at that too.

Whatever happened next was not curriculum.

You know what it is before I do.

He was watching me through the wet on his face. The agent sat in him half-finished, heavy in the shoulders and slow in the hands, and his head moved as if it weighed more than it had a minute ago, and he was still the best man in any small room he had ever been in. In this one he had found the person who needed looking after, and it was me. He leaned against the tie as far as it would let him, and the plastic creaked against the arm of the chair, and his breath came slower than it had, the long breaths of a man whose chest has begun to feel heavy and who is too polite to mention it. He was trying to say something to me, in short pieces, the way people speak at a bedside, and what reached me was not the words but the tone, low and level and patient, the voice of a man who has sat with a great many people on the worst day of their lives and knows that what you say matters less than that you stay. He was good at it. He had always been good at it. I was cold in a room that was warm enough for a man in shirtsleeves, and the warmth was coming off him.

I put the page on the table, beside the Marine. Face-down. My right hand stayed on it, flat, the black glove on the white back of the sheet. He didn't look at it. It had his title on the other side, and his eyes stayed on me.

He reached for my hand, to comfort me. His free hand came across the dressing table, slow, past his brother's face, the same right hand he had offered me when I told him my name, and this time it didn't wait to be taken. It covered mine. His fingertips found the gap at my wrist, between the cuff of the glove and the cuff of my sleeve, and rested there. Nothing came into my mouth.

His hand on mine, warm, over the glove and on the one inch of wrist it didn't cover. The rest of the agent on the table beside his brother. The first time in this book I had been touched by someone who didn't know who I was, and it was him.
