# Interlude — Diane

She was home by five past ten, which for a Wednesday was early. The donor dinner had run short.

She did not turn on the overhead light. She used the one over the range and left the rest of the kitchen dark. In the hall she had stepped out of her shoes, one and then the other, each heel pushed off with the toe of the opposite foot. The tile was cool through her tights.

Her phone said sixty-one unread. She turned it face-down on the counter. She had been on since six that morning. Eight weeks and six days. She knew it without counting.

She opened the fridge and stood in its cold breath. Everything in there had a strip of masking tape on it and a date in black marker, in her own hand, so that at least something in her life could be thrown away on schedule. The heel of cheddar said last Thursday. She cut around the dry edge and ate what was under it, standing, with the last of the water crackers, and poured half a glass of white from the open bottle in the door. It had gone flat. The rind tasted of wax.

Then she called Olivia. Not a text. A text could be managed, and tonight she did not want to manage anything.

Four rings. Olivia answered in a whisper, then a fire door sighed shut behind her and the whisper became her ordinary voice, out in the corridor. Two rooms along, someone was playing music.

"Kenzie's asleep," Olivia said. "With the window open. In September. Like a Viking."

It all came out at once. The window open all night, and a fan on. Olivia awake under one blanket, cold. And two nights ago Kenzie had borrowed the good sweater. To sleep in.

Diane sat down for the first time since six, on the step stool by the pantry. She pressed her thumb into the ball of one foot. She listened the way she listened at work, one ear on what was being said and one on what was wanted.

"Vikings didn't borrow sweaters," she said. "They took them. Write that down too."

"Mom."

"I mean it. Write it down."

Buy a second blanket; she would pay. Don't make it a fight. Put on paper what you want and what she wants, and a schedule for the window. If that didn't hold, take it to the RA and get it agreed.

"I don't want a process," Olivia said. "I want her to close the window."

"The process is how she closes the window."

She believed it. It was good advice, and kind, and the same sentence she would have used at the office.

Then Olivia, in a gentler voice: how was she. Was she sleeping.

Diane opened her mouth to say fine. It did not come. There was the old catch from childhood, a beat in which the word stood in the doorway and would not move. Then it moved, and a different word came out from behind it.

"Tired."

Olivia waited through the pause without filling it, as she had since she was small. People at work took the pause for thoughtfulness. Olivia never had.

"Are you still doing the walk?" Olivia said, teasing now. "Do you still wear the watch?"

Diane looked at the dress watch on her wrist, then at the sports watch lying on its back at the end of the counter. Olivia had given it to her three summers ago and set it up on her phone for her.

"Every Sunday," Diane said. Seven o'clock, the reservoir, round the loop. A bench about halfway. Ten minutes there, maybe, with whatever she was reading. Then home. Mother at four.

"Why don't you just sleep in?"

Her reading glasses were still pushed up in her hair, forgotten. For once she did not arrange the answer before she gave it.

"Because it's the one hour of my life I'm not reachable."

Then, because she edited everything, she said it again, tighter. "To not be reachable for one hour of my life. That's all I want. One."

"You answer when I call, though."

"You're the list."

"There's a list?"

"There's you."

On the walk the phone was on Do Not Disturb, and only one number rang through it. Olivia said she'd be home for the long weekend and would walk it with her. Diane said it was not a walk if you talked. Olivia said she would be quiet. Diane said she had never once been quiet, and they both laughed, and Diane told her to go to bed, and to close the window, and Olivia said *you* close the window, and was gone.

The dishwasher ticked through its last cycle.

Diane rinsed the glass and stood it upside down on the drainer. She unbuckled the dress watch. Under it the skin was paler, in a band wider than the watch. She set it down and put the sports watch on its charger. She switched off the light over the range. At the foot of the stairs she touched the alarm panel, and it gave its short tone.

The kitchen was dark except for the watch on its charger at the end of the counter, a small green light going round its face as it filled.
