# Chapter 13

His house was two degrees warmer than mine. I knew it before the door was shut behind me: the air came up against my face already at the temperature of another person's preferences. His heating ran on a timer. Mine is on a dial I turn down in September. I stood inside his back door at twenty past nine on a Sunday night, the pick still in my gloved hand, and let the house tell me about him before I looked at any of it.

Coffee grounds, a day old, gone to forest floor. Rye, faintly, from a bottle on the counter. Fennel and garlic from the day, the anise of the one riding the low sulphur of the other. And nearer the sink, something dry and green and rooty, a cellar somebody keeps well. I had last met it at the height of a standing man's chest, on the brass pull of my own drawer. Here it came off a bar of soap by the tap. I didn't put a name to it. I wanted something left for later.

He was on the thirty-first floor of the Calloway until about midnight, working the Sunday call sheet, which had been Diane's job until Diane stopped having Sundays. There was no alarm and no camera over any door. A man who knows what logs are for keeps none of his own. The deadbolt had taken me four minutes, and afterwards I put the penlight to the lock face: no scratches on the brass, no bright crescents where a pick had slipped. He had come through mine without a mark, and by now I had decided it was a pick. I had come through his the same way. We were even. I didn't give either of us a number for it.

He had come into my flat and touched nothing in the kitchen. I am a better guest. I brought something.

The print was in the inside pocket of my jacket. Eight by ten, glossy: the official scene close-up of Diane's mouth, the fragments crowding at the vermilion border like teeth that had come in wrong, and on the one at her lower lip, under its thin rule, half the matter number. I had printed it from the case file. The system logs every look I take and expects them, because the scene set comes to the reviewer of record as a matter of course. Reviewer: that is what the word is for. I laid the print face-up beside the kettle, squared to the counter's edge. He had looked at a copy of this on his phone for two minutes and eleven seconds. A man ought to see his work in the field.

Then I went through his house.

The sodium came through the blinds in bars, the colour of weak marmalade, and I kept a penlight with a darkroom-red lens low, at my knee. I didn't need to touch anything. I touched everything, as if I were choosing him a present and wanted very much to get it right. The charcoal suits in the wardrobe, each sleeve rubbed once between finger and thumb: worsted, a close twist that holds a crease without being asked. His reading glasses, one arm mended with clear tape. Not my tape. Two pillows. The near one dented and soft and faintly rancid with the oil of a scalp; the far one firm and cool. It had not been slept on in two years. You can tell, the way you can tell a lung that has never been smoked through: it is simply too pleased with itself.

The front room was the records. The piano was there too, a black shape at the far end with its lid down, and I didn't go to it, and I knew where it was the whole time I was in that room. The records ran the length of one wall, spines out, alphabetical by artist and then by the date of the recording. Coltrane. Dolphy. Mingus. Yards of names I had never heard. Cardboard, and the faint vanilla of ageing sleeves, which is lignin breaking down, which is a library dying contentedly.

The first unit, the War Room model, went on the top shelf, angled down over the turntable, the drinks tray and the piano, with one strip of black vinyl to hold it and its lead run down behind the spines to the turntable's socket strip. It would go out through his own router, on the network key from the first Lantern card. The tape coming off the roll was the loudest sound I made in that house, a small dry tearing, and in his warm front room it sounded like somebody clearing their throat at a funeral.

Then I chose, by the label, like wine in a language I can't read, and the Dolphy I chose had a clock on it with too many hands, and a sign under the clock that said WILL BE BACK. *Out to Lunch!* I liked the joke. I drew the disc out and got it wrong at once, flat between both palms like a plate out of a hot oven, the leather across the grooves; everything else in that room told me it was wrong. It doesn't matter how you hold one you are about to break.

The second unit went on top of the bedroom wardrobe, facing the bed, its lead down the back of the wardrobe to the socket behind it. I had to go up on my toes, and the wound on my left flank pulled once under my shirt, a short hot tug along the stitch line like a hem catching on a nail.

Then the break, which I did with my trade and not my strength. Anybody can snap a record. I wanted it to come apart where I said. I laid the disc on his chest of drawers and scored it with the scalpel across its whole diameter, through the label, one steady pass. Then I set the score along the edge and pressed down on the overhang. Vinyl breaks along a score the way a bone breaks along a fracture somebody has already started. One dry crack, like a knuckle, and two clean halves. My pulse was up in the pads of my fingers. I laid the halves on his pillow, the used one, a hairline apart, the split running through the middle of the label, and the sleeve beside them with its clock face up.

Then the licence. I had taken it off his warehouse floor two nights before and read it at my own table on the Saturday. Dale Garrity. Forty-three. A heavy, bearded man looking at the licensing office's camera as if it had asked him something personal. In the corner, a small heart, pillar-box red. He was an organ donor. Nobody had asked me.

I laid it beside the halves. It was an invoice. He had sent two men to take me without damaging me, which is a brief written by somebody who has never had to carry one out, and this was what it had cost, and he should see the bill, itemised, with a photograph. My fingertips had gone to my side while I thought it, through the shirt, to the place two days old under my own stitches. My face was warm. Then I reached out and slid the licence a quarter of an inch to the left.

I didn't say why. There was nobody to say it to.

* * *

At ten past ten I was back in the kitchen, and everything was placed. At work, when the last pot is labelled, you strip your gloves and leave the suite; anyone still in there after that is there for herself. He would be home at about midnight. I stood with my back to his counter, beside the print, and did not leave. I am reporting it.

I opened his fridge. The light came out over everything at once, white and total, and I read his shelves like an opened abdomen, everything in its place and telling me what it was for. Good butter. A lemon. A jar of cornichons. Eggs. Half a bottle of white with a stopper in it. And front and centre, where a man puts the thing he means to come back to, a lidded enamel dish. My mouth watered before I had the lid off. I had eaten a late lunch and nothing since. Then I shut the fridge on purpose, with the dish in my hands, to have the dark back.

Pork shoulder. Braised that morning with the fennel and the garlic, set cold in its own jelly, one end already carved, a few slices gone at lunch. It was far too much pork for one man. He cooked the way people cook who used to cook for two: too much, and well. The recipe had outlived the marriage.

I took off my right glove.

I decided it, and I did the sum first. My DNA is on the office's elimination register like every pathologist's, and he would wash me off his dish himself by morning. I peeled the leather off and pocketed it, and stood in a man's kitchen in the dark with a bare hand, and it was the first time in my life I had bared a hand anywhere I should not have been.

Breaking and entering is a felony. I have never been able to find out what the charge is for eating a man's pork.

I ate standing up, by touch, from the end he had already broached, so that whatever I took would come off his own cut. I ate as I palpate, with my eyes shut, by knowing where things were. The jelly first. Cold on the fingertip and cold on the tongue and then not cold, because jelly from a braise is collagen given back as gelatin, and gelatin lets go a little under the heat of a mouth; it melted at about thirty-five degrees, which is my tongue, and went from solid to gravy without passing through anything in between. Then the meat. The fibres of a shoulder braised long enough part along their own grain, the way muscle parts on the table once the fascia is open and it has been told, at last, that it may. A fennel seed cracked between my molars, sweet and then camphor, the cold anise going up the back of my nose. His hand with the salt was heavier than mine. And at the edge, where the crust had caught in his oven, a bitterness like burnt sugar, which was the best of it, which is always the best of it, which is why you never let anyone else carve. Distinction. I docked nothing for the salt.

About a hundred and fifty grams, two slices' worth and the jelly round them, about a kidney's weight. I'm giving you the number because you'll want it. It was not the meal after. Nobody was dead tonight. I didn't have a word for the other kind.

Then I tidied. With one bare fingertip I smoothed the cut face of the pork and drew the skin of the jelly back over the place I had taken from, the way you bring a wound edge to edge so that it heals flat, and wiped the rim of the dish with a square of his paper towel, and put the dish back in its place, and shut the door on its light. A burglar takes something. I smoothed the jelly. He was not going to know, and I meant him not to. Everything else in that house I had left for him to find, and billed. This was the one thing I kept off the invoice.

I turned his tap with my gloved wrist and washed my hand with his soap. That was a decision too. The bar was worn to a lozenge, and his first wash would take me off it. The lather came up thin and dry and smelling of the cellar, and I worked it into the webs of the fingers as I scrub at work, and dried my hand on the same square of towel and pocketed it, and put the glove back on over the smell of him. My right hand would smell of him for hours. I knew it, and didn't wash again.

The piano was in the next room, and this time I went to it.

A black baby grand, lid down, fallboard closed. I lifted the fallboard. It came up without a sound, which is the sound a thing makes when somebody looks after it, and in the sodium the keys were the colour of old teeth. They were ivory. A tusk is a tooth, and I have spent fifteen years looking at what people smile with. I have never had a piano lesson. His hands know where the notes are in the dark. Mine know where the aorta is.

I know one note. Everybody in an orchestra tunes to it, so it seemed the polite one to ask: the A above middle C. I counted up to it the way you count down from the angle of Louis to find a rib, and pressed it with one gloved fingertip.

It went out into the warm house like a drop of ink into water. I can't name a pitch by ear. But I can tell a clean note from a sour one, as I can tell a clean cut from a ragged one without measuring either. A sour note has a slow wobble in it, a beating, where the three strings that strike together aren't quite agreeing. There was no wobble. Three strings, one note, nothing between them. He kept it true for nobody, in a house with nobody in it.

I lifted my finger and the damper came down, and the room held the note a moment longer than the strings did, and I closed the fallboard over it while it was still sounding, and left the room before I could play more.

* * *

At twenty-five to eleven I stood in the bedroom doorway with the page in my hand. Lesson Two, a fresh print, made at my desk the night before. The master stays in the drawer with *Excellent.* in red in its margin, in my hand. That one is for me. You don't return a student's script with the grade on it before he has sat the paper. It only makes him careless.

I folded it in quarters, ran my thumbnail down each crease and laid it on the pillow beside the halves and the licence. I had known what it said for two years.

I did not stay to watch him find it. I went out through the kitchen, past the print and the fridge and what the fridge knew, and crouched on his step and relocked the deadbolt with the pick, which goes faster than unlocking it, because the pins have already told you everything they know. At ten to eleven I was walking down his lane with nothing of his on me but the smell. In the car I took the gloves off. My right hand, on the wheel, smelled of his soap. I own no scented products. For once in my life I smelled of something, and it was his.

I drove three miles to the hospital, where there is a multi-storey that never shuts, took a ticket at the barrier that I would pay in cash at the machine, and parked on the top deck by the parapet, facing the city, under the sodium deck lights. A hospital car park is the only place in a city where a woman can sit alone in a dark car at one in the morning and everyone who sees her assumes she has a good reason. I did. The engine ticked as it cooled. Cold vinyl, old coffee. I propped the tablet against the wheel, put it on the hospital's open guest Wi-Fi, and brought up both feeds, front room and bedroom, dark, hissing a little, listening to his rooms. The cold came into the car and I let it. It wasn't where I was keeping the warmth.

At twenty-six minutes past twelve the front-room feed heard his front door. He crossed the frame in charcoal with his collar up, as he comes out of the Tabard in the photograph in my drawer, and went out of the far side of it towards the kitchen, where there was no camera. The audio gave me the fridge's hum through the wall, and then, for a long time, nothing. I knew what he was looking at. I listened for the seal of the fridge, that small wet unpeeling, and didn't hear it. That is not the same as it not happening. The dish was in its place either way.

Then the bedroom. The lamp came on beside the bed, low and yellow, and he came in and saw the pillow and stopped, and sat down on the edge of the bed on his own side. He picked up the two halves, one in each hand, and put them together along the break, the way you try a fracture for fit: the ends offered to each other, a small press, a small turn, until they seat. Then he sat there holding them. He sat there a long time.

I did not time it.

I noticed afterwards that I hadn't. I timed his stillness in the War Room to the second. I timed Diane on her bench to the minute, Sunday after Sunday. I time my eggs. I do not know how long he sat on the edge of his bed holding my break together in his two hands. It was the first thing on any feed I had failed to measure.

He put on the glasses with the taped arm and picked up the licence. He read the name and the age, and his face did nothing at all: a man reading a stranger's name off a card. I could watch him not know it. He looked longer at the folded page, then unfolded it and read it once, slowly. It takes under a minute; I know, because I wrote it. He folded it again along my creases and put it in his inside breast pocket, on the left, and slid the halves and the licence into the Dolphy sleeve.

Then he laughed.

A real laugh, a surprised one, the kind that gets out before the person has been consulted. His head went back with it, and it was loud enough that the cheap microphone on the wardrobe clipped it, sheared the top off it into a burst of white noise. I had never heard him laugh. Not once in five weeks of feed. My pulse went up, and I counted it at the left wrist with two fingers of the right, the hand that smelled of him. Ninety-two, against a resting sixty-four. That one I counted.

He went into the front room, into the other frame, and put the sleeve back in its place on the wall, and at twenty to one he poured a drink from the tray by the turntable, rye, neat, two fingers. Then he chose a record. Not the broken one. *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady.* He lowered the needle on side one and sat down in the chair by the piano with the glass on his knee and did not touch the keys.

The first time I heard that record, in September, through the clean laptop, I turned it off after a few minutes. It was like having a menu read to you when you're hungry. It sounded no better now: three miles through a button, thin, a big band heard through a wall. I didn't turn it off. I sat in the dark with the city in the windscreen and listened to the whole side with him, about twenty minutes of it, the first whole side I had ever heard through, and once, without deciding to, I lifted my right hand to my face. I am putting it down as a finding. It still smelled of his soap.

At the end of side one the tone arm lifted and went home by itself, and there were two silent rooms three miles apart, and neither of us got up to turn it over.
