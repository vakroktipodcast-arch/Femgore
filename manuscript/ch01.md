# Chapter 1

*Lesson One. Subject: The Gatekeeper. Function: to convert the complaint into the file, the file into the folder, the folder into the cabinet, the cabinet into the room no one enters. You have performed this function with distinction. Your failing mark is therefore not a function of incompetence. You confused process for protection. You will be evaluated accordingly.*

"Liver," I said, and lifted it out, and for a moment I didn't put it on the scale. I held it. You are allowed to hold it. Nobody writes that in the manual, but nobody writes in the manual that you can't, and I have always read the manual the way a lawyer reads a contract, for what it forgot to forbid. It lay across both my palms and over the heels of my hands, smooth and cool and slippery as a wet stone from a river, with the capsule drawn taut over it like the skin on a drum, and it had that particular heft that nothing else in the body has, a dense, settled, obliging weight, the weight of a thing that has been doing a great deal of work quietly for a long time and would like, now, to be thanked. Then I put it on the scale, and the scale thought about it, and said eighteen hundred and forty grams.

"Heavy," Mara said.

"Fatty. Look at the edge."

She looked. Mara Lin watches me work the way people watch a chef through the pass, though in fairness to chefs, they rarely have to weigh the customer first. When I turned the liver on the board and cut it, one long stroke, the surface that came up was the colour of putty and old butter, and greasy under the blade, and the smell of it rose to meet the rest of the room.

Smell is first. It always is with me. The suite under the extraction fans smelled of cold iron and chlorhexidine and a faint sweetness I could pick out of a crowd, and under that, the low faecal note that a decedent's colon contributes to every conversation and which I stopped minding in my first year and have never once stopped naming. I like the smell of the suite. It is the smell of things that are exactly what they are.

She had come in early that morning, the woman on my table, from a bus shelter, where she had been sitting upright with her bag on her knees and her eyes open, and had been sitting that way, the driver reckoned, for most of a morning before anyone thought to ask. No identification. A woman in her sixties, small, with good teeth for her age and bad feet. Around her left wrist, a band of skin paler than the rest, a centimetre wide, where a hospital bracelet had been and had been cut off long enough ago for the skin to start forgetting it, and not long enough for it to finish. I read her from the outside before I read her from the inside. That is the order.

"And the heart was what?" Mara said.

"Five-twenty."

"That's big."

"Hypertensive. Left ventricle's two centimetres. She was pushing against a closed door for years."

"Would she have known?"

"Headaches. Maybe."

That is about how I talk. On the page I am a different animal; make allowances, the way you would for a woman who is quiet at dinner and then writes you a twelve-page letter. Inside I was thinking about the heart, which had been sitting on the board to my left for twenty minutes, thick-walled and stubborn, a fist that had been clenched so long it had forgotten how to open, and about how muscle answers resistance by growing, the way a hand gets a callus, until the very thing it built to cope becomes the thing that kills it. Five hundred and twenty grams. I had held that too. I hadn't mentioned it to Mara.

Through the open door of the office, past the coat stand, the break-room television was on with the sound down. A map in two colours. A bar chart, his bar the longer one. A man in a navy suit, smiling at the camera with his whole face. MARCHETTI, it said, and underneath, in the ticker, the anchor's line, which I could read without hearing: *his to lose.*

"Debate's tonight," Mara said, because she had followed my eyes. "Are you going to watch?"

"Might."

I said it the way you'd say you might take an umbrella.

The brain came last. It's the dessert. It had come out heavy and soft and pale as a peeled egg, and I took it to the slicing board and steadied it with the flat of my left hand and made a single stroke through it with the long knife, front to back, one pull, no sawing. It opened like a book on a stand. I want to tell you how small that satisfaction is, so that you don't make it bigger than it is. It is the satisfaction of a door that closes without being slammed. It is the click a good pen makes. I did not call it joy. I called it a good day's work, early.

There was a small bleed in the left basal ganglia, the size of a raisin, the colour of oxblood against the cream. "There," I said. And there she was: blood pressure, untreated, a long time, and one afternoon in a bus shelter the door finally gave. I showed Mara. Mara wrote it down.

We closed her up together. I labelled the specimen pots and taped the labels with surgical tape, three strips each, pressing them down with my thumbnail because I like the sound the tape makes when it has properly decided to stay. My hands were steady. They are always steady. They have not shaken since my second year of residency, and I mention it now so you'll know what it means later if they do.

Mara stripped her gloves off inside out, the way I'd taught her, and dropped them in the bin, and said, without looking up, quietly, as if she were telling me the time: "You work like you're taking apart something you built."

I didn't say anything. I let it go past me, the way you let a car go past before you cross.

I liked hearing it.

* * *

The park at noon was hot enough to lean on, and the air had the consistency of soup. I had spent the morning with my hands in things that come out of the fridge at four degrees, by choice, and the heat went for the back of my neck before I had reached the gate.

Hannah had the blanket out under a plane tree, the big one by the sprinklers, with the cool bag and the girls. The girls were wet. They had been in the sprinklers in their clothes, and June was still in them, running at the jets and away from them shrieking, as if each one were a personal betrayal, and Astrid, who is eight and has a sense of her own dignity, was sitting at the edge of the blanket in a towel watching her sister with the patience of a lifeguard.

"Ris." Hannah got up and hugged me, and held on, and then held me off and looked at me with her nose wrinkled. "You smell like a hospital."

"I was at work."

"You're always at work."

I didn't correct her about the hospital. The suite was still on my skin under the soap; it always is, and I have never particularly tried to get it off. It is easier to let her call it a hospital. People like the word *hospital*. It has hope in it.

She had made sandwiches. Roast chicken with tarragon mayonnaise on soft white bread, cut into triangles for everyone, because Hannah believes, rightly, that nobody has ever been sad holding a triangle. She unpacked them in their greaseproof paper onto the blanket, and I took one and ate it, and then I took another and ate that.

They were very good. I want to be exact. The chicken was from Sunday, so it had been in the fridge two days and had firmed up and gone sweeter, the way roast chicken does, and the tarragon had got into the mayonnaise overnight and turned it faintly green and anise-sharp, and the bread had gone soft where it touched the ice pack, and the whole thing was salt and fat and liquorice and the specific comfort of white bread, which is the comfort of being a child, which nobody should be allowed to buy as an adult and everybody should. I ate the second one faster than the first.

Hannah ate half of one. She ate the way mothers eat, half a sandwich and the edges of other people's, and when June came back dripping and wanted a triangle and then didn't want the crusts, Hannah ate the crusts without seeming to notice she was doing it, as if she were tidying.

I noticed the ratio. Two sandwiches to her half. I put it down to having skipped breakfast, which I had, and which was true, and which is the kind of true that is very convenient.

June had a sticker on her T-shirt, round and navy and gold, MARCHETTI in block capitals across it, gone wavy at the edges from the sprinkler. "They were giving them out at the library," Hannah said. "She wanted one. I quite like him, actually. The veterans thing seems real. His brother."

"Mm," I said.

June sat down in my lap without asking, the way small children do, as if you were furniture that had been placed there for them, and the sticker had begun to lift at one edge, so I peeled it back, carefully, a centimetre, and smoothed it down again with my thumb, and pressed until it had properly decided to stay. Nobody looked at my thumb. Why would they.

Hannah started on me then, gently, from the side, the way you approach a horse. Was I sleeping. Had I taken any holiday this year, because she happened to know I hadn't taken any last year. Was it good for me, really, being down there all the time with nobody but the, she lowered her voice for the children, *you know*. I answered everything. That is the trick. You never refuse a question; refusing is information. You answer every question warmly, a little too fully, with a funny story at the end, and the funny story is the door you show them out through. I told her the extraction fans are so loud that the pathologist before me used to sing under them, and nobody knew for years. I told her I'd take a week in October. I won't. She laughed. I was very good. I want you to see how good, because you will need to know later what I am like when I'm being good.

Astrid had been listening, pretending to look at the sprinklers. "What do you actually do," she said. "All day."

"I find out why people stopped," I said.

Astrid considered this. She nodded, as if it were a reasonable job. Hannah gave me a look over her head, which I received and filed.

There was a third sandwich in the cool bag. I could see it through the gap in the lid, a triangle and its other half, still in the paper. I wanted it. Not very much, and not in any way I'd have mentioned, but specifically, in the stomach, the way you want the next thing when the first two were good. I didn't take it. I'm telling you because it was a fact about lunch, and I tell you the facts about lunch.

"So," Hannah said, and leaned back on her elbows, and squinted at me through the leaves, and I knew from the *so* exactly what was coming, the way you know from the first bar. "Are you seeing anyone?"

"Sort of," I said. "Early days. He works weekends."

That's all a lie needs. Three facts, one of which forecloses the next question, and none of which gives her anything to look up. No name, because a name is a thing people repeat to their husbands. A smile on *early days*, slightly shy, slightly pleased, so she'd know to leave it alone out of tact, and she did, and she squeezed my ankle, and said *good, Ris, good*, and *call me on Sunday*, and meant both, which was the only part I minded.

I'd give it a B-plus. It lost marks for compassion. Compassion is the only thing that ever gives a good lie away; you can always hear it, a little give in the voice, the liar sparing you something. A better lie would have been less kind. I am telling you this so that you'll know I can tell the difference.

And I wasn't, strictly, lying about the seeing. Strictly, I was seeing three people. I had photographs.

* * *

I watched the debate in the dark, on the tablet, propped against the fruit bowl on the kitchen counter, with the blue of the screen the only light in the flat. I had not eaten. I was grading them on structure, which is the only fair way to grade a debate, since content is a matter of opinion and structure is a matter of anatomy. Televised debates are autopsies performed by people who have never been told where anything is.

He was better. I'll say that plainly, because I am fair. He was better on syntax. The other man used sentences the way you use a ladder you don't trust, testing each rung before he put his weight on it, and you could hear him doing it. Holt Marchetti didn't test anything. His sentences went up and came down and landed where he'd meant them to land, every time, with a little room to spare, the way a good surgeon's hands are never quite touching the thing they are about to touch. I gave him a distinction for syntax.

They asked him about veterans. He talked about Mark. He didn't do it the way they usually do it, with a pause and a hand on the lectern; he did it quickly and almost apologetically, as if he knew you'd heard it before and was sorry to make you hear it again, but it was the only true thing he knew about the subject and he wasn't going to pretend to know another one. His brother. 2003. It was sincere. It worked. I watched it work on the audience, a long soft exhalation through the hall, and I gave him full marks for it, which is the worst grade I know how to give.

And then, at the end of the answer, while the moderator was turning to the other man, he did it.

He reached across with his right hand and took his left cufflink between his thumb and forefinger and gave it a quarter-turn. A small, neat adjustment, as if it had come loose, which it hadn't. His eyes went down to it. Two seconds. Then they came up again, and he was listening to the other man with his whole face, and it was over.

It looked smaller on a tablet.

The cold began in my forearms. I want to report it accurately, because it was not a figure of speech; it was a finding. It began in the skin of the forearms, on the volar surface, as if I had put them into a sink of water left standing overnight, and it moved down into the backs of my hands, which were on my knees, and settled there, and then it came up the sides of my neck and into the hinge of my jaw, which went tight and stayed tight. It was not the suite's cold. The suite's cold is a cold I choose. I put my coat on the hook and walk into it every morning the way you walk into the sea. This was the other kind. This was the kind that comes to you.

I did not move for an hour.

I know it was an hour because the tablet told me. The debate ended. A panel came on and talked about the debate, and a woman said *his to lose*, and the panel ended, and something else began. I have sat still for longer, but on those occasions the other party was dead, and it was their stillness we were documenting. This was mine. I documented it. Body upright on a kitchen stool. Hands on knees, palms down, fingers slightly flexed. No voluntary movement observed over a period of approximately sixty minutes. Skin of forearms and dorsum of hands cool to the touch. Jaw held. Eyes open.

Then I got up.

The writing desk is the only thing in the flat I paid too much for. It's walnut, and old, and the drawer under it locks with a small brass key I keep on the ring with my car key, so that every time I drive anywhere I am carrying it, which I have always thought was the point. I unlocked it. It slid out on its runners with the sound a good drawer makes, a long wooden sigh.

The three dossiers were on top. Pell. Brandt. Marchetti. Manila, with the string and the button, because I don't trust anything I can't hold in my hands and set fire to. Under them, the photographs. Under the photographs, my own signed copy of the agreement, three years old, my signature on the last page in the blue ink they gave me, rounder than my signature is now. And under that, in a clear sleeve, three printed pages, two years old, with a title page I had typed and then, a week later, felt embarrassed by, and then, a week after that, stopped feeling embarrassed by.

THE BUTCHER'S SYLLABUS.

The paper had gone soft at the corners. That happens to paper that is handled and not used. I took out the first page and stood in the blue light and read Lesson One aloud, all of it, to the flat, which had nobody else in it, and I heard what my voice did to it.

It didn't sound like a woman reading a document. It sounded like a judgement. It sounded like me. *You will be evaluated accordingly.* I had written that two years ago at this desk, at about this time of night, and I had never once heard it said out loud, and it turned out that I sounded, saying it, exactly like what I was, which was someone who had been marking things for years and had finally been handed the right paper.

The kit was in the back compartment, behind a panel that comes away if you press it in the right place. I took it out and laid it on the desk in a row, the way I lay out instruments, left to right in the order of use. The marker first. I uncapped it to check it, and the solvent smell came up, sharp and sweet and chemical, the smell of a classroom whiteboard on the first day of term, and I capped it again. Then the tape, black vinyl, a full roll, with that dull sheen it has, like the back of a beetle. Then the zip-ties, a bundle of them, and I drew one through its own head to test the ratchet, and it went with that small rapid sound they make, a sound like a watch being wound very fast, and held. The red marker, the black tape, the zip-ties. I looked at them for a while.

I was hungry.

Not in any complicated way. Plainly, in the stomach, all at once, the way you are hungry when you come in out of the cold and smell something cooking in someone else's house. I noticed it the way I'd note it on a chart: no food since the sandwiches, nine hours, blood glucose presumably falling, an entirely ordinary finding. It was physiology. I want you to have that the way I had it.

I didn't eat. I put the three things in the pocket of my coat, and put the coat on, and turned off nothing.

I left the tablet on. When I closed the door behind me he was still talking, in the dark, to a room with nobody in it, and I had decided to stop being one.
