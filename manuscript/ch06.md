# Chapter 6

*Lesson One. Subject: The Gatekeeper. Function: to convert the complaint into the file, the file into the folder, the folder into the cabinet, the cabinet into the room no one enters. You have performed this function with distinction. Your failing mark is therefore not a function of incompetence. You confused process for protection. You will be evaluated accordingly.*

The pump station smelled of what it had been built to do and had stopped doing. Damp concrete, the cellar breath of it coming up through the floor. Machine oil gone to varnish in the seals of the old pump, sweetish, like the inside of a grandfather's toolbox. Rust off the iron pipework, which is iron oxide, which is a cousin at a couple of removes of what I smell at work every day, and which I found I liked having in the room. And Diane. A floral drugstore shampoo, freesia, or something that had been allowed to use freesia's name. The clean sweat of a Sunday walk, dried on her by now and being replaced, from the axillae outward, by the other sweat. The waxed cotton of her jacket, which smells like a wet dog that went to a good school.

I had turned the lantern on so that she could see me. It stood on the concrete between us, a battery LED, blue-white, the light of a fridge opened at night, and it threw both our shadows up the curved brick so that there were four of us in the room and two of them were very tall. Diane was on the floor with her back against the pump housing, knees drawn up, ankles bound, wrists behind her: one zip-tie per wrist, each joined to the iron bracket at the base of the housing. Zip-ties are designed by people who never have to take one off, which is how you know they work. Her wrists were already pink at the edges of the plastic. Beside the lantern lay her phone in its foil sleeve, wrapped like a leftover.

I was in a white disposable coverall with the hood up and two pairs of nitrile gloves, and I looked like a crime scene that had arrived early. My hands were cold inside the gloves. They were steady. I noted that they were steady and did nothing with the information. My pulse was still up from the door, the knee, the tape; I could feel it in the hinge of my jaw. Somewhere in the dark a drip fell into something, slowly, as if it were paid by the drop.

"I'm going to explain the course," I said.

Earlier, in the oxblood of the head-torch, she had seen a light and two hands. Now she had a face, and I watched her open a file on it.

I explained it the way the convenor does on the first morning, standing at the front with the handbook, to a room that has not yet worked out which of them will fail. Three pages. Three subjects. Three evaluations. She was the first. The grade was not negotiable, and it was not pending. It had been given two years ago. Today was only when it was handed back.

She listened the way compliance directors listen: as if she would be asked to initial it later. When I stopped she waited a full second to be certain I had stopped, which is a courtesy you learn in rooms where interrupting costs money.

"If this is about a complaint," she said, "there are channels."

"I know. I used them."

I was pleased with that. I hadn't planned it. Five words, and the whole case for the prosecution in them, and she would have to sit with them on the floor, because there was nowhere else for her to sit.

She was quiet. Then, carefully, choosing each word as though it came out of an account she intended to keep in credit: "Is anyone near Olivia?"

"No one is near Olivia."

It was true. I said it quickly, and heard myself say it quickly, and graded the speed as a kindness, and took a mark off for it. Nobody had asked me to be kind. It wasn't on the paper.

Then I gave her a number. I didn't need notes. "LC-0311."

It is in the footer of every page of the campaign's nondisclosure agreement, under a thin rule, the same on every page of every one. Counsel's matter number. A gatekeeper knows the numbers on her own gates; she had assigned that one, and she would have known it the way you know your own postcode, without ever once having had to look. Her eyes went to the number first. I watched them do it, a small movement up and to the left, a woman reading a footer off the inside of her own skull. Then they came down, in the lantern, to my face.

She made a sound. It was small and it came from the back of the throat, glottal, involuntary, the larynx closing on something it had not been told to swallow. I know that sound. I have heard it down the telephone from mothers and from husbands at the moment my sentence reaches its noun. I had never been the noun before.

She looked at me for a long time. I let her. At the fundraiser her eyes had passed over me the way you pass over a fire door. In three years nobody from the campaign had looked at me and had anything happen behind the face, and now something was happening behind hers, slowly, like a building being lit floor by floor from the inside, and I had waited long enough for it that I could wait through it.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm so sorry. I should have protected you from that."

She said it quietly, and fluently, and she meant it. That was the thing. She was not apologising for the agreement, or the file, or the folder, or the cabinet, or the room no one enters. She was apologising for the thing the file was about. She was sorry she had not kept me safe from it.

It was the worst thing she could have said. She still believed the file had been the protection and only the protecting had failed, as though the cabinet were a good cabinet someone had forgotten to lock. She had just read me her own evaluation in her own words, and she had done it kindly, and I had no column for her kindness. I watched her not know why. For one line I was not amused by anything, and a taste came up at the back of my tongue like a coin held there too long, and then the line was over.

* * *

I am going to describe this properly. I am the only person qualified to, and you came for this.

The black vinyl first. I tore two strips and the roll gave them up with a dull, almost polite unpeeling, the sound of someone getting up from a leather chair. Temple to temple, the first over the brows and the upper lids, the second a little lower, overlapping, pressed down along the zygomatic arches with my thumbs. Her eyelashes moved against the adhesive once, a small trapped flutter under my thumb like a moth in a cupped hand, and then they stopped. She was in the dark. Sensory deprivation lowers the startle threshold; everything after that would arrive as an ambush. That was the design.

The paper was in a clear zip bag. Photocopies of pages one to eight of my own campaign agreement, no signature page, cross-cut at my kitchen table into confetti the size of fingernail parings. My signed original stayed in the drawer at home, where it had always been. I held the bag up in front of her blindfold, for form's sake. It was the first time anyone at the campaign had been asked to swallow one.

Her mouth was open. I steadied her head with the fingertips of my left hand, one either side of the larynx, at the borders of the thyroid cartilage, where the head can be held still without any argument from the neck. With two gloved fingers of my right I packed the paper in past the incisors and over the dorsum of the tongue, and seated the bolus back towards the oropharynx with the pressure I would give a laryngoscope blade, so that the gag reflex fired once, early, and was spent. Then the paper did what I had watched it do at my counter the week before, a pinch of it in a glass of water and me pressing it with the back of a spoon like a woman checking whether a cake was done. Dry, it was card. It drank her saliva weight for weight, and under my fingertip it went from card to felt to something like the skin on a pan of porridge. It smelled of toner as it wet, warm and faintly electrical, the smell of an office at the end of a long day.

Then the industrial tape. Industrial tape cannot be unrolled discreetly. It is the only tool in the kit that announces itself, a shriek coming off the roll that in that room went up the brick and stayed there. I tipped her head forward with my palm on the occiput and went round: three turns, over the mouth and both nostrils and round the back of the skull, pewter, pulled snug. On the second turn it caught the fine hair at her nape. I felt it catch, and noted it, and did not solve it. I had a lot on.

My watch said 10:40.

First, nothing. She held still. Twenty seconds. Twenty-five. Thirty. It was discipline, the same muscle that had once taken a stammering child and made of her a woman whose pauses passed for thought, and I admired it out loud.

"That's very good," I said.

Then the body overruled her. The first involuntary inspiration came against the closed airway, and the diaphragm pulled down on nothing, and the tape dimpled inward over the nares in two small soft pits, and the suprasternal notch drew in above the collar of her shirt, and the intercostal spaces sank between the ribs as though each rib were being tugged on a string from the inside. It isn't the lack of oxygen that does it. Oxygen falls quietly; you could faint of it at altitude and never notice. It is the carbon dioxide, building in the blood, that the brainstem cannot tolerate. The body is not afraid of having no air. It is afraid of its own exhaust. I can think of an institution with the same reflex, and so can you.

Then the struggle. The zip-ties bit and knocked against the iron bracket, plastic on metal, a sound like someone trying a locked gate. Her heels went against the concrete together, scraping first, then drumming, the bound ankles lifting and coming down. A hum came up through the tape, a voice with nowhere to go, and went round the room and came back. Behind her back her fingers opened and closed on nothing. I checked each phase against my watch the way I would check a registrar's timings against the book, and each phase came when the book said it would, and the book was right.

Then the convulsion, the whole of her going rigid against the housing and shaking, and then the loss of consciousness, which you can see happen in the hands: they stop meaning anything. Then the bladder. Warm ammonia rose into the cold air, expected, logged. Then the agonal gasps, spaced further and further apart, each one arriving after the last like someone remembering something halfway through a sentence and then losing it again. Above the pewter band her face was going dusky, plum going to slate. I didn't need to see her eyes. I knew what was blooming in the conjunctivae under the black, the petechiae coming up like pinpricks of ink through blotting paper. I have opened this from the inside dozens of times. The lungs heavy and plum-dark and wet. The spots on the visceral pleura. The right side of the heart engorged, standing up like a fist that has been holding something too long. I had always come in at the end of this. I was watching the outside of it now, from the beginning, in order.

I put two fingers on her carotid and counted it down. It slowed and thinned and went irregular, a few beats and a gap and a few beats, and I counted the gaps as well, and then there was nothing to count, and I kept my fingers there anyway until I was sure, and it was 10:47.

Then the quiet, which I want to tell you about properly, because it was the quietest place I have ever been: the drip somewhere in the dark keeping its own slow time, the faint electrical whine of the lantern that I had not been able to hear over her, my own pulse loud inside the hood, a hundred and something, banging in my ears and in my jaw and in the soft tissue of my throat, my face hot, actually hot, in a room at eleven degrees, the first warm part of me all morning, and all of it arriving at once and staying, the way the sound had stayed, as if the room had been built to keep it for me. I am reporting that. It goes in the same column as the time.

I have left something out. After the black went over her eyes, and before the paper went in, Diane began to say a word. The old hesitation caught it on its first syllable: once, a stress and a fall, the pause her staff had always taken for thoughtfulness. That was why her mouth was open. I put the paper in before the word arrived. I never heard what it was. I have heard the hesitation since.

* * *

I sat back on my heels on the concrete for nine minutes by my watch.

She was cooling at the rate I would have expected, which in the first hour is almost no rate at all. The core holds for a while, a plateau, a shelf the curve rests on before it begins to fall. Only the periphery goes early: the fingertips, the tip of the nose, the backs of the hands, giving their heat to a room at eleven degrees that had more need of it. I read cooling curves every working day. I read them from the far end, from a thermometer and a liver and a clock, working backwards to a time nobody saw. I had never been present at the start of one. I sat and watched the first of it the way you watch the first minutes of a bath running, knowing exactly how long it will take and staying anyway.

My knees ached on the concrete. My fingers were stiff inside the nitrile. The heat had gone out of my face.

Then the tape. I peeled the industrial tape off slowly, the reverse shriek, a long tearing complaint going backwards round her skull. It took the fine hair from her nape with it and left a line of adhesive across both cheeks and round the back of the head, the colour of dishwater, catching the light. I bagged the tape. Her jaw stayed open around the bolus. The paper showed at her lips, softened, bone-pale going to slate at the edges where it had wet through, and on her lower lip one fragment still carried part of a footer: LC-03 and then the cut. I left it.

I cut the zip-ties at the bracket, one per wrist, and bagged them, because they carry lot numbers. Her wrists came round into her lap with the ligature furrows already printed in them, pale troughs with a pink margin. I let the furrows stay. They were evidence of nothing I owned. The ties at her ankles, and the band of tape there, went into the bag with the others.

Her jacket came open to the sternum. Under it the slate shirt, buttoned to the second button, warm still at the centre. The page was a fresh print of Lesson One; the master stays in the drawer, with my corrections on it, where nobody will read them but me. I set the page flat over the manubrium, in the midline, and put a single steel pin through the page and the shirt and the skin. Through skin that has stopped bleeding a pin goes in like a pin through a hem. The fabric doesn't argue. Nothing came. The dead don't bleed. I have known that for fifteen years, and it was still the plainest thing in the room.

The red marker was the only new smell in the room. The solvent came up sharp and sweet in the cold, and after the ammonia it was almost festive. I held the cap in my teeth, which is where it would leave with me, and drew the circle round THE GATEKEEPER the way I circle a finding for a registrar who has missed it: once, closed, no going round twice. The nib squeaked on the paper. The campaign never used red. Red was for corrections.

The Evaluation was where I had typed it two years ago. CONFUSED PROCESS FOR PROTECTION. I read it against the woman who had said she was sorry, the woman who had asked about channels and then about her daughter and then had apologised for not having kept me safe. It held.

The paper I left where it was: in her mouth, and the few pieces that had fallen onto her jacket and the floor. That was policy, and I'll give you the policy. The matter number is counsel's. It points at the people who keep the file. Whoever found her would pick up a fragment with tweezers and read a number, and the number would take them up the building, floor by floor, to the room no one enters. I was satisfied with that. I had thought about where it pointed, and I stopped thinking there.

As for the rest: it had gone well. The first lesson had gone very well, and the pleasure of it was the pleasure of the grade being handed back to the right person, in the right room, in full. I said that to myself in the dictation voice, and filed it, and I was confident about it, because I had decided to be.

My mouth was dry under the hood. Then it wasn't. I noted it.

I wanted the first investigator to read them. Whoever it would be, standing where I was standing, crouching, with a torch, with tweezers, picking a wet scrap off a dead woman's lip and reading counsel's number off it and understanding, slowly, floor by floor. I wanted that. And I noticed, as a finding, that I wanted a lot of things just then. I wanted to take the black off and look at her eyes, to see whether the petechiae were as I had pictured them. I wanted to open her. Professional curiosity. I had a bet with myself about her liver, fifteen hundred grams, and I wanted to put it in the pan and read the scale. I wanted to sit there longer. I wanted, badly, to eat, and there was nothing to eat, because I had brought nothing, which was a flaw in the planning, and I took a mark off for it. None of the things I wanted was to leave.

I left the way I had come in, out by the steel door, and closed it on her. I was ravenous.
