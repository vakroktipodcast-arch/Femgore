# Chapter 6

*Lesson One. Subject: The Gatekeeper. Function: to convert the complaint into the file, the file into the folder, the folder into the cabinet, the cabinet into the room no one enters. You have performed this function with distinction. Your failing mark is therefore not a function of incompetence. You confused process for protection. You will be evaluated accordingly.*

The pump station smelled of what it had been built to do and had stopped doing. Damp concrete, the cellar breath of it coming up through the floor. Machine oil gone to varnish in the seals of the old pump, sweetish, like the inside of a grandfather's toolbox. Rust off the iron pipework, which is iron oxide, which is a cousin at a couple of removes of what I smell at work every day, and which I found I liked having in the room. And Diane. A floral drugstore shampoo, freesia, or something that had been allowed to use freesia's name. The clean sweat of a Sunday walk, dried on her by now and being replaced, from the axillae outward, by the other sweat. The waxed cotton of her jacket, which smells like a wet dog that went to a good school.

I had turned the lantern on so that she could see me. It stood on the concrete between us, a battery LED, blue-white, the light of a fridge opened at night, and it threw both our shadows up the curved brick so that there were four of us in the room and two of them were very tall. Diane was on the floor with her back against the pump housing, knees drawn up, ankles bound, wrists behind her: one zip-tie per wrist, each joined to the iron bracket at the base of the housing. Zip-ties are designed by people who never have to take one off, which is how you know they work. Her wrists were already flushed raw at the edges of the plastic. Beside the lantern lay her phone in its foil sleeve, wrapped like a leftover.

I was in a white disposable coverall with the hood up and two pairs of nitrile gloves, and I looked like a crime scene that had arrived early. I sat on the pump's plinth, out of her reach, my face turned a little off hers; I know what breath carries. My hands were cold inside the gloves. They were steady. I checked, and did nothing with the information. My pulse was still up from the door, the knee, the tape; I could feel it in the hinge of my jaw. Somewhere in the dark a drip fell into something, slowly, like a clock that had been asked to keep it down.

"I'm going to explain the course," I said.

Earlier, in the oxblood of the head-torch, she had seen a light and two hands. Now she had a face, and I watched her open a file on it.

I explained it the way the convenor does on the first morning, standing at the front with the handbook, to a room that has not yet worked out which of them will fail. Three pages. Three subjects. Three evaluations. She was the first. The grade was not negotiable, and it was not pending. It had been given two years ago. Today was only when it was handed back.

She listened the way compliance directors listen: as if she would be asked to initial it later. When I stopped she waited a full second to be certain I had stopped, which is a courtesy you learn in rooms where interrupting costs money.

"If this is about a complaint," she said, "there are channels."

"I know. I used them."

I was pleased with that. I hadn't planned it. Five words, and the whole case for the prosecution in them, and she would have to sit with them on the floor, because there was nowhere else for her to sit.

She was quiet. Then, carefully, choosing each word as though it came out of an account she intended to keep in credit: "Is anyone near Olivia?"

"No one is near Olivia."

It was true. I said it quickly, and heard myself say it quickly, and graded the speed as a kindness, and took a mark off for it. Nobody had asked me to be kind. It wasn't on the paper.

Then I gave her a number. I didn't need notes. "LC-0311."

It is in the footer of every page of every one of the campaign's nondisclosure agreements, under a thin rule: counsel's matter number. A gatekeeper knows the numbers on her own gates; she had walked that one through the building herself, and she would have known it the way you know your own postcode. Her eyes went to the number first. I watched them do it, a small movement up and to the left, a woman reading a footer off the inside of her own skull. Then they came down, in the lantern, to my face.

She made a sound. It was small, a dry click at the back of the throat that she had not sent for, the larynx shutting on something it had not been told to swallow. I know that sound. I have heard it down the telephone from mothers and from husbands at the moment my sentence reaches its noun. I had never been the noun before.

She looked at me for a long time. I let her. At the fundraiser her eyes had passed over me the way you pass over a fire door. In three years nobody from the campaign had looked at me and had anything happen behind the face, and now something was happening behind hers, slowly, and I had waited long enough for it that I could wait through it.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm so sorry. I should have protected you from that."

She said it quietly, and fluently, and she meant it. She was not apologising for the agreement, or the file, or the folder, or the cabinet, or the room no one enters. She was apologising for the thing the file was about. She was sorry she had not kept me safe from it.

It was the worst thing she could have said. She still believed the file had been the protection and only the protecting had failed, as though the cabinet were a good cabinet someone had forgotten to lock. I watched her not know why. For one line I was not amused by anything, and a taste came up at the back of my tongue like a coin held there too long, and then the line was over.

* * *

I am going to describe this properly. I am the only person qualified to.

First I let her sit with the grade: two hours, she against the housing and I on the plinth, not speaking. It is the part of the course where the student reflects. Then I stood up.

The black vinyl first. I tore two strips and the roll gave them up with a dull, almost polite unpeeling, the sound of someone getting up from a leather chair. Temple to temple, the first over the brows and the upper lids, the second a little lower, overlapping, pressed down along the zygomatic arches with my thumbs. Her eyelashes moved against the adhesive once, a small trapped flutter under my thumb, like a pulse you are not sure you have found, and then they stopped. She was in the dark. When I opened the zip bag her shoulders came up at the sound, a noise she had heard over a thousand packed lunches and never once as a threat.

The paper in the bag was photocopies of pages one to eight of my own campaign agreement, no signature page, cross-cut at my kitchen table into confetti the size of fingernail parings. I held the bag up in front of her blindfold, for form's sake. It was the first time anyone at the campaign had been asked to swallow one.

Her mouth was open. The pump housing held her head. I put the fingertips of my left hand either side of the larynx, at the borders of the thyroid cartilage, to steady the head and pin the larynx so that a swallow could not carry the paper down from where I meant it to sit. With two gloved fingers of my right, my face turned up and away over her head, I packed the paper in past the incisors and over the dorsum of the tongue and seated the bolus towards the oropharynx in one push, with the pressure I use to seat packing in a cavity at the end of a case, and was most of the way out when the bite came. Her teeth met paper and the tip of the outer glove, which is what the outer glove is for. A pinch at a time gags in a series. One push gags once, hard, and is spent. I had tipped her chin down, because breakfast at seven can come back at half past ten, and I waited. It stayed down. Then the paper did what it had done in a glass of water at my counter the week before, when I pressed it with the back of a spoon like a woman checking whether a cake was done. Dry, it was card. It drank her saliva weight for weight, card to felt to something like the skin on a pan of porridge. It smelled of toner as it wet, warm and faintly electrical, the smell of an office at the end of a long day.

Then the industrial tape. Industrial tape cannot be unrolled discreetly. It is the only tool in the kit that announces itself, a shriek coming off the roll that in that room went up the brick and stayed there. I shut her jaw over the bolus with the heel of my left hand and laid the free end on her cheek first, to anchor it, and drew the first pass across her mouth under my hand, and only then let the jaw go. Then I tipped her head forward with my palm on the occiput and went round: three turns, over the mouth and both nostrils and round the back of the skull, pewter, pulled snug. On the second turn it caught the fine hair at her nape. I felt it catch, and noted it, and did not solve it. I had a lot on.

My watch said 10:40.

First, nothing. She held still. Twenty seconds. Twenty-five. Thirty. It was discipline, the same muscle that had once taken a stammering child and made of her a woman whose pauses passed for thought, and I admired it out loud.

"That's very good," I said. Even now she was doing it by the book, as if holding still were a channel.

Then the body overruled her. The smell came first, the other sweat sharpening through the freesia, before the rest of her had been told. Then the first involuntary inspiration came against the closed airway, and the diaphragm pulled down on nothing, and the tape dimpled inward over the nares in two small soft pits, and the suprasternal notch drew in above the collar of her shirt, and the intercostal spaces sank between the ribs as though each rib were being tugged on a string from the inside. It isn't the lack of oxygen that does it. Oxygen falls quietly; you could faint of it at altitude and never notice. It is the carbon dioxide, building in the blood, that the brainstem cannot tolerate. The body is not afraid of having no air. It is afraid of its own exhaust. I have some sympathy with the brainstem. I have been somebody's exhaust.

Then the struggle. The zip-ties bit and knocked against the iron bracket, plastic on metal, a sound like someone trying a locked gate. Her heels went against the concrete together, scraping first, then drumming, the bound ankles lifting and coming down. A hum came up through the tape, a voice with nowhere to go, and went round the room and came back. Behind her back her fingers opened and closed on nothing. My own pulse had come up into the hinge of my jaw and was keeping time with her heels, and I let it. I checked each phase against my watch the way I would check a registrar's timings against the book, and each phase came when the book said it would, and the book was right.

Then the loss of consciousness, which you can see happen in the hands: they stop meaning anything. Then the convulsion, the whole of her going rigid against the housing, and the shaking came through the concrete into my knees, and I kept them where they were. Then the bladder, warm urine rising into the cold air, expected, logged. Then a stillness long enough to be mistaken for the end, and then the agonal gasps, spaced further and further apart, each one arriving after the last like someone remembering something halfway through a sentence and then losing it again. Above the pewter band her face was going dusky, plum going to slate. I'd have put money, unseen, on petechiae in the conjunctivae under the black, pinpricks of ink through blotting paper; she had fought hard enough to earn them. I have opened this from the inside dozens of times. I had always come in at the end of this. I was watching the outside of it now, from the beginning, in order.

I put two fingers on her carotid and counted it down. It slowed and thinned and went irregular, a few beats and a gap and a few beats, and I counted the gaps as well, and then there was nothing to count, and I kept my fingers there anyway until I was sure, and it was 10:47.

Then the quiet: the drip somewhere in the dark keeping its own slow time, the faint electrical whine of the lantern that I had not been able to hear over her, my own pulse loud inside the hood, a hundred and something, banging in my ears and in my jaw and in the soft tissue of my throat, my face hot, actually hot, in a room at eleven degrees, warm again for the first time since the door, and all of it arriving at once and staying, the way the sound had stayed, as if the room had been built to keep it for me. It goes on the sheet next to 10:47.

I have left something out. After the black went over her eyes, and before the paper went in, Diane began to say a word. The old hesitation caught it on its first syllable: once, a stress and a fall. Not the pause her staff had always taken for thoughtfulness. This one stuck. That was why her mouth was open. I put the paper in before the word arrived. I never heard what it was. I have heard the hesitation since.

* * *

I sat back on my heels on the concrete for nine minutes by my watch.

She was cooling at the rate I would have expected, which in the first hour is almost no rate at all. The core holds on a plateau before the curve begins to fall. Only the periphery goes early: the fingertips, the tip of the nose, the backs of the hands, giving their heat to a room at eleven degrees that had more need of it. I read cooling curves every working day, from the far end, from a thermometer and a liver and a clock, working backwards to a time nobody saw. I had never been present at the start of one. I sat and watched the first of it the way you watch the first minutes of a bath running, knowing exactly how long it will take and staying anyway.

My knees ached on the concrete. My fingers were stiff inside the nitrile. The heat had gone out of my face.

Then the tape. I peeled the industrial tape off slowly, the reverse shriek, a long tearing complaint going backwards round her skull. It took the fine hair from her nape with it and left a line of adhesive across both cheeks and round the back of the head, the colour of dishwater. I bagged the tape. Her jaw stayed open around the bolus. The paper showed at her lips, softened, bone-pale going to ash at the edges where it had wet through, and on her lower lip one fragment still carried part of a footer: LC-03 and then the cut. I left it.

I cut the zip-ties at the bracket, one per wrist, and bagged them. Her wrists came round into her lap with the ligature furrows already printed in them, pale troughs with a margin the pink of a skinned knee. I let the furrows stay. They were evidence of nothing I owned. The ties at her ankles, and the band of tape there, went into the bag with the others, and left their furrows where they had been.

Her jacket came open to the sternum. Under it the slate shirt, buttoned to the second button, warm still at the centre. The page was a fresh print of Lesson One; the master stays in the drawer. I set the page flat over the manubrium, in the midline, and put a single steel pin through the page and the shirt and the skin. Over bone the skin is thin, and it went in like a pin through a hem. The fabric didn't argue. Nothing flinched, and nothing came. Nothing ever does, at my end of it.

The red marker was the only new smell in the room. The solvent came up sharp and sweet in the cold, and after the urine it was almost festive. I held the cap in my teeth, lips shut on it, face up off the page. A cap in a glove touches everything the glove touches after; a cap in the mouth touches nothing. It tasted of solvent and somebody else's stationery cupboard. I drew the circle round THE GATEKEEPER the way I circle a finding for a registrar who has missed it: once, closed, no going round twice. The nib squeaked on the paper. Then I dropped the cap from my teeth into a glassine envelope and pocketed it. The campaign never used red. Red was for corrections.

The Evaluation was where I had typed it two years ago. CONFUSED PROCESS FOR PROTECTION. I read it against the woman who had asked about channels, then about her daughter, then had apologised for not keeping me safe. It held.

The paper I left where it was: in her mouth, and the few pieces that had fallen onto her jacket and the floor. That was policy. I was satisfied with where the number pointed, and I stopped thinking there.

As for the rest: it had gone well. The first lesson had gone very well, and the pleasure of it was the pleasure of the grade being handed back to the right person, in the right room, in full. I said that to myself in the dictation voice, and filed it, and I was confident about it, because I had decided to be.

My mouth had gone dry under the hood. Then it was watering. I wanted the first investigator to read the fragments. Whoever it would be, crouching where I was crouching, with a torch, reading counsel's number off a wet scrap on a dead woman's lip without touching it, because you don't touch it, and understanding, slowly. I wanted that. And I noticed, as a finding, that I wanted a lot of things just then. I wanted to take the black off and look at her eyes, and collect on the petechiae. I wanted to open her. Professional curiosity. I had a bet with myself about her liver, fifteen hundred grams, and I wanted to put it in the pan and read the scale. I wanted to sit there longer. None of the things I wanted was to leave.

I left the way I had come in, out by the steel door, and closed it on her. I was ravenous.
