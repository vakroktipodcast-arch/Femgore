# Chapter 23

I told him my name.

"Iris Vale."

He sat down again. He had stood up when he saw I was standing, and now he lowered himself back into the chair by the dressing table, the one upholstered chair in the room, with his jacket on the back of it behind him. He put his hands on his knees. He looked up at me.

I was on the other side of the table. Between us on it were the lamp, a bottle of water with the cap still sealed, his remarks clipped at the corner, and the photograph, facing him. Behind me was the screen, and behind the screen was the door I had come in by. My hands were in the pale gloves. My right hand was in the right outer pocket of the blazer, beside the roll of tape. It was not holding anything. My left hand was flat on the outside of the blazer, over the inside pocket. I did not take either of them out. I did not touch the table. I did not touch anything in the room.

He looked at my face. Then he looked at the badge on my chest. Then he looked at my face again.

The badge had someone else's name on it. He did not say so. He did not say anything about the gloves. He did not ask how I had come in. He did not look past me at the screen.

He was looking for me. I could see him do it. His eyes went over my face, the eyes and the mouth and the hair, and back to the eyes. His head went a little to one side. He opened his mouth once and closed it again. He was careful about it.

I let him look. I had come for him to look.

Nothing came.

Through the wall the ballroom was playing music that was mostly drums, and the bass of it came up through the floor into my shoes, and under the music there was a crowd, a great many people talking at once and waiting for him.

He sat back. He breathed out through his nose. He smiled, a smaller smile than the first one, and shook his head at himself.

"I'm so sorry," he said. "I meet a great many people, and I'm afraid it shows."

He let that stand.

"It's the one thing I'm supposed to be good at."

He did not raise his voice for any of it. The room was small, and he knew how to talk in a small room. He talked to me and to nobody else.

I did not say anything.

He leaned forward in the chair and put out his right hand. He put it out across the dressing table, past the photograph, low, with the palm turned a little up. He held it there. He was patient about it.

I didn't take it.

My right hand stayed in my pocket. My left hand stayed where it was. He drew his hand back and put it down on the arm of the chair. He did not say anything about it. He did not look at it. He looked at me, and he was still pleasant.

I had built two years around him. He had looked. There was nothing there.

I did not look away from him. He did not look away from me.

I stood where I was. The lamp was on. The carpet was damp. It had kept my four paces in its pile. The photograph faced him and not me. His jacket hung on the back of the chair. His tie was knotted. He was waiting.

I had said my own name out loud in this building. Somewhere in the building there was an FBI liaison who was a line on page four of the run-of-show. Somewhere there was a list with my name on it.

He said it back to me.

"Iris."

He used my first name back to me, warmly, as if he had just learned it.

He had.

* * *

I had always assumed the syllabus would begin with his face. Every rehearsal of it had begun there, with him knowing me, and the heat coming up into my face when he did, the way it had come up on a concrete floor and over a desk, and the rest following from that the way a report follows from the external examination, in order, nothing skipped. It had not begun there. So I would give him the evidence, and he could examine it himself.

I said it the way I give evidence. In order. No adjectives. The voice I use in court, where they ask you to keep your answers short and you do, because short answers are the ones that hold. I had written it out at length, in the drafts, over two years, and cut it, and cut it again, the way you cut a report you expect to defend on the stand, until nothing in it gives the other side a handle. What was left was four sentences.

"It was June, three years ago."

He was listening. He leaned forward a little, his forearms coming to rest on his thighs, his hands loosely together between his knees. He did not interrupt. I had watched him listen on a feed for two months, to donors and to aides, and in a small room, close to, he was better at it. He listened with his whole face. He listened to me across his brother's photograph, and his brother faced him the whole time I talked.

"A hotel room after an evening function, not this hotel."

Between sentences there was the ballroom, a muffled thudding through the plaster, and a man on a microphone saying something I couldn't make out, and the crowd answering him. Somewhere on the far side of that plaster my name was on a page I had never seen. I could hear my own voice in the room. It was flatter than his. It went out across the table and stopped at him and did not come back.

"You closed the door."

Then I told him the fourth.

My right hand closed once inside the pocket while I said it, on nothing, and opened again. While I said it, his right thumb and forefinger went to his left cuff and gave the cufflink, plain silver, a quarter-turn, and his eyes went down to it, and stayed down for two seconds, and came up again.

That was the only place his eyes went. He did not look at the door, or at the photograph, or at my hands. He looked at me the way he had been looking at me since I said my name, attentively, with his whole face, and when I stopped he went on looking for a breath, as if there might be a fifth.

That was all of it. Two years of drafts. Out loud it took four sentences. I have dictated longer findings on a hand.

I had reported it once, three years ago, on paper, to the people whose job it was, and signed where they showed me. I had written it into the drafts. I had never said it to him. I had said it now, across a table, in the voice I use for the strangers I open, and the room took it and held it, and nothing in the room changed.

He frowned. It was a small frown, troubled and careful, two short lines drawn in at the glabella, the frown of a man being asked about a case nobody had briefed him on. He held it for a breath. Then he spoke, gently. He said it as a correction, and as kindly as a correction can be said.

"I think you may be thinking of someone else."

I heard the shape of it before I heard what it meant. *I think you may.* A woman had begun a sentence to me that way once, on a Sunday afternoon, from an office, into a machine. *I think we may have... known the same person.* She had stopped once, before *known*. He did not stop anywhere.

I waited for the taste. It had come every time that June came up, in a ballroom, on a feed, on a concrete floor: the coin at the back of the tongue, held there too long. It didn't come. My mouth was dry. My tongue stuck to the roof of it and came away.

My left hand came off the blazer and went to my side without my sending it, to the place below the ribs, and pressed there through the blazer, and stayed a moment, and went back.

I smelled for him before I let myself look, out of habit, across the table: cedar, and the floral cleaner coming up out of the damp carpet, and the warm dust off the lamp. None of it told me anything. Then I looked at his face for the finding. That is what a face is for, on my table. It is where the body keeps the part it couldn't help. The lamp on the dressing table lit him from the side. His eyes were steady on mine. The pupils did nothing. There was a fleck of powder beside his nose, where somebody had made him up for the cameras and missed a little. The frown was already easing, the two lines going out of the skin. The face gave me nothing I could have sworn to. I have sworn to a great deal. I could not have sworn to that.

My eyes went, once, past the corner of the table to the chair. To the place where its arm met the front leg, beside his knee. There was a gap there, under the arm. I noted it the way I note where the door is in any room, and looked back at him.

He was waiting for me to say something. I was waiting for something too. Neither of us got it. Through the wall the man on the microphone said something, and the crowd answered him again, louder, the long rising noise of a room being promised a thing.

His eyes went past me to the main door, the one with the latch he had thrown himself. It was forty, on my count. Out in the corridor the post would be stepping back to the landing. I had rebuilt the night on forty earlier that evening, on a stair, from his first minute, counted through a sealed door. In all the old footage he had never once gone over.

He glanced at the door. Not for rescue. For his minute. He was worried about the time.

* * *

I was cold. I had been cold since the bathroom floor, and in the room I could feel exactly where: the nitrile against the palms of my hands, cool and slightly tacky where it had warmed and gone cold again; the back of my neck under my hair where it was pinned up; my shins. I report it because it was mine. I don't know what the room was doing. The cold was coming from me.

He brought his eyes back from the door. It took him an effort, and he let me see the effort, and he did it courteously. He sat up a little in the chair. He was giving me time he did not have, and he did not hide that he was giving it.

"Whoever did that to you," he said, "I'm sorry. I mean that."

I could not have told you whether he meant it. I could tell you which voice it was. It was the voice he would have used for a woman at a rope line with a story about her son, the warm, unhurried voice that stops the line for her and lets everybody see it stop. He was offering me his sympathy as a bystander, with his whole attention, and the way he offered it there was nobody in the room for it to be about.

He did not ask me which function. He did not ask who else had been there, or what I had done afterwards, or why I had waited three years and come now, tonight, through a door he had not heard open. He asked nothing. A man who had forgotten would not have known what to ask. A man who remembered would not have needed to. I could not tell which kind of nothing it was. He had already given his answer, kindly, and the rest of the conversation, the way he held it, was about me.

Then he went further than I had expected, further than he went anywhere on the footage. He told me he had done a great many things in his life he would do differently, if he could have them back. He said he was not a man who pretended otherwise. He said he could name them. He leaned back while he said it, his hands loose on the arms of the chair, and he looked, for a moment, almost amused at himself, almost tired.

"Most of them were votes."

He was not defensive. He was not braced for anything. He was at ease. It sounded as honest as anything I had ever heard him say. The ones he could name were on the record, under his own name, with the yeas and the nays.

"Let me get someone for you," he said. "There are people here who are very good at this."

He meant staff. A quiet room off a corridor. A woman with a clipboard and a soft voice, who would take some details and give me a number to call in the morning, and would call me, once, to follow up. A file. The campaign had always been very good at finding someone. A file was the one thing in the building I already knew I was in. There was a list somewhere in it, in a federal file, with my surname on a line of its own, and the list had not needed anybody to be kind.

I heard Diane in it. The whole of her function, the complaint and the file and the folder and the cabinet, in one courtesy, from a man with his hands loose on the arms of a chair.

Two women had rung me once, looking for someone who knew. I had found them somewhere to go. It had taken one key, twice.

"No," I said.

He accepted it at once. He inclined his head, a small nod, and let it go, the way he had let his hand go back to the arm of the chair.

The syllabus had not anticipated this. I knew every line of the page in my inside pocket. I had written it two years ago, at the desk at home, and had read it more often since than anything I have ever signed. The other two had been addressed to people who knew who had written them, or who knew before the end. Lesson Three as written began from his recognition and went on to his exposure, the way the other two had gone. There was no clause in it for a man who would not stand where the page put him.

I had prepared for denial. I had prepared for rage, and for bargaining, and for tears, his or mine, and for a hand going under the table to a button. I had prepared for him to call out, and for nobody to come. I had not prepared for manners.

My hands were where they had been. The right in the outer pocket, beside the roll of tape and the scrap of the label I had taken off my glove at the door. The left flat on the blazer over the inside pocket, and under it, through the lining, the hard case. I could feel it through the nitrile and the cloth: the edge of it, the ridge where the hinge ran, the small weight of it against my ribs. Beside it the marker, and the pin, and the page, folded in thirds. I knew what was in every pocket I had on.

He glanced at the door once more, briefly. Then at the photograph, once, as if for permission to be late. Then back to me, pleasant, waiting, kind. He was letting his minute go for me a few seconds at a time, and he wanted nothing for it.

I stood very still. I had been standing still for a long time by the clock in my head, and for almost no time at all by anybody else's. The bass came up through the floor into the soles of my shoes. The crowd on the other side of the wall had not been told anything yet.

I didn't yet know what I was going to do, and for the first time in this book you were ahead of me, and I knew it.

Thirty seconds over, on a clock only I was counting. I put my hand inside my jacket and checked for it the way you check a pocket. There was the case. There was nothing else.
