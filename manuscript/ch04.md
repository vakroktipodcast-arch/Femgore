# Chapter 4

Diane Pell sat down on the bench at nine minutes past eight, which was within ninety seconds of what three years of her own fitness app had said she would do, and I gave the app a distinction before I gave her anything. I was a hundred and eighty metres away across the water, on the west shore, in a waxed hat and a fleece the colour of lichen, with binoculars, a field guide, a thermos and a logbook of herons. The logbook is real. I keep it. Nobody who watches a woman write *heron, 1, on the riprap, 7:52* in a small careful hand thinks about anything but herons, and the herons, in fairness to them, think about nothing at all. Birdwatchers are the only people allowed to stare at a stranger through ten-times magnification for forty minutes and be thought gentle.

My own phone was at home in its foil sleeve, in a drawer, telling nobody where I was. I timed her on my watch.

The reservoir smelled the way still water smells at the end of summer, before anyone has told it summer is over: wet stone, and the green slick of algae at the margin where the riprap went under, and pine resin coming down off the slope behind me, and goose droppings on the path, all of it sharpened by the damp that comes off a body of water in the early morning and sits in the air like breath on a mirror. A cormorant stood on a post out in the shallows with its wings held open to dry, like a man showing you he isn't armed. The water lapped at the stones below my boots. Across it, Diane took a paperback out of her jacket and opened it, and I heard the small flat sound a paperback makes when the spine is cracked back, which I could not possibly have heard at that distance and heard anyway. I am telling you so you'll know the difference between what I observed that morning and what I supplied.

She read for eleven minutes. She marked the page with a folded receipt, put the book back inside her jacket, and stood up at twenty past eight.

I had her body in the glass the whole time, and I want to give it to you the way I had it. About a hundred and sixty-five centimetres, about seventy kilograms, compact, with a swimmer's shoulders gone soft, the deltoids still broad under the olive waxed cotton but the definition long since banked and covered over like a fire; a slate shirt under the jacket, buttoned to the second button; the fat beginning its slow migration to the waist, the way it does after menopause, when the oestrogen stops telling it where to sit and it goes, like a guest nobody has seated, to the middle of the room. Her stride was short and brisk and economical, with almost no swing in the arms, the gait of a woman carrying a folder that isn't there. Hands in the jacket pockets as soon as the book was away, which is what people do with hands used to holding something. I put her liver at fifteen hundred grams and a good clean colour, a glass of white most nights and never two. I have been wrong about livers before, but never by much, and never in their favour. I liked her. I liked the way she sat down as if the bench had been booked. I had already weighed her. I want you to have both of those in the same hand, because I did.

The route I knew from the app, and that morning I watched it come true. Out of the south car park, clockwise, six point four kilometres of loop; the bench at three point one, the halfway mark; eleven minutes; then on round the second half, below the dam, where the path runs under a slope of sumac just starting to go the colour of a burn, past a trail marker post where a spur climbs forty metres through the scrub to a pump station whose brick roof I could just see from my side of the water. She passed the post at about eight thirty-three and did not look up the spur. In a hundred and forty-one Sundays she had never looked up the spur. Nobody walked with her. Nobody waited for her in the car park. There was no detail, and her phone stayed inside her jacket the whole hour, which for a woman who answers on the first ring was like watching a smoker not smoke. The Chief of Staff of a campaign nine weeks out from an election was as easy to find as a mailbox, and about as well guarded, and every person who might have told her so was downtown in a glass room being sure of things.

My hands were cold on the binoculars, the knuckles first, the damp getting into the joints the way it gets into an old house. It was not a cold morning. It was my hands. The coffee in the thermos had been made at five and had burnt in the flask, black and bitter and tasting of the inside of the lid, and I drank it anyway, all of it. I was hungry. I had brought no food, because a birder who eats looks like a picnic, and a picnic is remembered. I noted the hunger and drank the coffee. I'm giving you that as a fact about the morning.

The next Sunday was the sixth of September, and I had planned a close pass. I would watch her sit from the same place on the west shore, then walk round below the dam the other way and take the stretch from the bench to the marker post at her pace, about thirteen minutes, twenty past to thirty-three past, on my own legs, at the place where it would happen. You do not learn a road from a map.

At nine minutes past eight there was nobody on the bench. At twelve minutes past there was nobody. At sixteen minutes past she came out of the trees, late, and she was not alone. There was a young woman beside her, taller, with an iced coffee in one hand and the other hand talking, and I knew the face from the dossier photographs before the binoculars had finished deciding where it was. Olivia.

I graded myself before I graded anything else. It was Labor Day weekend. I had not checked the Wellesley academic calendar. The app had shown me four Sundays that ran a third slower than the rest, and I had looked at their dates and not at what the dates had in common. A two-year plan, failed on a public holiday. I docked the mark and logged it.

They shared the bench. Diane did not take out the book. Through the glass I watched the daughter say something with her free hand, a long shape in the air, and watched the mother laugh, head back, a sound I could not hear at all and did not supply. Olivia walked with Diane's short stride and none of her stillness; even sitting, her feet were going. Heritable gait. I noted it.

I capped the lenses, front and back, and closed the logbook on the morning's one heron, and walked back to the car the long way round. The target had a daughter in frame now, not in a file.

* * *

On the Tuesday I watched the War Room from my kitchen, standing at the sink with the laptop balanced along my left forearm and a peach in my right hand. I plan standing up. I eat standing up when I plan. It was about nine o'clock and the peach was one of the last of the year, the skin furred and loosening, and the flesh gave under my teeth all at once, sweet and a little fermented where it had bruised against its neighbours in the bag, and the juice ran down to my wrist and I let it.

The camera in the bezel of the wall screen looked straight down the table, so I had faces, a dozen of them, and the glass walls, and the city lit up behind them like a circuit board, but not the screen itself; the numbers I got by ear. The sound was compressed and slightly late, laughter arriving in flat little packets, and under everything the hotel's air handling, a hum like a held note, and somewhere a chair on carpet. Brandt's chair had no one in it. It was Tuesday, and on Tuesdays he played the piano at the Tabard Club from nine until eleven. I noted his absence the way you note a missing instrument on a tray, and went on.

Holt was being briefed, in his shirtsleeves, one ankle on his knee like a man at a barbecue. He was up six in the tracking average. An aide read it out and said, pleased with himself, "Less mistakes from here and it's his to lose."

"Fewer," Holt said, and smiled, so that the correction arrived with a gift in its other hand, and the aide laughed, and so did everyone else. He was right. He is always right about grammar. I gave him full marks for syntax again, because I am fair, and because there was nobody in the kitchen to see me do it.

Then the donor. A man who had given a great deal had written to ask whether the Senator might attend his daughter's wedding, and, separately, whether the Senator might be photographed with his dog. Holt did the donor's voice. I won't try to reproduce it. It was a slightly wet, slightly wounded baritone, the voice of a man who has been told no only by waiters, and the room came apart, and I laughed too, out loud, alone, in my kitchen, with peach on my chin. I am recording that I did. It was a very good impression. He has always been very good at doing other people.

Diane did not laugh. She was at his left, two seats down, with a tablet flat on the table and her reading glasses pushed up into her hair, and she let the laugh run exactly as long as it was useful and then took the wedding off the table in one sentence: "A card, a staffer, and no dog." She moved him on to the next item, and on the way, before one word, she paused. The staff took it for thoughtfulness. I had watched them do it at the fundraiser. I have the dossier. It wasn't thought. It was a door she taught herself to hold shut, a long time ago, and she still checks it every time she walks past.

Holt, who learns the names of his staff's children the way other men learn the names of wines, said, "She get back all right?"

"Monday night. The train."

"Sophomore year's the good one. Tell her I said so."

Two days before, I had been on the far shore of the same daughter. I noted it and went on.

You should have seen them. Twelve people in a glass box on the thirty-first floor, entirely confident that glass only works in one direction. Not one of them looked at the door, or at the walls, or at the bezel of the screen they were all facing, or at the lip of the credenza by the coffee urn, where a second little eye the size of a shirt button sat in its curl of black vinyl and watched them pour. They were the safest people in the city because they had agreed among themselves that they were, and they had been safe for so long that their safety had become a kind of furniture, and I thought that was very funny, and it was also exactly what had been done to me.

Holt stood. He gathered his jacket off the back of the chair and shook it out and put an arm into it, already half turned towards the door, and said over his shoulder, lightly, the way you'd ask whether someone had remembered to lock the car: "And the old files. Those are closed?"

"Yes," Diane said.

No pause. Not before that word.

It took five seconds. He went out. She said something to the woman beside her about Thursday. No name had been spoken, not mine and not anyone's, and I could not tell, then or now, whether he meant me, or me among others, or a cabinet he had never once pictured with anything inside it. I have watched people discuss a case over a body. I had never before watched two people discuss me, in five seconds, and move on to Thursday.

I had to put the laptop down. I set it on the counter and squared it to the edge, parallel, the way I put things down, and my hands did it well; they were steady. The peach was still in my right hand, bitten down to the last third, the stone showing through the flesh like a knuckle through skin, and my fingers were sticky to the wrist. There was a taste at the back of my tongue like a coin held there too long. The cold had come into my forearms, on the volar surface, where it had come on the night of the debate. I stood and held the peach and did not finish it.

* * *

On the Saturday I drove out in the late afternoon, in a hatchback the colour of slate that nobody has ever looked at twice, and turned off the county road onto the old water-authority service track. There was a lawn sign at the turn, navy and gold, MARCHETTI in block capitals, and the grass around it had been mown more recently than anything else in that part of the county. I parked in the gravel lay-by where the track bends out of sight of the trail and walked the last nine hundred metres with the bag on my shoulder.

The pump station sits below the dam like something the dam forgot it had: one storey of brick gone the colour of liver, iron pipework coming out of the walls and going back into them, a dry wet-well under a grating, and a steel door with a hasp. On the door a water-authority notice had been bleached by more summers than anyone had counted to the colour of weak tea. On an earlier night I had cut the authority's padlock off the hasp and hung an identical one of my own in its place. Nobody had noticed. Nobody checks. The county had put more care into the grass round a lawn sign than into a building with a door.

Inside it smelled of rust and cold wet concrete and machine oil gone to varnish on the pipes, and mouse, and from the grating a long mineral breath, like the inside of a well, which it was. It was colder than the evening outside, about eleven degrees, and it kept them.

I worked it the way I set out a tray. I swept the floor and bagged the sweepings. I laid the heavy sheeting and taped down its edges, and the plastic crackled with static against my wrists. I staged the kit along the far wall in a row, parallel to the brick, left to right in the order of use: zip-ties, industrial tape, black vinyl tape, the red marker, the foil sleeve. At the base of the old pump housing there was an iron bracket, bolted into the plinth, and I took hold of it with both hands and put my weight back on it, all of it, and it did not give a millimetre. Then I stood in the middle of the room and named, one by one, the things an investigator would look for in it, and took each of them away before he could find it. It is most of what I do.

The recorder was a cheap digital thing, bought for cash, and not my phone, because my phone was at home. I taped it to the back of the trail marker post, forty metres downslope, where the spur meets the path, which is where the trail is and where anyone on it would be, set it running, and walked back up through the sumac.

Clap one. Inside, door shut. A brick room with iron in it gives a clap back to you sharp, with a short slap off the pipes, like a ruler brought down on a desk. Then I went out and down to the post and played it back with the recorder against my ear. Under the birds, faint and flat, a knock, the sound of somebody's door three houses along. It had come through the seam. Fail. I marked it as one.

Clap two. Outside the door, for reference. On the playback it was loud and clean and dry, the scrub swallowing the tail of it, and the birds stopped for it, all of them at once, the way a room stops for a dropped plate. Now I knew what a sound from up there weighed by the time it reached the path.

There is no dignified way for a woman in her thirties to applaud a building with nobody in it and then go and listen to a tape of it. I have accepted this.

I fixed the seam. Adhesive foam weatherstrip round the frame, the backing paper curling off onto my sleeves; the hasp side packed; a moving blanket hung on the inside of the door; the hinges greased until the door swung without a word. It was tape, done properly, which is the only way I do tape.

Clap three. Inside, door shut. My palms stung by then, the thenar eminences pink and tingling where three claps' worth of small capillaries had been asked to take it, and my hands were steady through all of it. I checked. I walked down and pressed play. Birds. The water at the stones. A cyclist's freewheel ticking past and away along the path, a long insect sound. Nothing from the building. Nothing at all. I played it twice, to be thorough, and then once more for no reason I put a name to.

I peeled the recorder off the post and put it in my pocket, and went back up the spur, and inside, and pulled the door shut behind me against the blanket, and stood in the dark.

It is not instant. At first there is only black, total, with the afterimage of the doorway hanging in it, a pale oblong that slides away when you try to look at it. Then the rods take over from the cones. The rhodopsin in them, bleached by a day of light, regenerates a little at a time, and the room comes back to you not in colour but in no colour at all, the pipes first, then the edge of the pump housing, then the pale line of the kit along the far wall. Twenty minutes, by my count, and I counted. My pulse slowed while I stood there until I could hear it, and then I could hear nothing else. My patients are very quiet. It had never occurred to me to want that from a room.

I did not mind it. I stood in it longer than I needed to. I found I liked it.

The oxtail had been in the car since I left, in a plastic container in the footwell, braised the night before with butter beans and onions and most of a bottle of wine until the meat had let go of the bone, and in the cold of the footwell the fat had set to a pale lid on top and the gelatin underneath had firmed, so that the whole thing was a single trembling piece I could have turned out onto a plate. I didn't have a plate. There were bones in it, and I knew what they were: the coccygeal vertebrae, which is all oxtail is, each one a little smaller than the last, the transverse processes running down to nothing, like a sentence that knows how to end.

I ate dinner in the car at the lay-by with the dome light off, all of it, cold, out of the container with my fingers, and then I licked the container, corners first.
