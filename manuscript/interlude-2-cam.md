# Interlude — Cam

He was the last one in. Eight minutes to midnight on a Sunday, the charcoal suit and no tie, which was the only sign that anyone had called him at home. The War Room was loud. A dozen people standing, nobody sitting, every face lit from below by a phone.

There was an urn by the door. He took a coffee from it without asking how long it had been there. Tepid. It tasted of the urn and the wax lining of the cup. He drank it anyway. The cup gave one hand something to hold while the room told him things.

Comms wanted a statement by six. Counsel wanted nobody to say anything. Both of them would get what they wanted, Cam thought. In that order.

Someone said *activist*. Someone said *lunatic*.

The photograph was up on the wall screen. Everyone was reading the page in it.

"Phone," he said. "Not the wall."

Someone killed the screen. He opened the channel on his own phone and looked.

Diane on a concrete floor. The pump station; he knew the walk. Black tape across her eyes, the dull black of a record sleeve. A printed page pinned to her chest, one word circled in marker. GATEKEEPER. Under it, in a typed line: *Confused process for protection.*

He read it once.

Fair mark. The thought came and he turned it face-down.

Then he looked at what nobody else in the room was looking at. Her mouth. Packed full of paper, the paper gone soft where it was wet. Something she was no longer using, stuffed with something nobody had ever been meant to read twice.

He put two fingers to the glass and spread them.

A serif face. A thin rule along the foot of a page. Half a number below the rule.

He knew the typeface. He had chosen it. Four years ago, for the template he had Diane use. Century Schoolbook, because it looked like a judge had written it.

His thumb stopped on the glass and stayed there.

The coffee went on cooling in his other hand. The air system hummed. Somewhere behind him a phone was set face-down on the conference table, a small separate sound, and somebody's room-service fries were going cold on a tray by the window, and the hotel carpet smelled the way hotel carpet smells at midnight, of everybody. He did not move.

"Cam?"

The deputy comms director. Wanting to know if it was political.

"Crank," he said. "Probably."

Then: "Who's in the channel."

Fourteen people. He asked who had posted it. A boy near the window put his hand half up. Theo Akers, junior digital. A contact at the county sheriff's office had sent it to campaign security. Security had sent it to Theo. Theo had thought everyone should see it.

Cam's thumb moved. It saved the image to his own phone. Then, as channel admin, it deleted the post. The order of those two things was not quite a decision. He noticed it afterwards, the way you notice you have already locked a door.

"Off your phones," he said. "All of it. Nobody has seen this."

Theo began to say something about the record. About keeping a record.

Cam looked at him until he stopped.

Counsel was standing at the glass with the city behind him, one hand flat on the window. He asked whether the paper in her mouth was anything. He asked it without looking at the photograph. Cam watched him not look.

"Paper," Cam said.

Counsel had drafted those agreements. He had never seen what they looked like torn up and wet. Cam thought about how many things had Diane's name on them. Some of them were his. He did not say that either.

He finished the coffee. Cold now. He dropped the cup in the bin by the urn. He put the phone in the inside breast pocket of his jacket, on the left side, where the page had been pinned on her, and he buttoned the charcoal jacket over it.

He did not tell anyone what he had recognised. Not counsel, who had written the agreements and would not know them in pieces. Not comms, who wanted a sentence by six. Not the Senator, who would be told in the morning that this was a tragedy, and would be handed a statement that said so. Standing in a room of fourteen people who all thought they were working on the same problem, he understood that he was already working alone, that he had begun the moment his thumb stopped on the glass, and that it did not feel like a decision at all. It felt like twenty years of habit, turned for the first time on the room he was standing in.
