# Chapter 18

My hands were on the wheel, and now I looked at them.

The blood had changed colour since the suite. On the backs of both hands it had gone from the inside of a cherry to oxblood, and it had dried first where it lay thinnest, so that it was setting in the creases of my knuckles the way grout sets, darker in the lines than on the tiles. The right was worse, still tacky past the wrist where the glove had come off for the heart. There were prints on the rim of the wheel at ten and two, because I drive at ten and two, like a woman who has never once been pulled over. A thumb on the gear knob, the whorl clear enough to classify. I sat in the printworks' rear car park at a quarter past two and read my own car the way a scene examiner reads a kitchen, and it was the first time that night I had seen myself as evidence.

The tub of seventy per cent isopropyl wipes lives in the door pocket, as it has in every car I have owned. One wipe for the wheel, one for the gear knob, one for the indicator stalk, one for the inner door handle, each folded in on itself and bagged with the towels. It was a stopgap. The belt, the key, the outside handle: those would wait for the car's own night. Alcohol fixes blood; it doesn't lift it. The solvent bit my nose and went up behind my eyes, and the tub went into my jacket pocket with the phone. Then I pulled a fresh pair of nitrile on over the blood, left and then right.

I did not clean my hands. I covered them. Those are different procedures, and I know which one goes in the report.

The drive is thirty minutes. Sodium by the river, then the blue-white of the ring road, which makes everyone under it look as if they are waiting to be identified, and I kept to the limit exactly. The elation was still in me like a second drink, but it had stopped reaching my face. Under it, on a loop, his voice. *She died eight months later.* And before it, a name. I could not hear the name. I could hear exactly where it had been, the way your tongue can find where a tooth was.

Procedure is that the bag inside the bag goes where it goes. I put the bags down inside the front door, beside my shoes, like shopping, and walked away from them.

I went into the bathroom, turned on the fluorescent over the basin, and did not turn on the shower. I sat down on the tile with my back against the side of the bath, in the clothes I had worn to the suite, my knees up and my gloved hands on them, and I did not get up for six hours.

The weather had turned while I was inside, the first cold night. Tile is the only honest surface in a flat. It tells you exactly what temperature you are. It drew the heat out of me through my thighs and my palms, and the enamel of the bath drew it out of my spine, and I knew the cold when it came the way you know someone's step on the stair. It was the old cold, the one I had thought was gone.

I began to shiver. I reported it to myself as rigors, and then I corrected the report, because rigors want a fever and I had none. This was shivering thermogenesis: skeletal muscle contracting against itself to make heat, a sign that the body has not yet given up on the house.

That was the last clean sentence I had for a while.

Then the diaphragm went. I know the words for the rest. The vocal folds closing and opening on air the body is pushing out of its own accord, a sound below decision. I had used those words a few hours before, about him. I heard that they were the same words.

It went on.

I didn't stop it. I didn't try.

My nose ran and I wiped it on my sleeve.

It went on for a long time.

The suite had kept every sound he made and given none of them back. My bathroom is tile and enamel and a frosted window, and it gave back every one of mine, a fraction late, so that I heard myself the way he never got to hear himself in that room. I have attended a great many bathroom floors professionally. It is where the body goes when it wants to do something without a witness, and the body is always wrong about that.

And under it, the whole time, the thing I couldn't stop seeing. Not him. Me. I played it back the way you scrub footage, a frame at a time and in order, because order was what I had. The smell of the open chest first, warm iron and the butcher's-counter sweetness of opened meat, with the vetiver over it. Then the fourth costal cartilage going under the shears with the sound of celery, and a name going under it. The lung on that side letting go of the wall. *I'm sorry. I thought you should know.* And I had been happy. I need to be exact. I was happy while he said it, in the same seconds, with the shears in my hand: my pulse loud in my ears, my mouth watering, the heat low down that I had put in the same column as his sweat. Then the heart in my bare palm, and a thought in my own voice: three hundred and eighty grams, give or take. Then the stairs, two at a time. Then the chicken. I had been thinking about the chicken.

I did not grade any of it. There was nothing to write in the margin.

The cistern ticked. At some point I peeled the gloves off inside out, the way I had taken off the right one in the suite, and let them lie on the tile. Nitrile doesn't breathe. The blood under it had never dried; sweat had held it soft and pink and slick, and the skin beneath was pruned. In the air it dried at last, and when I made a fist it cracked along the knuckle creases and flaked the way paint flakes off a wet-room ceiling. At seven, through the wall, someone's shower started. Nobody would ring from work; I had booked the day off on the Friday, because I plan the day after the way I plan the meal after. My hair was against my face and the suite was in it, vetiver and rye and warm dust and him, with the isopropyl sharp on top. My left flank pulled where I was folded against the bath, the stitches two days out. I pressed it once with the heel of my hand. I didn't think anything. The frosted window went from black to dishwater, and the mirror was over the basin above my head the whole time, and I did not look up into it.

At about ten to nine I could stand, and I stood facing the door. The first thing I reached for was my phone, in its foil sleeve in my jacket pocket.

Not to call anyone.

* * *

I slid it out of the foil, and when I turned it over there I was on the glass: a thumb, and the side of a forefinger, ridges up, as legible as any print I have watched the lab lift off a decedent's spectacles. My hands had been on my wet face, and the blood on them had remembered it was blood. Procedure came back all at once, the way feeling comes back into a foot. I laid the phone face-down on the rim of the bath. I washed my hands at the basin, nails and webs and backs and up past the wrists, and then my face, the water running rust and then rose and then clear, my eyes on the plughole. I dried my hands and face. I stepped out of the suite clothes onto the tile and left them there, and put on a clean shirt. I cleaned the phone, and the inside of the foil, with a wipe from the tub in my pocket, and put the foil with the bags. Before I knew anything, I had been careful. You may make of the order what you like.

The flat was cold. The heating was off, and I left it off. My feet were bare on the boards and my knuckles were raw where the blood had cracked and the nailbrush had finished the job. I sat at the writing desk with the locked drawer under my knees and opened the county voicemail app, and then the folder I had never opened. Archive.

Two messages.

I keep everything. Mara once said I would archive a sneeze. In my years of county service I have returned every call I was ever left, or saved it, and I have pressed 7 exactly twice, and both times I was busy. Of all the statistics I have compiled for the county, that is the most damning.

The top one: Wednesday 30 September, 11:38 a.m., forty-seven seconds. Marked as read. Fourteen days old to the day. I did not play it. I knew what was on it: a woman saying sorry twice, men outside her house, and behind her a dog shifting its claws on a hard floor.

The second: 17 September, three years ago, 4:52 p.m. Thirty-four seconds. Marked as read.

I played it.

A breath first. Behind the breath an office: a phone ringing at somebody else's desk, a door. Then a woman's voice, young, careful with itself, coming small out of the speaker into a cold room.

"My name's Nora Wade."

There it was. I heard it in her own mouth, and I heard it at the same moment a second time, under the sound of celery breaking.

"I think we may have..."

She stopped. Once. I had heard a woman stop like that before, on a concrete floor, and I knew what it cost to get past it.

"...known the same person. I just wanted to talk."

Then a number, said slowly, for someone who might be writing it down. I was not writing it down. I have never rung it.

I know the rest of that day without remembering it, the way you know the route to work. I played the message the next morning, between cases. I pressed 7. I ate lunch, because I always eat lunch. I do not know what it was. I can tell you everything I have eaten since August. I cannot tell you what I ate on the day I put that woman in a folder. That is the finding.

On the clean laptop, never the phone, I searched her name. It took one page. An obituary in a newspaper in another county: Nora Elizabeth Wade, twenty-seven, died suddenly at home on the twenty-first of May the following year. Eight months. He had been exact. *Suddenly* is the word families use at twenty-seven when the answer is one of two things. I have signed both, many times, and spelled them correctly. I would not pick. I could have asked that county for the file. A request is a document, and documents are read. I closed the laptop.

I unlocked the drawer and took out the master of Lesson Two. *Excellent.* in red beside the subject line, in my hand. Below it *Tessa Quinn* in black, struck through with one line, ruled against the edge of his dossier. I laid the page beside the phone, the struck name next to the two messages, and looked at the three of them together, and it was very simple, the way the bad ones are always simple once the bloods come back.

I did it twice.

She had been one of them. Brandt had said eleven names, and I had counted myself in and stopped counting. A list of eleven had no room on it for a woman who had been dead two years and more. She had known the same person, and signed what I signed, and found the one other woman she could find, and rung her at work. The other woman was me. I had spent two years writing pages about the cabinet, and I had been one of its drawers. I had been running it. And I had been eating well the whole time.

I can tell you what I ate after the second one. Cold lamb, the fat set white and melting on the tongue, the salsa verde sharp with caper and anchovy and too much lemon, eaten sitting down at work, with the fork I keep in my desk drawer there, every mouthful. It was as good as it had been the night before. Fourteen days on, I could still itemise it.

The red master of Lesson One was in the drawer under my hand. I didn't take it out. I wanted to know whether I still had it by heart. I had. *Function: to convert the complaint into the file, the file into the folder, the folder into the cabinet, the cabinet into the room no one enters.* Key 7. The archive. The folder nobody opens. I wrote that page two years ago with the first message already in the folder. That is all I am going to say about it.

My stomach made a long, plain noise in the quiet. Borborygmi. I noted it and stayed where I was.

I had been the Gatekeeper. My page would read: *Confused process for protection.*

I took a blank sheet from the printer tray. I wrote it in black ink, in the small upright hand I keep for case numbers, and the nib dragged a little on the paper, and my hand was steady all the way down, which I would have preferred it not to be. My name was on it. I did not circle anything. My desk red stayed capped in the pen tray.

I didn't pin it to anything.

* * *

In the afternoon I sat with the page face-up in front of me and did what I do with a body I don't yet understand, which is lay it out.

The syllabus is not justice. It is a way never to face that I could have helped, before, twice, and both times pressed a key.

That was the first thing it was. The second I am going to say plainly. It was a way to have what I wanted, and what I wanted was this, all of it. The weeks of planning, standing at the counter. The suite. The record turning. The volar skin of his forearm pushing back against the blade, the way nothing on my table ever has. The heat of him coming up through the nitrile at thirty-seven degrees. Flexor digitorum superficialis sliding in its sheath when he played. Pectoralis major moving when he breathed. His heart in my bare hand, working, and then not. I wanted his blood on my wrists. I wanted to be inside him with the lamp on. I got it. I have listed it the way I list findings and I will not take back a word of it.

Then I laid out the other thing, the way I laid out the syllabus, in steps.

Stop.

Do not destroy the kit. Bag it, seal it, label it and keep it, the way you keep a specimen for the other side's expert.

Go to the field office and ask at the desk for Special Agent E. Reyes, the signature quoted in counsel's covering email, which I had read on a Thursday at ten past one.

Let it come out in a courtroom, where nothing can be managed, because a matter stops being a matter when a judge is looking at it.

Let the other ten be believed. The dead one too.

I touched the scar through my shirt while I wrote the last step, because I was thinking.

I graded it honestly. It was the best plan I had ever written. It had only one flaw, which was that it ended.

I made tea. Getting the milk meant opening the fridge, and the chicken was on the bottom shelf in its brine, under a plate, waiting for the oven I had promised it. The skin had gone white and puckered and loose on it. In the trade it is washerwoman's skin. We see it on bodies pulled out of baths, and I know how bad a clock it is. I took the milk and closed the door.

I didn't eat.

I made the tea strong, with milk, and drank it standing at the window. It was too hot, and took a layer off the roof of my mouth; then it was tannic, the dry furred grip of it on my gums; then it was cold, and I finished it. The heat went out of the mug into my palms and out of my palms into the room, and the flat took it, and gave nothing back.

I turned my head and the suite was in my hair.

On the stairs I had turned my head more than I needed to. Now I did it on purpose, slowly, the way you smell a glass before you are allowed to drink it, and the vetiver came up, and the rye, and the warm dust, and under them him, and my mouth did what it had done in the suite. I wanted it. My body had decided before I had a sentence for it, the way it always does, and the sentence came after, like paperwork.

Not because the philosophy held. I had watched it fail on my own desk, in my own handwriting.

I was going to go on because I wanted to.

That is the most honest sentence in this book. Keep it next to the other word, the one from the suite. They are a pair.

Then I did what you do when you have decided. The clothes from the suite came up off the bathroom tile and went into the bags with the towels, not into my machine: heat sets blood, and a drum keeps everything it is given. The bags inside the front door went where they go. The instruments I cleaned at the sink and dried and rolled, the rib shears last, the way they had gone in, and then enzyme cleaner and peroxide down the sink and the basin, because I had run blood down both. The car would get peroxide and a night of its own. I did not wash my hair. I graded the exit: competent. I did not grade myself.

Early in the evening I dressed for the bar in the business hotel by the river, the one I had gone to after the fight. I got as far as the front door. My hand was near the deadbolt and the hall mirror was at my shoulder, and I did not look into it, and I did not go. Going meant washing the suite out of my hair. I wasn't finished with it.

I took the shoes off in the hall and went back to the desk.

Outside, the light went to sodium, block by block. My stomach made its noise again and I logged it as a finding: hungry, refused. I put the master of Lesson Two back in the drawer and locked it. I turned the page face-down on the desk and left it out. It isn't part of the syllabus, so it doesn't go in with the syllabus.

At twenty to seven the phone lit up beside it. Mara.

*Hope the day off was nice. Saved you a coffee, drank it for you. x*

I didn't reply.

The screen went dark on its own. I knew what was behind it: in the archive, the two voicemails side by side, thirty-four seconds and forty-seven, both marked as read. The page with my own name on it lay face-down on the desk.
