# Chapter 26

I drew the service door shut behind me with the flat of my left hand, and the torn sticker was beside my palm, white on the paint, split along the seam where I had split it and lifting at one corner like a nail that has caught on something. The knock came through two doors and the room between them. It was the third knock. Through two doors it sounded like a man knocking somewhere else, about something else, which in a sense it was.

I took my hand away. That was the last time I touched the sticker.

The passage was eight paces and a door. I had counted them going in and I counted them coming out, because the count had not stopped when he did and nobody had told it to. I had been counting since the service door. It was the only part of me still taking instructions.

The Stair C door opened to my shoulder. Nobody was on the landing. Its post had stepped forward into the corridor to widen the cordon when he came, and the whole cordon was facing the other door. The stairwell air came up at me, colder than the passage, smelling of concrete and old paint and the dust that lives on the top of fire extinguishers. Concrete treads. A painted steel rail. At each landing an emergency light in a wire cage, throwing a sodium-orange that made the treads look like something left in an oven.

I went down at a walk. A run is a sound, and a stairwell is a throat; it passes any sound you give it up and down to whoever is listening. On the flat my side said nothing, the way it had said nothing since my hands came away from him. On the steps it pulled, on the left, low under the ribs, once for every tread, in time, like a metronome somebody had left running in another room. My knee remembered the carpet. My thighs were tired in the long muscles at the front, and the backs of my knees with them, the tiredness I would have read off a decedent in the lactate if anybody had asked me to.

Thirty-one seconds to the closet door. I had paced it on the Sunday, at a working walk. It was thirty-one again.

I opened the closet with the gloves still on, which is what they were still on for. It smelled of laundered cotton and the bleach-and-citrus that housekeeping chemicals all share, and under that the rubber matting on the floor, warm and faintly sour. Shelves of folded linen to the ceiling. The par sheet taped inside the door. Hotels keep their linen better than hospitals keep their dead. It is folded, it is labelled, and nobody has to sign for it.

The round ivory cloths were where I had left them, not due until the Wednesday wedding. Under the bottom one, the catering jacket, folded over the black tote. My bag from the afternoon on the shelf beside them. The campaign's box of press folders beside that. It was all there. I noted it the way I note an instrument on a tray: present, in its place, nothing further to say about it. I did not feel anything I would call relief. I looked.

Then the change, which went the way a change goes in the locker room at the end of a shift, without anybody watching it, me included. The gloves came off, both pairs together, one turned inside the other, black into pale, and into the tote. The blazer came off. The page came out of its inside pocket and went into the inside pocket of the catering jacket, which had held nothing since a quarter past six, and lay against the left side of my chest, folded once down the middle with the old thirds still in it. I didn't open it. Then the jacket went on, black, with a slate collar and the caterer's wordmark stitched on the breast, which is what I had bought it for.

Into the tote: the blazer, folded, and inside it the lanyard and the RUTH CALDER badge, face-in, the borrowed name against the lining. The hard case, with the capped syringe in it. The marker. The pin. The roll of vinyl from the blazer's outer pocket, never used. The scalpel from the inside pocket, guard on. The fingernail of seal label with VOID on its face. Last, my bag off the shelf. Nothing else. The long tie was still on his wrist, with the chair on it.

I took the band off my wrist, let down what I had put up by feel upstairs, and tied it again low at the nape, the way the women who carry trays wear it. I squared the cloth stack the way I had found it. The box of press folders I left squared on its shelf. It was the only thing in the room I had brought, and it had never been mine.

I checked the closet the way I had checked the green room. The shelf. The floor. The handle. Then I closed the door with the side of my wrist, the sleeve pulled down over it. You open doors with your elbow when your gloves are dirty. There was nothing on my hands now, and they did it anyway. They have been doing it for fifteen years without consulting me.

The second flight. Halfway down it, the sound above me changed. The knocking stopped. There was a gap, about the length of a held breath, and then a voice said his name in a register I knew from corridors, from relatives in the anteroom, the register for a door you have already decided to open. Then several voices at once, and a radio squalled and was answered, and the shaft carried all of it down to me as if it were being poured. That was the door. I was two floors under it. I did not speed up.

At the bottom the fire door had a push bar. I put my hip into it and my left forearm, the sleeve over the wrist, and stepped through, and the closer took the door off me and drew it home with a long hydraulic hiss and a latch. I started a new count from the latch.

Back of house, street level. A concrete corridor painted slate to the waist and bone above it. Roll cages of linen. A stack of hotel pans. A time clock on the wall. On my left, a few steps from the fire door, the glass booth of the dock office, and in it a man in a hotel security fleece, sitting with his back to the corridor, and on the desk in front of him the radio in its cradle. At the other end of the corridor, forty metres off, the loading bay: the dock shutter half down onto the night and November coming in under it along the floor, and beside it the steel vendor gate. Neither could be seen from the booth. I had checked that on the plan.

I was level with his glass when the radio spoke.

"Security, all stations. Lockdown, lockdown. Stairs and lifts are locked. Hold all staff where they are."

Behind me the maglock on the stair door went home. It is a dull, total sound, like a fridge door closing on a full fridge. Eleven.

I was on the wrong side of it by eleven seconds. I kept walking.

* * *

Past the booth the corridor ran straight. Painted block. Fluorescent tubes in cages, one of them going, ticking at the end of each flicker like a fingernail on a tooth. A mop bucket against the wall with the mop standing in it. A laminated sign about manual handling, a stick figure bending at the knees. I walked at the pace of staff going off shift who would very much like not to be kept back for anything. The tote was on my left shoulder. My left hand was on its strap.

A porter was coming up the corridor towards me, and behind him two servers with trays, in the same jacket I was wearing. Ahead of them, from the mouth of the service-lift lobby, radio voices carried out into the corridor, several sets saying the same thing half a beat apart, and the three of them flattened against the wall with the trays up at their chests, the way people do in a corridor when they hear a tone of voice they don't have the rank to ask about. I passed them. Neither side looked. That is what the wordmark is for.

Behind them, out of the lift lobby, a woman came the other way.

She was walking fast, a stride short of a run, which is a skill: the corridor had people in it and she didn't yet know what she was running to, and a running woman in a corridor full of trays is a woman who arrives late and covered in soup. Mid-forties. Flat shoes. A dark trouser suit, cut a little long on the right. A visitor lanyard, turned round backwards, the way every lanyard turns itself round.

I read the walk the way I read anything that comes through the doors at work, before the paperwork. The right arm swung less than the left. The right elbow stayed close to the body, and the hips were faintly bladed, the right side carried as if it had something on it that it had carried for twenty years and would carry tonight whether or not it was there. I have opened three police officers. All three had the same thickening of the skin over the right iliac crest, where twenty years of holster had been filing a complaint. I wasn't going to get to check. I'd have bet on it.

No earpiece. No pin on the lapel. Not hotel, not campaign, not Service; the Service would have been on the channel, and she was not listening to anything but the corridor. I did the arithmetic before she was level with me. There was one federal person in this building who was not on the Secret Service's channel. She was on the distribution list of the security annex and on page four of the run of show, and I had read both more than once.

Then we were level, for half a second.

She looked at my hands. I looked at hers.

I can tell you what she saw of mine only by where her eyes went, and they went down and to my left and stayed a moment longer than a woman looks at a stranger's hands in a corridor, and I don't know which of my hands it was that held them. I know about hers. Short nails, unpainted, filed rather than bitten, with no varnish ever lifted off them; a callus in the web between the right thumb and forefinger, yellowed and smooth, where a grip has closed around the same shape at a range, year after year, for twenty years, to keep a qualification; on the inside of the right middle finger, at the last joint, a small blue mark where a biro sits when you write a great deal by hand and press harder than you need to; on the left, no ring, and at the base of the ring finger a faint pale line where one had been, a while ago, long enough for the skin to start forgetting it. They were the hands of someone who writes things down. I felt it the way I feel a finding, low and certain and before I had the words for it.

Her face I took in without marking it. It was a face that had been awake since early and expected to be awake much later, and had made its arrangements. As she went by she gave off cold air on wool, a suit that had been outside more than once today, and coffee. Urn coffee. I know that brand of tired; I watched a whole campaign drink it for two months.

My face was in no file she had. The Medical Examiner's office does not release photographs of its pathologists. The badge in my tote had no photograph on it, and nothing behind it in any database. Since Saturday, if counsel had done what the judge told him, she had had my surname on a page and a control number beside it. Tonight she had my face, and nothing to attach it to. I had her surname and her face. I wasn't sure that made us even.

Something in me reached for a mark for her, out of habit, the way a hand reaches for a rail on a stair. It didn't find one. There was no page for her. For the first time, I had the feeling the marking ran the other way.

My mouth was dry. There was a pulse in the side of my neck that I could feel without touching it, the first thing my body had volunteered in weeks that was not a complaint, and it was not hunger. I didn't name it. My legs kept the pace. I didn't slow and I didn't step further aside than staff would.

For one stride our flat soles came down together on the concrete. Then they were out of step again, and she was behind me.

She didn't stop.

I didn't look back. A few paces on I heard her soles change, the walk breaking into a run, now that there was nobody left between her and the stairs, and I didn't turn round for that either.

I knew exactly who she was. I am telling you now, before anybody else can: Special Agent E. Reyes. I had never known what the E stood for. I still don't. An initial is not a person, I had thought once, reading her signature in somebody else's email. It had a face now.

* * *

The vendor gate was a steel pedestrian gate beside the dock shutter, with a card reader on the street side and, on mine, a green button for getting out. I had found it on the floor plan. It ran on a nineteen-second timer. I pulled the cuff of the jacket down over my knuckles and pressed the button with the knuckle of my left hand, and the lock dropped with a click like a tongue against teeth, and I pushed the gate with my shoulder and walked through it. I didn't wait to hear it close.

Fire code requires a building to let you out. I have done the autopsies on the buildings that didn't.

The cold was the first thing. It came at the jacket and through it as if the jacket were a rumour, five degrees and dry, no wind, the kind of cold that doesn't bite so much as take an interest. I hadn't brought a coat. I didn't do anything about it.

The cross street behind the hotel was bins and a catering van with its back doors open, nobody in it, and steam coming out of a grate in the road in a slow white column, lit sodium from underneath. Then the avenue. Traffic. A bus with its interior lights on, three people in it, each one alone in a different way. Two men outside a bar arguing about something with great pleasure, their hands going. A woman walking a dog in a coat; I mean the dog. Nothing in any of it had changed. A woman in a catering jacket walking home at nine o'clock on a Monday is the least interesting thing in any city, and I knew it, and I walked down the middle of that knowledge like a pavement.

I didn't take a cab. A cab is a record. A ride from an app is a record with a map attached. I know what investigators look for because I have spent fifteen years telling them, in writing, with diagrams. So I walked. It is about nine kilometres, and it took me just under two hours, by streets I chose for being ordinary.

Through the window of the second bar there was a television over the optics, and on it the ballroom. A lectern with nobody at it. Flags. A crowd from behind, the backs of heads. A banner along the bottom: REMARKS DELAYED. Nobody in the bar was watching it. A man at the end of the counter was eating crisps out of the bag and reading his phone, and the barman was drying a glass with the absorption of a surgeon. I didn't stop either. Somewhere behind me a room full of people were being asked to be patient with a man. They were very good at it. They'd had practice.

There were sirens, once, a long way off, going the other way. I heard them the way you hear a tap running in another flat.

The cold settled into me and I let it. It had been there for weeks; it simply had more room now. It came up through the soles of my shoes from the paving and in at the cuffs, and the back of my left hand on the tote strap went the colour of a cold kidney, slate under the pink. I watched it do it and did not put it in a pocket. My side went from pulling to a dull regular ache, low on the left, keeping a time I wasn't keeping. My shoes were the flat black ones I had worn under the press assistant's navy, which pass for anything, and somewhere in the second hour a blister began at my left heel, where the service stair had given me one in August.

A kebab shop's vent breathed onto the pavement as I passed it: lamb fat on the spit, cumin, the char on the outside of the cone where the knife had been and would be again. Then a bakery, shut for the night, the shutters down, and through its grille the smell of yeast, of tomorrow's dough proving in the dark. Further on, exhaust, and a gutter packed with wet leaves going to mulch, the smell of a ditch in a park. I identified all of it correctly. It went by. Nothing followed it. My mouth stayed as it had been since the green room, clean and dry, a glass out of the dishwasher.

I was walking towards a locked drawer with my own page in it, face-down, and I was carrying the third page, folded once and still creased in thirds. That was where the walk went.

About halfway, at a crossing on a wide road, I stopped for the light. I pressed the button with my left knuckle through the cuff, and the steel of it was cold through the cloth. There was nothing coming either way. I waited anyway. Then I looked down.

My right hand was flat across my chest, bare, over the inside pocket. It was pressing the page through the jacket. Under the palm I could feel the fold down the middle, and under that, fainter, the two older creases running across it. It had been there since the closet. At the green-room door it had lain on the other jacket, over the same page, and I had known it then. Somewhere after that I had stopped knowing, and it had stayed. I had been holding it the whole way, the way I had held my own left wrist in the lift at the fundraiser, two fingers across it, counting. Except that this was the whole palm, and it wasn't counting anything.

The light changed. I left the hand where it was and crossed.

I was not hungry. I noted that I would once have called that a finding.

Behind me, an hour back, the vendor gate had closed on its timer, unhurried. I hadn't counted it.
