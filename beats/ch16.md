## Scene 1: Arrival

1. The corridor of the media consultancy after hours, lit by its one working light, a tube humming as if asked to do the work of three, and under the hum the Mingus leaking round a door that is latched but not pressed into its seals, turned down so he would hear her knock. She doesn't knock. She keys in the code (carried in her head and nowhere else), and the door comes away from its frame with a small sucking sound, swings to behind her and seats itself into its seals with a pressure in her ears, like a lift.
2. Inside, the room swallows the corridor. Smell first: rye; warm dust off equipment that has been on all day; the new-foam smell of the acoustic panels, faintly sweet like a packet of earplugs; and, at the height of a seated man, the dry green note she last met at his kitchen sink in Ch 13, worked into the webs of her own fingers. Then it was a bar of soap; now it is sitting down. She doesn't name it. Sight last: the one lamp, the row of amber standby lights along the wall at knee height, the long edit desk under its monitors, matte as slate and giving nothing back, a low table, a leather chair beside it and a second chair across.
3. He looks up from the chair. Charcoal shirt, no tie, sleeves down, the jacket over the chair back. "I wondered if it would be you." He isn't afraid, he's curious. She reads that as a finding: pupils normal for the light, hands loose on the chair arms, breathing slow, no sweat. She grades his composure in her head: excellent.
4. She reads "if it would be you" for what it implies. Someone else might have come first: Holt's people, now that Holt has locked him out of his own campaign (the card that failed at 7:51, Ch 14). He has been waiting for whichever of them arrived first, and he is glad it is her. She notices that she is pleased he is glad, and lets herself be.

It is her own print, the 8x10 close-up of Diane's mouth with the shredded NDA at the lips and half the matter number LC-0311 legible, the one she left squared on his kitchen counter nine days ago. She reads it like a body. The corners have gone soft. The gloss is worn through to the fibre at the tips. 6. Pleasure, reported without apology: she has been read before (a War Room reading the page, Ch 8), but she has never been kept. She allows herself to say it plainly: nobody has ever looked at her work this long, including her.
7. Brief, in fragments: "Phone?" "Jacket. Inside left." He leans forward to give her the pocket of the jacket on the chair back, the way a man does for a waiter. She notes that her own pulse is up against the silk of her collar (no number; Ch 17 counts) and that it isn't fear. ## Scene 2: The Conversation

1. He pours for her without asking how she takes it, two fingers, neat, which is how she takes it. She takes off her right glove to hold the glass, lays the glove across her knee, and tells herself the inventory, the way you count swabs before you close: this hand will touch the glass and nothing else, and the glass leaves with her. (This seeds Ch 17's bare right hand without naming it.) She sits on the arm of the second chair, not in it.
2. Taste: the rye is dry, pepper first, the char of the cask behind it, no sweetness anywhere, and the heat arriving late at the back of the tongue, like the sting of a cut after you have looked at your hand. It is the first thing she has put in her mouth since the light meal, and she describes it as exactly as tissue. She drinks the first mouthful slowly. (Avoid Ch 17's "crust of a dark loaf".)
3. He knows the syllabus is for him, and he says so in fragments that are starting to join up. He draws the page out from under his glass and lays it, still folded, on his knee. "Mine. Read it a lot." "I can see." He puts it back and stands his glass on it in the same place, as if the place had been measured. And then: "I read the other one too. In your drawer." (He means the Apex page, from Ch 9. Don't quote it or describe it.) She says nothing. He doesn't say what was at the head of it, though it would have bought him her face for a second. She grades his restraint, and it is high.
4. He asks how she came to it. He means the syllabus, not the injury. She answers out loud in short sentences: she reported, she kept records, she signed in Diane's office, and then the blind items came, his. He doesn't look away, explain or apologise, and she gives him a mark for each. In narration she gives the reader more ("since you have been through my drawer as well"): two years ago, at about this time of night, at her writing desk, a sentence arrived already addressed, not *she*, not *I*, but *You*, and she knew who; she sat there writing until the first bus went past under the window (Ch 1: "written two years ago at this desk"). She doesn't describe what Holt did. Keep the original injury out of the room.
5. Partway through her answer the side that was playing runs out. She doesn't hear the arm go home (her voice and his listening cover it) and only notices the room has got bigger, the way a kitchen does when the fridge stops. Neither of them changes it. (The audible arm is saved for the Snapper.) He doesn't beg, apologise or explain the blind items. His grammar comes together for the first time aloud: "I would have done the same thing." He doesn't mean the NDA. He means the syllabus.
6. She doesn't know what to do with that, so she drinks. She tells the reader so: she has a procedure for every kind of sentence a man can say to her in a room (threats, charm, lawyers, *I don't know what you're talking about*), a joke ready for each, and no joke prepared for being understood.
7. Now he is honest, and his sentences are complete. After the photograph he went down the NDA signatory list privately, looking for one name, and that is how he found her: not from her own agreement, as she has believed since Ch 9, but the list. The pages in Diane's mouth don't say whose they are; the footer is the matter, not the woman; you need the list for the woman. He turns his glass a quarter-turn on the page. Counsel wrote the template ("I want to be accurate about that"); the only thing on those pages that is his is the face, the typeface he chose, Century Schoolbook, "because it looks like a judge wrote it." "You think you're the only one." A pause, which is kind. "There are eleven names on that list." He says eleven flatly, with no twelfth and no struck name. He doesn't read the names. He doesn't say which one has medical training, because he doesn't need to.
8. Her body before she does. Her left hand goes to the flank wound and presses it through her shirt. She knows this is her thinking gesture and it is happening without her. Ch 9's line, rendered here as *We were the same kind of alone* (thought a month ago, and kept), comes back for the length of a clause and falls over. "Ten others had signed what I signed." Her face is hot, as when caught at something in a room full of people, with only one person in the room. He seems about to say something more, his mouth begins it, he decides *not yet*, lets it go, and puts his glass down on the page instead. (This seeds Ch 17's "There's one more thing." No hint of the woman or of death.)
9. [TURN] She had believed herself alone. She had built two years on being the only one, every page in that drawer stood on it, and he has just told her the floor is a list. He watches it go in and is kind about it. He gets up out of the leather chair and turns his back so she can have her face to herself. He lifts the arm, sets the needle back at the start of side one (the lead-in crackles like a fire being lit in another room) and turns it up a notch, so that neither of them has to speak for a while. That kindness, which he gives without being asked and which costs him nothing he will ever need again, is unbearable.

## Scene 3: The Preparation

She takes off her jacket (her phone asleep in its foil in the pocket) and hangs it over the back of the second chair. She stands and clears the long edit desk: ninety-six inches, which is two hundred and forty-four centimetres (she always does the conversion; tonight the furniture will hold a body and she wants them in the same units). The keyboards and the jog wheel go down on the carpet along the wall, the coiled cables laid in loops like a hose; the monitors are pushed back on their arms, dark. The cable grommets run along both long edges, with a cable-tray rail under each, exactly where the fit-out drawing in his facilities attachments put them (Ch 15). Joke: it is a deep pleasure when a building agrees with its paperwork.
2. She unrolls the instrument roll on the low table, between the photograph and the two glasses, and fits the blades there: a 10 onto the No. 3 handle, a 22 onto the No. 4, each slid down its bayonet with the forceps until it seats with a click. Then the small retractor, the toothed forceps, the spare blades in their sleeve, and the rib shears last, heavy, "the cheese course". The box of nitrile and the towels in their bags go on the floor beside the second chair. Ch 15's tasting menu, served. He comes to look at it with his hands in his pockets, the way a man reads a wine list he can't afford, looks at each piece in order, and asks no questions. She grades it: the highest mark she has given him yet.
3. "On the desk. On your back." "One thing first." He goes to the turntable, lifts the arm and puts the needle back at the start of side one, a third time, and she lets him, because she wouldn't have known how. Then he sits on the edge of the desk, swings his legs up and lies down without being helped, head toward the lamp, and lays his arms out along the edges, palms up, waiting, with room to spare at both ends.
4. The binding, done with procedure and pleasure. She unbuttons his left cuff and rolls the sleeve to the elbow, two neat turns and a third to lock it, then the right. His forearms: long and lean, a little dark hair along the ulnar border and none on the volar skin, pale and fine as good paper, the tendons fanning at each wrist; she wants her mouth on the inside of his wrist the way she wants to eat. Industrial tape round each wrist, palm up, and down to the edge of the desk (the tear off the roll enormous in that room, a long rip like canvas). Then the zip-ties: each wrist down through a grommet and round the steel rail of the cable tray beneath, "because a tie through a hole is tied to nothing". Halfway round his left wrist she thinks of the other ten, ten signatures she will never see, folds the thought once along its length and lays it under the work like a towel under a limb; her hands don't stop. Each ratchet goes under the Mingus like a watch being wound. The ankles taped and tied to the desk legs at the far end. A band of industrial tape across his thighs, thoroughness, not need. She keeps the leather gloves on for all of it: tape adhesive takes a print better than glass does.
5. He tests each tie as she finishes it, a small courteous flex against the plastic, the way a man tries a door after he has locked it for you. His hands lie open on the wood, fingers a little spread, like hands resting on a closed lid. She looks at them longer than she needs to and doesn't touch them (the pads stay for Ch 17's examination). She does not report that he is warm. The smell changes now he is lying down: rye on both their breaths, the dry green note rising off him into the heat of the lamp at the height of a table, the height of her work. She reads him from the top: the suprasternal notch in the open collar above the manubrium, the carotid lifting the skin, slow and even. Her pulse is high and unhurried; her face is hot and her knickers are wet, noted in the same hand as everything else; she is hungry, has not eaten since the bread, and "that was not why".
6. Brandt, in full and level grammar: "Take your time. I would rather it was you than anyone else."
7. She tells the reader plainly that no one has ever said anything like that to her. Not a lover (the ones who talked never talked like this), not Hannah, who loves her and wants her to take a holiday, not one colleague in fifteen years of corridors, and not one of the fourteen hundred, who never said anything to her in any form.
