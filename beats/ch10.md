## Scene 1: The Bait

1. Monday 28 September, 6:40 a.m., the ME's locker room. Iris is half into scrubs when she opens *The Cloakroom* on her phone, as she has every morning for three years, the way you press an old fracture when the weather turns. It's the anonymous political gossip site where the blind items about her ran. There is a new item, posted at 6:00 a.m., and by its second sentence she knows whose hand it is. That is the chapter's trouble, on the first page.
2. The item: a "former campaign volunteer", let go by Diane Pell last spring for "erratic conduct", has been posting about Pell for weeks under an anonymous account. Then a line from "a source close to the investigation" that whoever killed Pell "knew anatomy". Iris knows his sentences: the ones he wrote about her are clipped at the back of his file in her drawer (the passive verb that lets someone be let go without anyone doing the letting, the single phrase in quotation marks, put there to be repeated). *Knew anatomy* is Mara's word *practised*, carried out of the building and come out shorter and worse. Somebody had the file and handed it to him. It is the first time anything in that file has been used against anybody, and it has not been used against her. Under the item there is a thumbnail of a woman's face and a link to the account.
3. She doesn't tap the link. It runs through a shortener, and she reads that as a door with someone standing behind it, counting. Her thumb stops a few millimetres above the warm glass; she watches it as she would a thumb on the table (flexor pollicis longus holding the distal phalanx bent over nothing), notes it, and takes it away. She puts the phone in her locker with the link unopened and carries it through the day like a sweet in a pocket, deferred on purpose. "I knew what the deferring was. I am a woman who eats the crust first." (The prose does not have her name the item's two jobs here; the lure is reasoned out in S2 beat 5.)
4. The working day, briefly, for texture and for the body. The air conditioning has been turned down for autumn and autumn hasn't come; her neck is damp before her hair is pinned. A man in his seventies has come off a ladder while clearing gutters: a subdural haematoma, a crescent of clot the colour of blackcurrant jam, which comes away in one piece and she weighs. The gutters, per the report, were clear; he had finished. The tablet is in her bag in the locker, but she doesn't open the feed inside a county building, and she spends the afternoon wishing she could.
5. Evening, the flat, the windows open on a warm night. She eats standing (bread torn off a loaf, a tomato salted over the sink and eaten like an apple: "I eat standing up when I plan"). Then she scrubs back through the feed's recording to the eight o'clock meeting and watches the War Room find the item. Staff are delighted, because a crank volunteer is a gift five weeks out; somebody claps. Comms is drafting "we do not comment on online speculation" aloud, trying the words in different orders. Brandt stands at the glass with his back to the city and says nothing. Every face in the room does surprise once (frontalis, the jaw); his doesn't.
6. Then she reads the account on her clean laptop through a cached archive copy, never the live page. Its first post is dated Thursday 17 September, the day after he stood at her desk and read her drawer. It is eleven days old and grown like a sourdough starter: fed at the same hours, kept somewhere warm, to be thrown out once it has done its job. The misspellings are placed like seasoning, never in a name. The grievance begins small (how Diane once spoke to her) and grows louder in a curve she has seen in real files (who Diane was really working for, what they were all hiding). Nothing about paper, nothing about anyone else: one woman, one grudge, one dead boss. That is the craft, and it is his old craft. He built a woman whose accusations sound like symptoms.
7. She grades it: "I gave it an A. I don't give A's to fiction." Then she names the appetite, the first time in the book she does so plainly. "I was enjoying it." She locates it: heat in the face, along the forearms bare on the window ledge, a quickening under the sternum that is not anxiety. Somebody good has come looking for her, the most interesting thing in three years. "He had read me, and now he had written back." She does not apologise.
8. The photograph. At first she takes it for a composite or a stock face, the kind a careful man would use. Then she looks at it as a pathologist looks. The pores, a slight asymmetry at the alae of the nose, a healed laceration through the lateral third of the left eyebrow with the hair growing back white along it. Nobody invents a scar like that. It is a real face. A reverse image search on the clean machine resolves in seconds, which tells her he wanted it to. It finds a public social profile: a veterinary technician, a greyhound the colour of wet slate asleep on a sofa with its legs folded like a deckchair, a sister's wedding, a birthday cake with candles reading 31.
9. **[TURN]** The profile had a real face. It is a woman who looks like her: the same jaw, the same widow's peak, the parting on the same side, a few years younger. Brandt chose her for that. Her name is Tessa Quinn.

- **Sensory lead:** Smell. The locker room at 6:40 a.m. smells of floor disinfectant and the clean-laundry steam of fresh scrubs. In the evening, the warm flat with the windows open: tarmac giving back the day's heat, someone's barbecue two streets over (charcoal and fat and something sticky). Light by source: the phone's white in a dim locker room, the laptop's blue in the evening flat. Texture: the phone's glass, warm from her palm, and the deliberate not-tapping thumb.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**
  - "He'd been growing her for eleven days, the way you grow a sourdough starter: fed at the same hours, kept somewhere warm, and thrown out the moment it's done its job."
  - On *The Cloakroom*: "I read it every morning the way you press on an old fracture when the weather turns. Not because it helps. Because you want to know it's still there."
  - On the face: "Nobody puts a scar like that on a composite. Composites are drawn by people who want you to remember them, and scars are given you by people who don't."
- **Body:** Hands. The thumb hovers over the link and stays off it. She notes the restraint as a finding, the way Ch 9 noted the hover over his dossier. She is warm (Act II begins): her neck in the locker room, her forearms bare at the open window in the evening. She eats standing in the evening before she watches and reads, because she is planning: bread torn from a loaf, a tomato salted over the sink. She mentions it only in passing. Her face against Tessa's on the laptop screen, though she does not hold up a mirror. Mirrors belong to 15, 19 and 20.
- **Motifs:** The grade (Brandt's profile, graded high). Hands (the thumb). The paperwork thread (Mara's word leaking out of the file, the first time the file is used against anyone). Tape: not yet. Mirrors are deliberately withheld. The resemblance is read off a screen, not a glass.

## Scene 2: Collateral

1. Tuesday 29 September. By 9 a.m. the internet has her name. He left the image findable and it was found, and it moves the way infection moves through a ward, along the hands. Between cases Iris follows it on her phone, from the public side only, where she is one of thousands of people looking and therefore no one.
2. The catalogue inside forty-eight hours, kept forensic and brief. Her home address. Northgate Animal Hospital, the clinic where she works, closed for the day after its phones are flooded. A man livestreaming from the clinic car park. *KILLER* sprayed on her garage door. The police asking her where she had been that Sunday. Strangers analysing her hands, with arrows, in a photograph of her holding a spaniel puppy (palm under its chest, hindquarters along her forearm, the way you are taught to carry anything that might wriggle). Iris corrects their anatomy in her head and cannot stop.
3. Mid-morning the office's public information officer (unnamed) comes down with a sheet of paper and a pen. One line, answering the "knew anatomy" claim by saying nothing: the office "does not comment on active investigations". The Chief has approved it. He wants Mara kept out of it and his own name further out, so it goes out over Iris's, as the senior reviewer on Pell. She signs it. By the afternoon a wire story quotes "Dr. Iris Vale, the senior pathologist who reviewed the autopsy". The office does not release photographs of its pathologists. Her name is in the news and her face is not.
4. Tuesday evening, the flat. She braises a lamb shoulder (browned hard till the fat goes toffee-coloured and spits, a head of garlic in its skins, rosemary, half a bottle of wine, lid, low oven) and chops the salsa verde (parsley, capers, anchovies, too much lemon) while the War Room feed runs live on the tablet against the fruit bowl. Later Theo Akers turns his laptop round to show the table that the volunteer is trending. Someone cheers. Brandt says, "Don't share it," without looking up, the way you'd tell a dog to leave something. Then he takes out his own phone and watches it for a long time with nothing on his face. She can't read his screen and doesn't need to. (Do not reuse Ch 9's 2 min 11 s figure.)
5. She narrates his reason for him, as she did in Ch 9, and gets it right. The bait only works if nobody cuts the line. Whoever moves to help Tessa Quinn is the person who knows she didn't do it. He wasn't watching Tessa Quinn. "Nobody fishing watches the fly." He was watching the water around her, for a ring. She grades it: "Correct. Full marks."
6. Then her own reason, as the outline requires, honestly. First she lays out the tactical reason: any contact (a call, a message, a word to the investigator) would be the rise to his fly, "and I am not a trout". She tells the reader that is true, and that it isn't the reason. The reason is that Tessa Quinn is not on the syllabus. Three names in her drawer. There is no fourth. She tells it the way she told herself, with the lamb in the oven and lemon on her fingers, "and it sounded very clean".
7. Wednesday 30 September, morning. Mara stops at Iris's office door, hair still in its cap, arms folded across her scrubs. Mara: "They're saying a vet tech." Iris: "I saw." Mara: "It's my word. They've got my word." (half a step in) "Iris, the adhesive alone..." Iris: "Not in the corridor." Mara stops and looks at her; Iris lets her look. Iris: "It's still in the file. Where you put it. Go and wash up." Mara goes; Iris listens until the doors take her. Iris does not grade Mara, aloud or in narration. Keep the gap visible.
8. Iris is in the suite until noon. At 12:20 she sits down at her desk with lunch in its glass container (cold lamb and salsa verde in two compartments), and the desk phone's message light is going. One new message, left at 11:38 a.m. She plays it on the handset, receiver to her ear, the container's lid in her other hand. It is forty-seven seconds long; a woman's voice, close and compressed by the cheap line, a breath before each sentence. "Hi. My name's Tessa Quinn. I'm sorry. I'm sorry to call the office like this." She is looking for the pathologist in the news, the woman, because she hadn't written the name down, and the front desk put her through to the name on the wire story. She has never met Diane Pell and never been to the reservoir. The police have asked her where she was that Sunday. There are men outside her house. She thought that someone who actually knew what happened could just say so. "I'd just like to talk to someone who knows. If you can." She says a number, slowly. Behind her the whole time a dog shifts its weight on a hard floor, claws ticking as it settles.
9. Iris knows the office rule: there is a form for a call like that (the time, the number, what was said), and it goes to the investigator the same afternoon. She skips it. She presses 7 with the hand that holds the lid, one finger free of it, and watches the finger do it; it is steady (she is busy; Ch 18 says "both times I was busy"). The automated voice says the message has been moved to the archive. She knows, as everyone in county service knows, that nothing in a public office is ever deleted. It is only moved to a folder nobody opens. She has never opened hers. She does not write the number down. "I listened to it once. I deleted it." (These two sentences close this paragraph in the prose, just before the Turn's lunch.) **Do not let Iris notice that this rhymes with the Lesson One page ("the cabinet into the room no one enters"). The reader notices, or the second read does.**
10. **[TURN]** She ate lunch: the lamb cold and dense, the fat set white in its seams overnight and melting on the tongue as it warmed, faintly of rosemary; the salsa verde sharp and green and briny with caper, the anchovy coming up underneath (salt and rot), and too much lemon, so her jaw aches at the hinge. She takes the fork from her desk drawer and eats all of it, sitting down, and it is as good as it was the night before. Report the taste in full. No commentary.

- **Sensory lead:** Sound. Tessa's voice through the cheap handset is compressed and close, with a breath before each sentence. Then the dog's claws on a hard floor, the automated voice of the county system ("in the pleasant tone the county pays for"), and the small electronic chirp of the 7. Then taste, at the Turn: cold lamb fat, caper brine, anchovy, lemon. Smell on Tuesday evening: the braise, wine and resin and sweet lamb fat, filling the flat down to her hair. (The office air-conditioning detail sits in S1, on the Monday, not here.)
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**

  - On the internet's forensic experts: "They had found a killer's grip in a photograph of a woman holding a spaniel puppy, which is a thing you can do if you have never held a scalpel or a spaniel."
  - On Brandt: "He wasn't watching Tessa Quinn. Nobody fishing watches the fly."
- **Body:** Eating. On Tuesday she cooks, standing (planning). On Wednesday she eats lunch sitting down, which is how she eats after a kill. Do not say so. Let the posture be the only signal. Hands: one finger of the lid-holding hand, freed from the lid, on the 7. She watches it press, the way she watches her hands everywhere, and reports that it is steady. Warm all through the scene, including at the delete.
- **Motifs:** Voicemail (the second of the three unreturned. Set it up so Ch 18 can lay it beside the first. It is played once, and the archive is real). The meal (not the meal after, but eaten in that posture). The grade (Brandt's reasoning graded "Correct. Full marks."; Mara pointedly not graded). Hands. ## Scene 3: Detachment

1. Wednesday night, the flat, by ten. Every window is open and the heat sits in the rooms at the temperature of a proving drawer. A stereo down the street plays music with a lot of horns, which she can't name and doesn't try to. She stands at the counter with the heel of the loaf and the end of the salsa verde in its jar, not eating, and thinks about machinery: an account that regular is not run from a man's phone at a conference table but from somewhere that keeps hours. Nine days earlier she followed his car out of the hotel garage at eleven at night to a building in the warehouse district; she knows where it stands and almost nothing about what is in it, and she would like very much to see inside. She doesn't go that night. She puts the bread back in its bag: ten at night, nothing eaten since lunch, not hungry. "I am reporting it." (Seed for Ch 11. No company, no names, nothing about what the building holds.)
2. Detachment is the first principle, and she gives it its morgue form. You meet the body before you meet the name. You read the history after the findings, never before, because history contaminates. The syllabus is the same rule widened: three names, and everybody else is history that isn't available.
3. She maintains it, mostly. She does not search Tessa Quinn tonight. One tab is still open on the laptop from Monday, and she closes it without looking. Then she admits she looked long enough to know what it was: the greyhound.
4. The inventory, involuntary. She lists what she knows about Tessa without ever having tried to learn it. The eyebrow scar, the white hairs. The clinic. Her age. The greyhound's name, which is Pilot. The colour of the garage door under the paint. "I knew the dog's name. I hadn't asked for it. Nobody ever asks me what I know, and I know everything anyway." A pathologist cannot unsee a finding, and she presents the list as proof of training, not as care.
5. Direct address, the voice doing what the plot can't yet. There is something here. She can see its outline the way you see a shadow on a scan before anyone has put a calliper to it (a difference in density, bone-white going a shade towards ash), and she is not going to measure it. "I am telling you that I am not. Watch me not."
6. The drawer, the brass lock (the key's small oiled click, the drawer's long wooden sigh). Brandt's dossier is on top, still a quarter-inch to the left of the knot, where he put it in Ch 9 and she left it. She lifts the three files out as a block, fingers spread under and thumb flat on top, the way he had, without letting his slide; slides the sleeve out from under the photographs and her agreement; takes out the Lesson Two page, her drawer master, soft at the corners from two years of handling, with *Excellent.* in red in her hand in the margin beside the subject line, written a fortnight before. The red pen stays capped in the tray. She takes the black pen from the tray; it smells faintly of ink, and under that her fingers still smell of lamb fat, though she has washed twice since lunch.
7. Below *Excellent.* she writes *Tessa Quinn* in the small upright hand she uses for case numbers, "so that it could be read by anyone and mistaken by no one". Her stated reason is administrative. A name that isn't written down goes on following you about. Once it is written it is filed.
   - Between beats 7 and 8 a car alarm starts a couple of streets away, goes on a while and stops on its own, without anybody coming: "Most of my patients went the same way."
8. **[TURN]** She draws his dossier to the edge of the page and lays her left palm flat on it, on his name, hands as steady as they are over a body. "I had never struck through a name before." She draws the nib along the manila edge from the start of the name to the end, one stroke without lifting, the nib dragging very slightly on paper two years soft, and the line comes out straight and level. She doesn't say that the care she took is the tell. The line is the crack. She caps the pen; Lesson Two goes back in the sleeve, squared to its own edge; the files go back as a block, his set a quarter-inch left of the knot and checked against her fingernail; the drawer is closed and locked.

- **Sensory lead:** Texture and temperature. The heat of the flat on her skin. The paper softened at the corners from two years of handling, the nib dragging slightly on the dossier's edge as she rules. Smell: the black ink, faint (her red pen, which wrote *Excellent.*, stays capped in the tray tonight), lamb fat on her fingers that she has washed off twice and can still smell. (No light source is named in the prose.) Sound: the street through open windows, the horns on the stereo down the street, a car alarm a couple of streets away that stops on its own.

## Snapper

She isn't tired and goes to bed anyway, lying on top of the sheet with the windows open and the heat on her skin, not sleeping the nine hours of Ch 9 or anything like it. Late, the stereo down the street stops. In the quiet it leaves she finds she has something by heart that she never tried to learn: Wednesday 30 September, 11:38 a.m., forty-seven seconds. She deleted the message and kept the time.
