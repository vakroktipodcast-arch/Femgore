# Beat sheet — ch23: The Climax Begins

- **Unit:** ch23 (`manuscript/ch23.md`)
- **POV:** Iris, first person, past tense. The outline's Turns and Snapper are written in present tense. Put them in past tense on the page ("He used my first name back to me...", "I checked for it the way you check a pocket").
- **Target words:** ~3,150. Suggested split: S1 ~900 (flat and short on purpose), S2 ~1,100, S3 ~1,150.
- **Chapter head:** no syllabus insert (style 08).
- **Act pressure:** Act III. Reyes is closing on paper, and the knowledge from 18 is in every room. In 23 Reyes is felt as paper and names. Iris says her own name out loud inside the campaign for the first time, in a building where an FBI liaison is carrying a list with that name tenth on it (ch19: Reyes is on the Election Eve distribution list). The knowledge from 18 enters through his sentence in S2, whose shape echoes the dead woman's voicemail, and through his offer in S3 to "get someone", which is Diane's procedure and, since 18, Iris's own.
- **Appetite at entry:** Starved, and found missing at the table. 22 ended with "nothing in my mouth" when he smiled. She has been hungry for this one meal for two years, and the meal does not know it is being served.
- **Appetite at exit:** Absent, and she checks for it. At the Snapper she puts her hand into her jacket pocket to look for it, the way you check a pocket. Nothing comes up through the chapter: no salivation, no heat, no metal. In S2 she waits for the metal taste of the original injury (ch02) and it doesn't come either. That keeps 24's "the first thing I've tasted in five chapters" true. Never "empty", "hollow" or "numb" (style 11).
- **Voice rules in force:**
  - No syllabus voice and no grading anywhere (style 02: gone in 22–25). Holt is not graded, not even by implication.
  - S1 is the one place in the book where the voice goes flat. Short declaratives. No Latin, no anatomy, no simile, no joke, no "you". She reports what he says and does. She does not comment and does not interpret.
  - Jokes come back thinly in S2 and S3, one or two each, dry and turned on herself, never on him.
  - Holt has the best syntax in the book: whole, warm, subordinate sentences. No em-dash cut-offs for him (that licence is 24, S2 only), and no em-dash cut-offs for anyone.
  - Never go inside Holt's head (cast). Every gesture he makes must read two ways: forgetting or lying. The drafter must not tip it either way. If a line makes the reader sure, cut it.
- **Real time (keeps the ledger's forty seconds):** Iris has been counting since the detail's hand left the door (21 Snapper, 22). Out loud the chapter is short. S1 and S2 together run about forty seconds of real time, and narration stretches them. Out loud she is clipped (style 07), which is how two years of syllabus fit into about ten seconds of speech. His glance at the door at the S2 Turn falls at about forty seconds, when his slot ends: he has never gone over. S3 runs into the overrun. The Snapper's "thirty seconds" is thirty seconds past the slot, counted only by her. The detail does not knock in 23: nobody knocks, by standing instruction (ch17 anchor). The knock comes in 25. Do not put a wall-clock time on the page.

## Scene 1 — The Naming

1. First sentence: "I told him my name." Give the full name, in dialogue, as one line: "Iris Vale." It is the first thing she says to him on Election Eve (22 ended with her having no line). No preamble, no setting. 22's Snapper (the Marine between them on the table, facing him) is already in place, and the reader needs nothing more.
2. Positions in plain words. He is in the one upholstered chair by the dressing table. She is standing on the other side of the photograph, with her back to the service door she came through. Both her hands are in the pockets of the press-assistant jacket. She touches nothing in the room in this chapter.
3. He searches. It is visible and sincere, a man going through a drawer. His eyes go to her face, then down to the lanyard on her chest, which carries someone else's name, then back to her face. He doesn't mention the mismatch. Report it and do not interpret it.
4. Nothing. The pause is long enough for her to hear the ballroom through the wall: warm-up music, bass coming through the floor, a crowd being kept warm. Give it one sentence of sound and no more.
5. He apologises for his memory, in his best syntax: "I'm so sorry. I meet a great many people, and I'm afraid it shows." Then, ruefully: "It's the one thing I'm supposed to be good at." Let it stand. The reader knows he memorises staffers' children's names. Iris does not say so.
6. He offers his hand, the right one, across the dressing table and past the photograph. It is the hand of a man who has shaken a million hands, palm slightly up, patient.
7. She doesn't take it. Her hands stay in her pockets. Her right hand is on the kit and her left is flat against the outside of the jacket over the inside pocket where the third page is. Report it flatly: "I didn't take it." He lowers his hand without offence, the way you put down a glass someone has declined. He makes no comment, and that is his grace.
8. The single flat statement the outline asks for, with no commentary: she has rearranged two years of her life around this man, and he has looked at her face and found nothing in it. Make it one short line of fact and do not elaborate it, so the flatness does the work. Something close to: "I had built two years around him. He had looked. There was nothing there." The drafter may find a better version, but it must stay this plain.
9. Pressure, one line, also flat: she has said her own name out loud in this building. Somewhere on a lower floor it is the tenth name on a list. She does not say Reyes. The fact sits in the paragraph like a dropped key.
10. **[TURN]** He used my first name back to me, warmly, as if he had just learned it. He had. Give his line as one word: "Iris." Then her single sentence, "He had.", and end the scene.

- **Sensory lead:** sound. The ballroom through the wall and the bass through the carpet. His voice in a small room, which is the instrument he is best at. The hotel's carpet cleaner from 22 is still there underneath, given at most one word. Temperature and taste are withheld, because the flat voice reports almost nothing from her own body.
- **Jokes / dry observations:** none. The outline makes this the one flat place in the book, so there is no joke. The reader should feel the floor go before they can say why. (If the drafter must have something dry, keep it in his mouth: "It's the one thing I'm supposed to be good at." He doesn't know it's funny. She doesn't laugh.)
- **The body:** hands in pockets, kept there, with the right on the kit and the left over the page. She doesn't take his hand. She stands still. No report of pulse, heat or mouth, and their absence is the content.
- **Motifs:** hands (his offered, hers withheld); the Marine's photograph (face-up, facing him, between them, and his hand passes over it); the minute (her count running, not stated); the grade, by its total absence.

## Scene 2 — The Reminder

1. Voice comes back a little, but only on her side of the page. She decides to tell him, and narrates the decision in one sentence: she had always assumed the syllabus would begin with his face, and since it hadn't, she would hand him the evidence.
2. She tells him exactly what he did. Out loud it is clipped: four sentences in the order it happened. The date. The place. The door. What he did. No adjectives and no euphemism. She speaks the way she dictates a finding.
3. How to render the telling (author's call, flagged under Conflicts). Recommended: the reader hears the first three sentences verbatim (the date, the place, "You closed the door") and then the narration says only "Then I told him the fourth." Keep the act unscripted on the page, consistent with every chapter before. The weight lands on the fact that she said it, to him, at last, in the voice she uses for strangers on a table.
4. A dry line on herself, the first allowed in the chapter: two years of drafts, and out loud it took four sentences. "I have dictated longer findings on a hand."
5. He listens. He is a superb listener and kind in small rooms (cast). He leans forward slightly. He doesn't interrupt. At some point while she speaks, his right thumb and forefinger give his left cufflink a quarter-turn and his eyes go down to it for two seconds (ch01's gesture, exactly as fixed there). Name the gesture. Do not say what it is to her. It is the gesture she last saw from much closer. It is either a habit or a tell, and the book never says which.
6. He frowns. It is a small, troubled, careful frown, the frown of a man being asked about a constituent's case he hasn't been briefed on.
7. With what he believes is gentle correction: "I think you may be thinking of someone else." Give it exactly as the outline has it, said kindly.
8. The knowledge from 18, one line and no gloss: she hears the shape of that sentence before she hears its meaning. "I think we may have known the same person." Two women, three years apart, beginning the same way. Iris notices the rhyme and does not explain it. Then she waits for the metal taste of the original injury, which has come every time she has been unseen (ch02), and it doesn't come. Her mouth is dry. Keep it to a clause.
9. Whether he is lying the reader is never permitted to know, and neither is she. Stay in Iris: she looks at his face for the finding and the face gives her nothing she could testify to. Give one exact physical observation of him, such as the frown already easing or the pupils unchanged in the vanity light, and let it settle nothing.
10. **[TURN]** He glanced at the door. Not for rescue. For his minute. He was worried about the time. (In real time this falls at about forty seconds. He has never gone over. Let the reader feel that his body knows the slot even though his memory doesn't know her.)

- **Sensory lead:** taste, by its absence: dry mouth, and the metal not coming. Then sound: her own voice in the small room, flatter than his, and the ballroom's bass under it. Light from the bulbs round the dressing-table mirror lies on his face. She does not look into the mirror (the mirror motif belongs to 15, 19, 20 and 27, so keep this one an unlooked-at light source).
- **Jokes / dry observations (Act III ratio, so use one, two at most):**
  - "Two years of drafts. Out loud it took four sentences. I have dictated longer findings on a hand."
  - Reserve, on his frown: "It was the frown of a man being told about a pothole in a district he didn't represent."
- **The body:** her hands still in her pockets, the right closing once on the kit while she says the fourth sentence. Dry mouth. Her left hand moves from the page to the left-flank scar through the jacket when he says "someone else" (style 05: she touches it when she is thinking). His hands: the cuff.
- **Motifs:** voicemail (the dead woman's sentence, echoed, and the knowledge from 18); hands (his cuff, her pockets); the minute (his glance); the Marine's photograph (in his eyeline the whole time she talks: he listens to her across his brother).

## Scene 3 — The Refusal

1. The overrun begins, and only she is counting it. He brings his eyes back from the door to her with a visible, courteous effort: he is giving her time he doesn't have, and he wants her to know it.
2. He declines to be the antagonist. He does it as he does everything, generously and in whole sentences. "Whoever did that to you, I'm sorry. I mean that." He gives her sympathy as a bystander. He offers it to her as he would to a woman at a rope line with a story about her son.
3. He goes further, and it is the most self-aware he gets. He tells her he has done a great many things in his life he would do differently, and he can name them. "Most of them were votes." Render him as a man who has done many things and remembers almost none of them as wrong. He is not defensive. He is at ease. That is what is unbearable.
4. The offer: "Let me get someone for you. There are people here who are very good at this." He means staff. He means a quiet room, a person with a clipboard, a follow-up, which is to say a file. Iris hears Diane's whole function in one courtesy. One line of the knowledge from 18, unglossed: it is also exactly what she did with two voicemails. She does not draw the line. The reader does.
5. She says "No." Out loud, one word. He accepts it at once and gracefully, the way he lowered his hand in S1.
6. The syllabus does not anticipate this. State it as structure, not emotion. Lesson Three as written starts from his recognition and moves to his exposure. There is no clause for a man who will not stand where the page puts him. She knows every line of the page in her inside pocket, and none of them is addressed to someone who isn't there. Do not reproduce or quote the page (style 08).
7. The one dry line, turned on herself: she had prepared for denial, rage, bargaining, even tears. She had a contingency for a panic button. She had nothing for kindness. "I had planned for every response but the one he was best at."
8. Her right hand in her pocket on the kit. Name it plainly: the case, the syringe in it, the agent from her toxicology access (never named; Open item). The reader can see her hand. She can't yet see what it is going to do.
9. **[TURN]** I didn't yet know what I was going to do, and for the first time in the book you were ahead of me, and I knew it. Use the direct address to implicate, as style 01 allows. This is the line, and it ends the scene.

- **Sensory lead:** temperature. The room is warm, hotel-warm and over-heated for a man in a suit, and she is cold inside it (cold again since 18). The bass through the floor goes on. Texture: the case in her pocket, its hard edge and its hinge.
- **Jokes / dry observations:**
  - "I had planned for every response but the one he was best at."
  - Reserve: "He offered to find me someone. The campaign had always been very good at finding someone."
- **The body:** cold in a warm room; her right hand on the kit and the left on the page; standing very still. No hunger, no heat, no salivation. Don't name the absence. He is seated and at ease, hands loose on the arms of the chair, and he checks the door once more, briefly.
- **Motifs:** hands (hers on the kit, and the reader watching them); the syllabus page (in her pocket, unquoted, unpinned); the minute (the overrun); the Marine's photograph (still facing him, and he glances at it once as if for permission to be late); the meal after (its absence carries into the Snapper).

## Snapper

Outline minimum, in past tense: *Thirty seconds on a clock only I was counting. No appetite. I checked for it the way you check a pocket.*

Sharpened option (author's call): *Thirty seconds over, on a clock only I was counting. I put my hand in my pocket and checked for it the way you check for keys. There was the case. There was nothing else.* The literal hand in the literal pocket makes the outline's simile physical. The "case" is the kit, so the reader feels that the only thing she can find in herself is the means. If this reads as too explained, keep the outline's three sentences exactly. Either way it is the last line. Do not add "appetite" to the sharpened version: the reader supplies it.
