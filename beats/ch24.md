## Scene 1 — Provocation

1. The first line is the method, begun. Over the pale nitrile she has worn since the service door, she takes a second pair, black, out of the blazer and snaps it on, left and then right, each cuff. Her fingers are cold inside the first pair. Unlike 17's suite, this room gives the sound back (shampooed carpet, one lamp), small and domestic, and the ballroom's warm-up bass comes through the wall behind it. He watches the way he watches staff, politely, hands loose on the chair arms, waiting to be told what it is for.
2. Lesson Three as written, in one plain sentence without the syllabus voice: immobilise him, keep him conscious, pin the page, leave him alive for the women to finish. Then, short, the knowledge from 18: the eleven names on the list, less the one standing in the room (Iris), which leaves ten others; and one more, outside the eleven, who signed what she signed and died suddenly at home and will finish nobody. Nora is not subtracted from the eleven.
3. She takes his left wrist the way she takes a pulse, and he lets her. He holds it out the way men hold a wrist out for a watch. The long zip-tie comes out of the blazer lining and goes round his wrist and under the chair arm where it meets the front leg, through and tight before his face has caught up with his arm. From his shoulder she sees the photograph as he sees it: Mark in dress blues, plain black frame, on its cardboard easel, looking at the chair. His jacket stays on the chair back.
4. The hard case is in the left inside breast pocket, against the page. She opens it one-handed and takes the syringe out capped. A line of paper pressure: it came from the ME office's toxicology lab, and somewhere a log no longer agrees with the shelf; someone will count, and the count will reach a woman she knows only as E. Reyes. The lesson always needed the eleven names in a federal file and him alive to be asked about them. She takes the cap off with her thumb, drops it into the case, and puts the case back in the pocket.
5. He sees the needle and flinches. She hears it before she sees it: the back of his head taking the upholstery, the plastic pulling once at the chair arm and stopping.
6. The appetite comes back in the chapter's one long, greedy sentence, and smell comes first: up through the cedar aftershave, the shampooed carpet and the lamp dust, the sharp first note of the other sweat, the one she named in Brandt (17), rising off him into a shirt ironed for television. Her mouth fills with water. It is the first thing it has wanted since the bathroom floor, and she reports it and does not apologise.
7. She looks last, as always. It is a real flinch, orbicularis oculi closing hard round both eyes. He is frightened, and she has watched him on rally footage and a feed and in the papers and never once seen it. Nobody has ever made him be.
8. The needle goes in through his shirt, through both pairs of her gloves, a small resistance and then none, and her thumb presses the plunger. While her mouth is still full she loses the count. She has counted from the green-room door closing, through her name, his not knowing it, the four sentences and his kindness, to seventy and on through the gloves and the tie and the case, and somewhere in it there is no number. It is the first count she has ever lost, and she did not feel it go; she found it gone. Nobody opens the door. She looks at it once, a door with a latch he threw himself, and goes back to her thumb.
9. He doesn't shout. Even now his syntax holds, and he goes to the one move there is in a small room, the one he has made across a thousand tables, with a needle in him: "All right. All right. What do you want?"
10. **[TURN]** Her thumb stops on the plunger; she draws the needle out, steps back round the table to her own side and stands holding the syringe needle-up between them, thumb off it. The water has gone out of her mouth; what is left is ordinary spit, not much. He had asked what I wanted. Not what I was going to do: what I wanted. It was the first question anyone in the campaign had ever asked me, and I didn't have an answer any more.

## Scene 2 — The Crack

1. She has no number for how long the syringe takes. She watches it reach him: the tied wrist turning in the zip-tie with nothing behind it, the fingers opening on the chair arm, the shoulders going down into the upholstery. He looks at the tie, then at her. He can still talk, but every word comes set down a little after the one before. Then he works it through, visibly, the way he works a room, and arrives by himself at the only loss in the campaign he is able to feel.
2. "Is this about Diane?" His voice breaks on her name, mid-word, the way a string is cut. It is the first time in the book his syntax has failed.
3. Holt, in fragments, em-dash licence used here and only here: "She was family. I haven't slept since. Her daughter — Olivia — I was at the —". He does not finish it. The word is funeral. Iris has seen the press photograph, him beside the taller girl, both looking below the frame. She does not say it for him.
4. He weeps, and she reports it exactly: corrugator drawing the brows together, inner frontalis lifting them into the peaked shape nobody has ever produced on purpose, the corners of the mouth pulled down, the chin dimpling, tears from both eyes running from the outer corners, the free hand coming up to cover his face. She has stood on the far side of the viewing-room glass more times than she can count and knows what is performed. This was not. It was real, and she is the one person in the building qualified to sign for it. Through the wall the ballroom music stops and a microphone tells the crowd to practise their applause, and they roar, at exactly the wrong moment, and he weeps through it. (The Latin here certifies the tears; it does not relish them.)
5. He gives Diane back to her in pieces, and every piece is accurate: she stopped before certain words and people thought she was choosing them; she told him once, on a plane, about the stammer as a girl and how she had beaten it out of herself; Olivia is a sophomore at Wellesley; Diane rang her mother every Sunday at four, no matter what. Iris knew the stammer from a dossier, a profile and the pump station. He knew it because Diane told him. The rest she has too, from a lens, a feed and a trade magazine. We had both done the reading.
6. She looks for herself in it, from the top of the list and then again from the top. He is grieving the woman who filed her into a cabinet. There is nothing in it for her, and it is not meanness and not even forgetting: nothing is available. Then the finding, the way a finding arrives at the bench: the machine she spent two years writing pages about never failed to see women. It saw Diane down to the stammer. It chose which ones. His wife is in the ballroom on the other side of the applause; he does not say her name. He says Diane's again.
7. Her left hand has gone to her side without being sent, flat on the scar below the ribs through the blazer and both pairs of gloves, and stays there while he talks. Her right holds the syringe needle-up, thumb off the plunger.
8. "They still don't know who," he says. They would, from a list of eleven names, in its own order, with nobody's grief attached to any line, because a list doesn't stand beside anyone at a funeral. Nora Wade signed what Iris signed and died suddenly at home, and her obituary ran in another county's paper. Iris has no reason to think anyone from the campaign stood beside anybody at hers.
9. The syllabus assumed he would recognise the lesson; every page began there. He is grieving the Gatekeeper. Out of fifteen years' habit something in her begins to write it up, the reflex going for the pen like a hand for a light switch in a known room, and finds no hand to hold the pen. Leave the refusal unremarked.
10. **[TURN]** Warmth runs down her face. She notices it by temperature, as she noticed Brandt's blood: a line a degree or two under blood heat from the inner corner of the eye, beside the nose, then another, and they are hers. She had been cold since the bathroom floor and hadn't known there was anything that warm left to come out. He takes his hand from his face and looks at hers, wet and swollen at the lids and still kind, and nods slowly as if she had said something and he agreed. I understood he would never be taught. There was no page that reached him. The syllabus shattered from the inside, and I was crying too, and he thought we were grieving the same woman. "You knew her," he said.

- **Sensory lead:** Sound. His voice going: the break mid-word, the wet breath catching at the top, the ballroom's applause rehearsal coming through the plaster at the wrong moment. Temperature second: her own tears, warm, noticed the way she noticed Brandt's blood at thirty-seven degrees, and surprising her.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations (one at most, before the Turn):**
  - "We had both done the reading." (In the prose, closing the overlap of the two files.)
  - Reserve, cut it if it grades him: "Grief is a finding I can date to the hour. His was seven weeks old and had not been embalmed."
- **The body:** Hers: tears arriving without permission, the first since the bathroom floor in 18; the left hand flat on the flank wound through the blazer; the right holding the syringe needle-up, thumb off. His: weeping, the free hand at his face, shoulders heavy, the tied wrist turning in the zip-tie with no strength behind it, speech slow.
- **Motifs:** Hands (his at his face, hers at the wound). The Marine's photograph (he has a dead brother and does not mention him; he mentions Diane). The grade (the reflex with nowhere to go). The meal after, by echo: Diane's Sunday, the mother's call at four.

## Scene 3 — The Rupture

1. She stops. No method, no next step. I abandoned the protocol, and she tells the reader plainly so they can't say they weren't told: Lesson Three as written went on from here, and none of it was addressed to the man in the chair; it was addressed to a man who knew who she was.
2. She sets the syringe down on the dressing table beside the photograph so that her right hand is free. She does not recap it. In fifteen years she has never put down an open needle, and she puts this one down and takes her hand off it. The black nitrile makes a small dry sound against the wood.
3. The tape is still in her right outer pocket; she hasn't touched it all night. Lesson Three keeps his eyes and mouth open, a lesson about being seen and heard, and the tape was for the other two. She looks at her hands instead: black to the wrist, and clean.
4. The third page comes out of the blazer's inside breast pocket. It has been folded in thirds since the morning and she opens it along its creases with both hands. The creases hold; good paper keeps a fold the way skin keeps a line. The glove drags on it a little. Nothing on it is marked, nothing circled. Never reproduce or quote it (style 08). Never give its Evaluation. She tells the reader: you have had two of them; this one stays where it is.
5. The pin is in her right hand, taken from the same pocket without her telling her hand to, the single steel pin of 6 and 17, the page in her left. Its point presses through both pairs of gloves and breaks neither. She looks at his shirt where it would go, over the manubrium, and the word arrives in Latin, stands in her, and goes no further.
6. The knowledge from 18, one line: the only other page she has written since the syllabus has her own name on it and is at home, face-down in the drawer under the place where this one lay. Neither of them has been pinned to anyone.
7. The pin goes back into the left inside breast pocket, by her hand and not by decision, beside the capped marker and the hard case with only the cap in it. She watches it go in and looks at the empty hand too. Whatever happened next was not curriculum.
8. Direct address, once, to implicate: You know what it is before I do. (Style 01: to make the reader an accessory, not to explain.) Do not say what it is.
9. He watches her through the wet. He is heavy in the shoulders and slow in the hands, his head moving as if it weighs more, and still the best man in any small room. He has found the person who needs looking after, and it is her. He leans against the tie as far as it lets him and speaks to her in short slow pieces, the way people speak at a bedside; what reaches her is the tone, low, level, patient, the voice of a man who has sat with a great many widows. She is cold in a room warm enough for a man in shirtsleeves, and the warmth is coming off him. She does not know what the count is and has not looked. Her own tears are not reported either way.
10. **[TURN]** I put the page on the table, beside the Marine. Face-down. Her right hand stays flat on it, black glove on the white back of the sheet. He does not look at it. He reached for my hand, to comfort me: his free right hand comes across the dressing table, slow, past his brother's face, the same hand he offered when she told him her name, and this time it does not wait to be taken. It covers hers. His fingertips find the gap at her wrist between glove cuff and sleeve and rest there. Nothing came into my mouth.

- **Sensory lead:** Texture. The page (the creases, the weight of good paper, the drag of the glove on it), the pin's point through two gloves, then his hand: warm and dry over the glove, fingertips on the one bare inch of wrist, the inch that was clean in 17. Temperature closes it: he is warm; she has been cold since 18.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:** None after the S2 Turn. The absence should be felt in the rhythm. The nearest thing to wit is plain statement: "I would like to say it was a decision." (In the prose.) If the drafter needs one dry beat before the page comes out and cuts the above: "Paper remembers. It's the one thing in an office that does."
- **The body:** Her right hand: empty, then flat on the page, then covered. The pin has gone back to the pocket. The tears are not reported. No metal taste at his touch (ledger: absent here). The flank wound is not mentioned in S3. His body: warm, slow, kind. The long zip-tie holds his left wrist to the chair throughout.
- **Motifs:** The syllabus page (unpinned, face-down, never reproduced). The Marine's photograph (the page beside it, face-up still). Hands (the handshake from 23 turned into comfort, and the bare inch of wrist from 17). Tape (the vinyl unused in her right outer pocket). The minute, by absence: still no knock. The fourth page, at home.
