- **Act pressure:** Act II, The Practicum. The pressure is Brandt. He is in every scene even though he is absent for two of them: his house and his things in S1, his food and his piano in S2, his body on the feed in S3. Under it, appetite is named, indulged, and here turned towards a person as courtship. The gifts are flirtation. Iris must not call them that. The reader has to get there first (outline S1 Turn).
- **Appetite at entry:** Act II, appetite as identity, freshly named. In Ch 12 the fight was a pleasure she described as one. She killed a man she had not planned to kill. She went home with a stranger from a late bar and slept better than she has in years. She worked the Saturday rota the next day. She has not graded the dead man and never will. The knife wound on her left flank is two days old, stitched by her own hand, and she touches it when she is thinking. She is warm (Act II). Since Ch 9 she has admired Brandt as a reader of people. Since Ch 11 she has known his surveillance runs through a shell company with Diane as its officer of record.
- **Appetite at exit:** Committed and unadmitted. She has entered his house the way he entered hers, left him things, eaten from his fridge with a bare hand, and touched his piano. She chose not to time the one thing in the chapter that matters. She has listened to a whole side of a record for the first time in her life, with him, without his knowing. In Ch 8 she switched off the Mingus after a few minutes ("like being read a menu when you're hungry"). Tonight she eats the whole menu. Her right hand smells of his soap all the way home. She is still warm. She reports every one of these things and names none of them.
- **Voice rules in force:** smell first, sight last. At least one joke per page. Iris narrates long and greedy, and there is no dialogue to speak of: she is alone for the whole chapter, and Brandt speaks no words on the feed. **No mirrors** (the motif belongs to 15, 19, 20 and 27): she does not go into his bathroom, and the dark windows and the piano's black lacquer do not show her. No "beautiful" (Ch 17 only). No "perfect", "monster", "numb/hollow/empty", or "I shouldn't have enjoyed it". She never uses the word *flirting* or any synonym for it. The page is not quoted.

## Scene 1: The Visit

1. Open already inside, in the dark, in his kitchen, at about twenty past nine on a Sunday night. He is on the thirty-first floor of the Calloway working the Sunday call sheet, Diane's old Sunday job, and will be there until about midnight. She came in by the back door, with no alarm to defeat. She gives one clause to the reason: a man who knows what logs are for keeps none of his own. The deadbolt took her four minutes with a pick and left no marks. She grades herself against his entry into her flat in Ch 9 and gives herself the same mark: entry competent, no scratches on the brass. They are even.
2. Smell first. The house smells warmer than her flat, because his heating is on a timer and set two degrees higher than hers. Coffee grounds. Rye, faintly, from a bottle on the counter. Fennel and garlic from the afternoon's cooking. Under it, at the sink, the dry green cellar smell she last met at chest height by her own desk (Ch 9). It comes from a bar of soap by the kitchen tap. She does not read the wrapper. She tells the reader she wanted something left for later.
3. Diane first. From the inside pocket of her jacket she takes an 8x10 glossy print: the official scene close-up of Diane's mouth, the shredded paper at the lips, half a control number legible on one fragment. She printed it at home from the case file she opens every working day as senior reviewer. She lays it face-up on the counter beside the kettle, squared to the counter's edge. Her reasoning: he looked at this photograph for two minutes and eleven seconds on the War Room feed (Ch 8), the paper is his template (Ch 9), and he should have a picture of it in use.
4. She moves through the house by sodium light through the blinds, plus a penlight with a red lens held low. "I handle his things like I'm choosing a gift": the charcoal suits in the wardrobe, felt between finger and thumb for weight of cloth; the reading glasses on the bedside table, folded, one arm mended with tape (clear tape, not hers, which she notes); the pillow on the far side of the bed, firm and pressed, not slept on in two years. She reads the house as she would read a body, from what was used and what was not.
5. The living room. The piano is under its closed lid and she does not go to it, and says so. The narration should make the reader feel her walk past it. The records run the length of one wall, spines out, alphabetical by artist and then by recording date. Coltrane, Dolphy, Mingus, and a great deal she has never heard of. Cardboard and old paper, the faint vanilla of ageing sleeves.
6. She plants the first unit: a button-sized camera-and-microphone, the same model as the War Room pair, bought for cash. It goes on the top shelf of the records, angled down over the turntable, the drinks tray and the piano, and gets one short strip of black vinyl tape. The tape's ratchet as it comes off the roll is the loudest sound she makes in the house.
7. Choosing. She runs a gloved finger along the Dolphy spines and takes out *Out to Lunch!* for the sleeve: a clock with too many hands, a WILL BE BACK sign. She does not know what a first pressing is and does not care. She draws the disc out of its inner sleeve wrongly, gloved palms flat on the grooves, and knows it is wrong from how carefully everything else in the room has been handled. She has never owned a record, and it does not matter how you hold one you are about to break.
8. The bedroom. She plants the second unit on top of the wardrobe facing the bed, with one strip of black vinyl. Then the break, done with her trade and not her strength. She scores the disc across its diameter with a scalpel, through the label, and snaps it over the edge of his chest of drawers. Vinyl breaks along a score line the way a bone breaks along a fracture someone has already started. It makes one dry crack. Two clean halves. She lays them on his pillow, the used one, with a hairline gap between them, the split running through the label. The sleeve goes beside them, its clock face up.
9. **[TURN]** Beside the record she lays the dead contractor's driver's licence, taken from his wallet on the warehouse floor in Ch 12: Dale Garrity, 43. His photograph, the name, the small red heart that marks an organ donor. She tells the reader it is an invoice: this is what it cost, and he should see the bill. She touches the two-day-old wound on her left flank through her shirt while she says it. Then she slides the licence a quarter-inch to the left, and does not say why. The reader knows the quarter-inch is his (Ch 9) and understands before she does what she is doing in a man's bedroom with his pillow.

- **Sensory lead:** smell (the warmer house, coffee grounds, rye, the afternoon's fennel and garlic, then the cellar smell at the sink, unnamed). Sound: the boiler ticking, the fridge cycling, the ratchet of the tape, the one dry crack of the vinyl. Texture: worsted wool between finger and thumb; the sleeve's soft cardboard corners; the grooves under her gloved palms, finer than any tissue she has dissected. Temperature: two degrees warmer than her own flat, and Act II warmth on her face. Light: sodium through the blinds and a red penlight held low. No overhead lights.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**
  - "He had come into my flat and touched nothing in the kitchen. I am a better guest. I brought something."
  - On the licence: "He was an organ donor. Nobody had asked me." (Iris's register: anatomy as punchline, and a private grief she does not open.)
  - On the second pillow: "It had not been slept on in two years. You can tell, the way you can tell a lung that has never been smoked through: it is simply too pleased with itself."
- **Body:** gloved hands throughout, steady. The wound is two days old under her own stitches. It pulls once when she reaches up to the wardrobe, and she touches it at the Turn. She is warm. She is hungry and does not mention it until S2 (she had a late lunch and nothing since). Her pulse is up and she reports it as a finding, not a feeling.
- **Motifs:** Vinyl (the collection, the chosen sleeve, the break, her not knowing how to hold one). Tape (the black vinyl strips fixing both units; his clear-taped glasses as a contrast). Hands (the wrong way to hold a record; the quarter-inch nudge). The grade (her entry against his, scored even). The paperwork thread, quietly: half a control number in the photograph on his counter.

## Scene 2: His Kitchen

1. Back in the kitchen, about ten past ten. Everything is placed. She should leave, and she tells the reader so in the syllabus voice, as a protocol: egress begins when placement ends. She does not leave. She reports the not-leaving as a finding, without explaining it.
2. She opens his fridge. The light floods the kitchen and the print on the counter, and she reads the shelves the way she reads an opened abdomen, everything in its place and telling her what it does: good butter, a lemon, a jar of cornichons, eggs, half a bottle of white with a stopper, and, front and centre, a lidded enamel dish. She lifts the lid. Then she shuts the fridge on purpose, to have the dark back, with the dish in her hands.
3. Inside the dish is a pork shoulder he braised earlier that day with fennel and garlic, before he went in for the call sheet, now set cold in its own jelly and barely broached. He cooks the way people cook who used to cook for two: too much, and well. One dry line on the divorce, without naming the wife. She knows about it because he never talks about it (Ch 8).
4. She takes off her right glove. She states it as the first time she has bared a hand anywhere she should not, and she does it without deliberating. The reader keeps this for Ch 17. Iris does not flag it forward.
5. She eats standing up, in the dark, by touch, with her fingers, from the middle of the dish so that she touches nothing of his but the food. Eating standing up is how she eats when planning (style 05). Tonight there is nothing left to plan.
6. **The taste, described with the same precision as tissue.** The jelly is cold, then not, melting at the temperature of her tongue, which she gives as about thirty-five degrees. The fibres of the shoulder part along their own grain, like muscle that has been told it may. Fennel seed cracks between her molars, sweet and then camphor. His hand with the salt is heavier than hers. There is a burnt-sugar bitterness where the crust caught in his oven. She eats more than a taste and tells the reader exactly how much. She does not call it the meal after, because nobody is dead tonight, and she notes that too.
7. She washes her bare hand at his sink with his soap, dries it on a square of paper towel, and pockets the towel. She puts the glove back on. Her right hand now smells of him and will for hours. She knows it, and reports it in one line, and does not wash again.
8. She puts the dish back exactly where it was, with an exact absence in the middle of it. He will know. She tells the reader she meant him to.
9. The next room. The piano is a black baby grand with the lid down and the fallboard closed. She lifts the fallboard. The keys in the sodium light are the colour of old teeth, and she says so, because they are ivory and she knows an ivory when she sees one. She has never had a lesson. She knows from the dossier and from his fingertips (the callus she will feel in Ch 17) that his hands know where the notes are in the dark.
10. **[TURN]** She touches one key: the A above middle C, the note an orchestra tunes to, which is the one piece of music she knows. It sounds into the warm house. It is in tune. She cannot tell pitch, but she can tell a clean note from a sour one: three strings sounding together without the slow wobble of beating between them. There is no wobble. He keeps it true for nobody. She closes the fallboard over the note while it is still sounding and leaves the room before she can play more.

- **Sensory lead:** taste (the jelly melting, fennel seed, his salt, the burnt-sugar crust). Then temperature (the cold dish, her bare hand on cold enamel, the jelly giving way at tongue heat). Then sound: the fridge seal unpeeling, the seed cracking, the single A blooming into a quiet house. Light: the fridge's sudden white, then dark by choice, then sodium on the keys.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**
  - "Breaking and entering is a felony. I have never been able to find out what the charge is for eating a man's pork."
  - "I ate the way I was eventually taught anatomy: with my eyes shut, by knowing where things were."
  - On the piano: "I know one note. Everybody in an orchestra tunes to it, so it seemed the polite one to ask."
- **Body:** this scene belongs to the body. Hunger fed, standing, in the dark. The right hand is bare, then washed with his soap, then gloved again over his smell. Her mouth waters before she lifts the lid, and she reports that without comment. Warm in the face. The wound is quiet. Her fingertip is on the key and comes off it fast.
- **Motifs:** Hands (bare right hand, a pre-echo of Ch 17 that is never pointed at; the one key). The meal after, by explicit absence: she eats seriously, and it is not the meal after, and she says so. Vinyl, by adjacency: the piano is the instrument of his Tuesdays. The grade: she grades his braise and his tuning, both high, and the reader hears it as a compliment.

## Scene 3: The Letter

1. The bedroom doorway, about twenty-five to eleven. From her inside pocket she takes the second page: Lesson Two, a fresh print made at home the night before. The master stays in the drawer with *Excellent.* in red in its margin, for her and not for him. She tells the reader she does not give a student the marked script before the exam. One line.
2. She folds the page in quarters, the way you fold a letter, and lays it on the pillow beside the broken record and the licence. She does not unfold it to check it. She knows what it says. The narration does not quote it.
3. She does not stay to watch him find it. Egress at about ten to eleven: kitchen door, the deadbolt relocked with the pick so the door is exactly as she found it, then the street. She leaves no marks, as he left none at hers. Her gloves come off in the car, and her right hand smells of his soap.
4. She drives three miles to the top deck of a 24-hour hospital multi-storey and parks facing the city. She can sit there at midnight and nobody will ask why. The tablet goes on the steering wheel and both feeds come up: the living room, the bedroom.
5. At 12:26 a.m. the living-room feed hears the front door. Brandt crosses the frame in charcoal with his collar up, the way he leaves the Tabard in her long-lens photograph (Ch 8), and goes out of shot to the kitchen. There is no camera there. On the audio she hears the fridge hum and then nothing, for a long time. She knows what he is looking at. She lets the reader work out that he has also seen the dish.
6. The bedroom feed. He turns on the bedside lamp and sees the pillow. He sits down on the edge of the bed, on his side, and picks up the two halves of the record and holds them together along the break, the way you try a fracture for fit. He sits there a long time. **She does not time it.** She notices she has not timed it only afterwards, and reports it as the first thing on any feed she has failed to measure. That is the tell. Do not explain it.
7. He picks up the licence and looks at it, then looks for longer at the folded page. He unfolds it and reads it once, slowly. She can see his eyes move and knows the page takes under a minute to read (Ch 8).
8. **[TURN]** He laughs: a real, surprised laugh, his head going back, loud enough that the cheap microphone clips it. She has never heard him laugh, not in five weeks of feed. Then he goes to the living room and into the other frame. He pours a drink from the tray by the turntable, rye, neat, two fingers. He chooses a record that is not the broken one, *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*, lowers the needle on side one and plays it through, sitting in the chair by the piano with the glass and not touching the keys. In the car, three miles away, she sits and listens to the whole side with him, about twenty minutes, through a microphone the size of a shirt button. It still sounds thin, like the laptop in Ch 8. This time she does not turn it off.

- **Sensory lead:** sound (the car's engine ticking as it cools; the feed's compressed hiss; the clipped laugh; then the Mingus, thin and whole). Smell: his soap on her right hand on the steering wheel, and the car's cold vinyl and old coffee. Temperature: the car cools around her and she does not run the engine, while her face stays warm. Light: the tablet's blue, the hospital's sodium deck lights, the city beyond the parapet.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**
  - On the car park: "A hospital car park is the only place in a city where a woman can sit alone in a dark car at one in the morning and everyone who sees her assumes she has a good reason. I did."
  - On not handing over the marked copy: "You don't return a student's script with the grade on it before he has sat the paper. It only makes them careless."
- **Body:** bare hands now, on the wheel and holding the tablet. The right hand smells of him, and she lifts it to her face once without deciding to, and reports it as a finding. The cold comes into the car and she lets it, because the warmth is in her and not in the car (Act II). The wound is quiet. She does not eat, because she has eaten. Her pulse rises at the laugh, and she gives the number if she counted it. She did count that.
- **Motifs:** Vinyl (the broken Dolphy held together; the Mingus played through; she still owns none). Hands (his on the break, hers to her face). The grade: the timing she does not do is the absence of the grade, the first in the book, and a quiet pre-echo of 22–25, which the reader is not told. The page, folded, as a letter. Tape, unseen: the two black strips holding the lenses she watches him through.
