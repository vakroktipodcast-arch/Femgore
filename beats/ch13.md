## Scene 1: The Visit

1. Open already inside his back door, in the dark, at twenty past nine on a Sunday night, the pick still in her gloved hand, letting the house tell her about him before she looks at any of it. (The prose gives the smell paragraph before the Calloway one.) He is on the thirty-first floor of the Calloway working the Sunday call sheet, Diane's old Sunday job, and will be there until about midnight. She came in by the back door: no alarm and no camera over any door. She gives one clause to the reason: a man who knows what logs are for keeps none of his own. The deadbolt took her four minutes with a pick, and she checks the lock face with the penlight: no scratches on the brass, no bright crescents where a pick slipped. She compares her entry with his into her flat in Ch 9 (she has by now decided he used a pick): he came through hers without a mark, she came through his the same way. They are even. She gives neither of them a number.
2. Smell first. The house is two degrees warmer than her flat, because his heating runs on a timer; hers is "on a dial I keep low". Coffee grounds, a day old, "gone to forest floor". Rye, faintly, from a bottle on the counter. Fennel and garlic from the day. Near the sink, the dry green rooty cellar smell she last met at the height of a standing man's chest, on the brass pull of her own drawer (Ch 9). It comes off a bar of soap by the tap. She doesn't put a name to it. She tells the reader she wanted something left for later.
3. Diane first. "I am a better guest. I brought something." From the inside pocket of her jacket she takes an 8x10 glossy print: the official scene close-up of Diane's mouth, the fragments crowding at the vermilion border, half the matter number on the fragment at her lower lip, under its thin rule. She printed it from the case file (where she printed it is not said). The system logs every look she takes and expects them, because the scene set comes to the reviewer of record as a matter of course. She lays it face-up beside the kettle, squared to the counter's edge. Her reasoning: he looked at a copy of this on his phone for two minutes and eleven seconds (Ch 8), and "a man ought to see his work in the field".
4. She moves through the house by sodium light coming through the blinds in bars, plus a penlight with a darkroom-red lens held low, at her knee, in the thin black leather gloves. She didn't need to touch anything; she touches everything, "as if I were choosing him a present and wanted very much to get it right": the charcoal suits in the wardrobe, each sleeve rubbed once between finger and thumb (worsted); his reading glasses, one arm mended with clear tape ("Not my tape"); two pillows, the near one dented and soft and faintly rancid with scalp oil, the far one firm and cool, not slept on in two years. She reads the house as she would read a body, from what was used and what was not.
5. The front room. The piano is a black shape at the far end with its lid down, and she does not go to it, and knows where it is the whole time. The records run the length of one wall, spines out, alphabetical by artist and then by recording date. Coltrane, Dolphy, Mingus, and yards of names she has never heard. Cardboard and the faint vanilla of ageing sleeves (lignin breaking down, "a library dying contentedly").
6. She plants the first unit, the War Room model, on the top shelf of the records, angled down over the turntable, the drinks tray and the piano, held with one strip of black vinyl, its lead run down behind the spines to the turntable's socket strip. It will go out through his own router, on the network key from the first Lantern card. The tape coming off the roll, a small dry tearing, is the loudest sound she makes in the house.
7. Choosing. She chooses by the label, "like wine in a language I can't read", and the Dolphy she takes is *Out to Lunch!*: a clock with too many hands, a WILL BE BACK sign. She likes the joke. She draws the disc out wrongly at once, flat between both palms like a plate out of a hot oven, the leather across the grooves, and knows it is wrong from how everything else in the room has been handled. It doesn't matter how you hold one you are about to break.
8. The bedroom. She plants the second unit on top of the wardrobe facing the bed, its lead down the back of the wardrobe to the socket behind it. She has to go up on her toes, and the wound pulls once. Then the break, done with her trade and not her strength: she lays the disc on his chest of drawers, scores it with the scalpel across its whole diameter, through the label, in one pass, sets the score along the edge and presses down on the overhang. Vinyl breaks along a score the way a bone breaks along a fracture somebody has already started. One dry crack, like a knuckle. Two clean halves. Her pulse is up in the pads of her fingers. She lays them on his pillow, the used one, a hairline apart, the split running through the label. The sleeve goes beside them, its clock face up.
9. **[TURN]** Beside the halves she lays the dead contractor's driver's licence, taken off his warehouse floor two nights before (Ch 12; the wallet itself was dropped on the concrete beside him and is not in her possession) and read at her own table on the Saturday: Dale Garrity, forty-three, a heavy, bearded man, and the small pillar-box-red heart that marks an organ donor. She tells the reader it is an invoice: he sent two men to take her without damaging her, this is what it cost, and he should see the bill, itemised, with a photograph. Her fingertips have gone to the two-day-old wound on her left flank through her shirt while she thinks it. Her face is warm. Then she slides the licence a quarter of an inch to the left, and does not say why ("There was nobody to say it to"). The reader knows the quarter-inch is his (Ch 9) and understands before she does what she is doing in a man's bedroom with his pillow.

- **Sensory lead:** smell (the warmer house, day-old coffee grounds, rye, fennel and garlic from the day, then the cellar smell at the sink, unnamed). Sound: the small dry tearing of the tape, the one dry crack of the vinyl. Texture: worsted wool between finger and thumb; the disc flat between leathered palms. Temperature: two degrees warmer than her own flat, and Act II warmth on her face. Light: sodium through the blinds in bars, "the colour of weak marmalade", and a darkroom-red penlight held low. No overhead lights.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**
  - "He had come into my flat and touched nothing in the kitchen. I am a better guest. I brought something."
  - On the licence: "He was an organ donor. Nobody had asked me." (Iris's register: anatomy as punchline, and a private grief she does not open.)
  - On the second pillow: "It had not been slept on in two years. You can tell, the way you can tell a lung that has never been smoked through: it is simply too pleased with itself."
- **Body:** gloved hands throughout, steady. The wound is two days old under her own stitches. It pulls once when she goes up on her toes at the wardrobe, and she touches it at the Turn. She is warm. She is hungry and does not mention it until S2 (she had a late lunch and nothing since). Her pulse is up in the pads of her fingers and she reports it as a finding, not a feeling.
- **Motifs:** Vinyl (the collection, the chosen sleeve, the break, her not knowing how to hold one). Tape (the black vinyl strip fixing the front-room unit; his clear-taped glasses as a contrast). Hands (the wrong way to hold a record; the quarter-inch nudge). The even entries (hers against his, no scratches either side; no number for either). The paperwork thread, quietly: half the matter number in the photograph on his counter.

## Scene 2: His Kitchen

1. Back in the kitchen, at ten past ten. Everything is placed. She should leave, and she tells the reader so in the terms of her work: when the last pot is labelled, you strip your gloves and leave the suite; anyone still in there after that is there for herself. He will be home at about midnight. She stands with her back to his counter, beside the print, and does not leave. She reports the not-leaving ("I am reporting it"), without explaining it.
2. She opens his fridge. The light comes out over everything, white and total, and she reads the shelves the way she reads an opened abdomen, everything in its place and telling her what it is for: good butter, a lemon, a jar of cornichons, eggs, half a bottle of white with a stopper, and, front and centre, where a man puts the thing he means to come back to, a lidded enamel dish. Her mouth waters before she has the lid off (late lunch, nothing since). Then she shuts the fridge on purpose, to have the dark back, with the dish in her hands.
3. Inside the dish is a pork shoulder braised that morning with the fennel and the garlic, set cold in its own jelly. One end is already carved, a few slices gone at lunch. It is far too much pork for one man. He cooks the way people cook who used to cook for two: too much, and well. One dry line on the divorce, without naming the wife: "The recipe had outlived the marriage."
4. She takes off her right glove and pockets it. She decides it, and does the sum first: her DNA is on the office's elimination register like every pathologist's, and he will wash her off his dish himself by morning. It is the first time in her life she has bared a hand anywhere she should not have been. The reader keeps this for Ch 17. Iris does not flag it forward.
5. She eats standing up, in the dark, by touch, from the end he has already broached, so that whatever she takes comes off his own cut. She eats as she palpates, with her eyes shut, by knowing where things are. Eating standing up is how she eats when planning (style 05). Tonight there is nothing left to plan.
6. **The taste, described with the same precision as tissue.** The jelly is cold, then not: collagen given back as gelatin, melting at about thirty-five degrees, which is her tongue, solid to gravy with nothing in between. The fibres of the shoulder part along their own grain, the way muscle parts on the table once the fascia is open and it has been told that it may. A fennel seed cracks between her molars, sweet and then camphor, the cold anise going up the back of her nose. His hand with the salt is heavier than hers. At the edge, where the crust caught in his oven, a bitterness like burnt sugar, "the best of it". She grades the braise: "Distinction. I docked nothing for the salt." She tells the reader exactly how much she ate: about a hundred and fifty grams, two slices' worth and the jelly round them, about a kidney's weight ("I'm giving you the number because you'll want it"). It was not the meal after, because nobody is dead tonight, and she has no word for the other kind.
7. She tidies. With one bare fingertip she smooths the cut face of the pork and draws the skin of the jelly back over where she took from it, the way you bring a wound edge to edge, wipes the rim of the dish with a square of his paper towel, puts the dish back in its place and shuts the door on its light. He will not know. She tells the reader she meant him not to: everything else in the house she left for him to find, and billed; this is the one thing she kept off the invoice. (Ledger: he never learns what she ate.)
8. She turns his tap with her gloved wrist and washes her bare hand with his soap, and calls that a decision too: the bar is worn to a lozenge, and his first wash will take her off it. The lather comes up thin and dry and smelling of the cellar; she works it into the webs of the fingers as she scrubs at work. She dries the hand on the same square of towel, pockets it, and puts the glove back on over the smell of him. Her right hand will smell of him for hours. She knows it, and does not wash again.
9. The next room: "this time I went to it." The piano is a black baby grand with the lid down and the fallboard closed. She lifts the fallboard; it comes up without a sound, which is the sound of a thing somebody looks after. The keys in the sodium light are the colour of old teeth. They are ivory, and she knows it ("A tusk is a tooth, and I have spent fifteen years looking at what people smile with"). She has never had a piano lesson. His hands know where the notes are in the dark. Hers know where the aorta is.
10. **[TURN]** She presses one key with one gloved fingertip: the A above middle C, the note an orchestra tunes to, which is the one note she knows. She counts up to it the way you count down from the angle of Louis to find a rib. It goes out into the warm house "like a drop of ink into water". It is in tune. She cannot name a pitch by ear, but she can tell a clean note from a sour one: three strings sounding together without the slow wobble of beating between them. There is no wobble. He keeps it true for nobody, in a house with nobody in it. She lifts her finger, the damper comes down, and she closes the fallboard over the note while it is still sounding and leaves the room before she can play more.

- **Sensory lead:** taste (the jelly melting, fennel seed, his salt, the burnt-sugar crust). Then temperature (the cold dish, the jelly giving way at tongue heat). Then sound: the seed cracking, the single A going out into a quiet house, the damper coming down. Light: the fridge's sudden white, then dark by choice, then sodium on the keys.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**
  - "Breaking and entering is a felony. I have never been able to find out what the charge is for eating a man's pork."
  - "I ate as I palpate, with my eyes shut, by knowing where things were."
  - On the piano: "I know one note. Everybody in an orchestra tunes to it, so it seemed the polite one to ask."
  - On tidying: "A burglar takes something. I smoothed the jelly."
- **Body:** this scene belongs to the body. Hunger fed, standing, in the dark. The right hand is bare, then washed with his soap, then gloved again over his smell. Her mouth waters before she lifts the lid, and she reports that without comment. The wound is quiet. Her gloved fingertip is on the key and comes off it fast.
- **Motifs:** Hands (bare right hand, a pre-echo of Ch 17 that is never pointed at; the one key). The meal after, by explicit absence: she eats seriously, and it is not the meal after, and she says so. Vinyl, by adjacency: the piano is the instrument of his Tuesdays. The grade: she gives his braise "Distinction", nothing docked for the salt; his tuning is described, not marked. The reader hears it as a compliment.

## Scene 3: The Letter

1. The bedroom doorway, at twenty-five to eleven, the page in her hand: Lesson Two, a fresh print made at her desk the night before (Sat 3 Oct). The master stays in the drawer with *Excellent.* in red in its margin, in his hand; that one is for her. She does not return a student's script with the grade on it before he has sat the paper. One line.
2. She folds the page in quarters, runs her thumbnail down each crease, and lays it on the pillow beside the halves and the licence. She does not unfold it to check it. She has known what it says for two years. The narration does not quote it.
3. She does not stay to watch him find it. Egress: out through the kitchen, past the print and the fridge "and what the fridge knew", crouched on his step relocking the deadbolt with the pick (faster than unlocking, because the pins have already told her everything), so the door is exactly as she found it. At ten to eleven she is walking down his lane "with nothing of his on me but the smell". Her gloves come off in the car, and her right hand on the wheel smells of his soap.
4. She drives three miles to the hospital multi-storey that never shuts, takes a ticket at the barrier that she will pay in cash at the machine, and parks on the top deck by the parapet, facing the city, under the sodium deck lights. She can sit there at one in the morning and nobody will ask why. She props the tablet against the wheel, puts it on the hospital's open guest Wi-Fi, and brings up both feeds: the front room, the bedroom.
5. At 12:26 a.m. the front-room feed hears his front door. Brandt crosses the frame in charcoal with his collar up, as he comes out of the Tabard in the photograph in her drawer (Ch 8), and goes out of the far side towards the kitchen. There is no camera there. On the audio she hears the fridge's hum through the wall and then nothing, for a long time. She knows what he is looking at: the print by the kettle. She listens for the fridge seal and does not hear it, and notes that this is not the same as it not happening. The dish is in its place either way.
6. The bedroom feed. The bedside lamp comes on, low and yellow; he comes in, sees the pillow, stops, and sits down on the edge of the bed, on his own side. He picks up the two halves, one in each hand, and puts them together along the break, the way you try a fracture for fit: the ends offered to each other, a small press, a small turn, until they seat. He sits there a long time. **She does not time it.** She notices she has not timed it only afterwards (she timed his War Room stillness to the second, Diane on her bench to the minute, her own eggs), and reports it as the first thing on any feed she has failed to measure. That is the tell. Do not explain it.
7. He puts on the taped reading glasses and picks up the licence. He reads the name, Dale Garrity, and the age, and his face does nothing: a man reading a stranger's name off a card. It means nothing to him, and she can watch him not know it. He looks for longer at the folded page. He unfolds it and reads it once, slowly. It takes under a minute; she knows, because she wrote it. He folds it again along her creases and puts it in his inside breast pocket, on the left, and slides the halves and the licence into the Dolphy sleeve (ledger).
8. **[TURN]** He laughs: a real, surprised laugh that gets out before he has been consulted, his head going back, loud enough that the cheap microphone on the wardrobe clips it into white noise. She has never heard him laugh, not once in five weeks of feed. Her pulse goes up, and she counts it at the left wrist with two fingers of the right, the hand that smells of him: ninety-two, against a resting sixty-four. Then he goes into the front room, into the other frame, and puts the Dolphy sleeve back in its place on the wall. At twenty to one he pours a drink from the tray by the turntable, rye, neat, two fingers. He chooses a record that is not the broken one, *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*, lowers the needle on side one and sits down in the chair by the piano with the glass on his knee, not touching the keys. In the car, three miles away, she listens to the whole side with him, about twenty minutes, the first whole side she has ever heard through. It sounds no better than the first time, in September, through the clean laptop (Ch 8): "three miles through a button, thin, a big band heard through a wall". This time she does not turn it off. Once, without deciding to, she lifts her right hand to her face. It still smells of his soap.

- **Sensory lead:** sound (the car's engine ticking as it cools; the feeds hissing a little; the clipped laugh; then the Mingus, thin and whole). Smell: his soap on her right hand on the steering wheel, and the car's cold vinyl and old coffee. Temperature: the car cools around her and she lets it, because it isn't where she is keeping the warmth. Light: the tablet, the hospital's sodium deck lights, the city in the windscreen beyond the parapet.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**
  - On the car park: "A hospital car park is the only place in a city where a woman can sit alone in a dark car at one in the morning and everyone who sees her assumes she has a good reason. I did."
  - On not handing over the marked copy: "You don't return a student's script with the grade on it before he has sat the paper. It only makes him careless."
  - On the soap: "I own no scented products. For once in my life I smelled of something, and it was his."
- **Body:** bare hands now, on the wheel, the tablet propped against it. The right hand smells of him, and she lifts it to her face once without deciding to, and reports it as a finding. The cold comes into the car and she lets it, because the warmth is in her and not in the car (Act II). The wound is quiet. She does not eat, because she has eaten. Her pulse rises at the laugh, and she gives the number: 92, against her resting 64 ("That one I counted").
- **Motifs:** Vinyl (the broken Dolphy held together; the Mingus played through; she still owns none). Hands (his on the break, hers to her face). The timing she does not do: the first thing on any feed she fails to measure, a quiet pre-echo of 22–25, which the reader is not told. The page, folded, as a letter. Tape, unseen: the black strip holding the front-room lens she watches him through; his clear-taped glasses.
