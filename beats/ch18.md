## Scene 1 — The Collapse

1. Open in the car, in the printworks' rear car park, at about 2:15 a.m. Her hands are on the wheel, red to the wrist, picked up straight from the Ch 17 Snapper. Now she looks. The blood on the backs of her hands has gone from cherry to oxblood and is setting in the creases of her knuckles. The right is worse, still tacky past the wrist where the glove came off for the heart. There are prints on the rim of the wheel at ten and two, and a thumb on the gear knob with the whorl clear enough to classify. She reports it as a scene examiner would. It is the first time tonight she has seen herself as evidence.
2. Competence before collapse, because of the hero rule. From the door pocket she takes the tub of 70% isopropyl wipes she keeps in every car she has owned. One wipe each for the wheel, the gear knob, the indicator stalk and the inner door handle (four, ledger), each folded in on itself and dropped into the bag with the towels. She notes it is a stopgap, since alcohol fixes blood and doesn't lift it. The tub goes into her jacket pocket with the phone. Then she pulls a fresh pair of nitrile gloves on over the blood. She covers it rather than cleaning it, and she notes the difference.
3. The drive home, about thirty minutes, in sodium by the river and then the blue-white of the ring road. She keeps to the limit exactly. The elation is still in her body like a second drink, but it has stopped reaching her face. Under it, on a loop, his voice: *She died eight months later.* She can't hear the name. She can hear where the name was, the way a tongue finds where a tooth was.
4. Home, about 2:45 a.m. She puts the bags down inside the front door beside her shoes, like shopping, and walks away from them. This is the first breach of procedure, and the reader should catch it against Ch 7's "procedure first. The bag inside the bag goes where it goes". She goes straight into the bathroom and turns on the cold fluorescent over the basin. She doesn't turn the shower on. No forty-minute shower. She sits down on the tile with her back against the side of the bath, still in her suite clothes, knees up and gloved hands on them, and doesn't get up for six hours.
5. The cold comes back. The weather has turned while she was inside, and the tile pulls the heat out through her thighs and palms, the enamel through her spine. This is the old cold, the unchosen kind that went away in September, returning to a body that has been warm since, and she knows it the way you know someone's step on the stair. She shivers. She reports it as rigors, then corrects herself, because rigors want a fever. It is shivering thermogenesis, the muscle contracting against itself, a sign the body hasn't given up on the house. The correction is the last clean sentence for a while.
6. The cold cracks and she breaks. The register reaches for it: the diaphragm going, lacrimal glands, the glottis closing and opening on air the body pushes out of its own accord, a sound below decision. These are the words she used about Brandt's sounds a few hours ago, and she hears that they are the same words. Then the words stop. The sentences go short. It goes on. She doesn't stop it and doesn't try. Her nose runs and she wipes it on her sleeve. It goes on for a long time. Nothing in the narration ranks it as a first, only or last.
7. Sound is what the scene is built on. His suite kept every sound and gave none back. Her bathroom is tile and enamel and a frosted window and gives back every one, a fraction late, off the walls and bath and cistern, so she hears herself the way he never got to hear himself in that room. For once there's a witness in the room, and it is her.
8. Under the crying is the thing she can't stop seeing: not him, her. How happy she was, exactly, while he said it. She plays it back like footage, a frame at a time and in order, because order is what she has. The smell of the open chest first, warm iron and the butcher's-counter sweetness, with the vetiver over it. Then the fourth costal cartilage going under the shears with the sound of celery, and a name going under it. The lung on that side letting go of the wall. *I'm sorry. I thought you should know.* Then the confession, made exact: she was happy in the same seconds, pulse loud in her ears, mouth watering, the heat low down she had put in the same column as his sweat. Then the heart in her bare palm and her own voice thinking *three hundred and eighty grams, give or take*. Then the stairs, two at a time. Then the chicken. She was thinking about the chicken. She does not grade any of it. There is nothing to write in the margin.
9. The hours pass, measured by the body rather than the clock. The blood under the gloves finishes drying and cracks along the knuckle creases when she makes a fist, flaking like paint off a wet-room ceiling. At some point she peels the gloves off inside out, the way she took off the right one in the suite, and lets them lie on the tile. At seven, through the wall, a neighbour's shower starts, someone else's ordinary morning. Nobody will ring from work (the day off, booked on the Friday). Her hair is against her face and the suite is in it: vetiver, rye, warm dust, him, with the isopropyl sharp on top. Her left flank pulls where she is folded against the bath, stitches out two days, and she presses it once with the heel of her hand and thinks nothing. The frosted window goes from black to the colour of dishwater. The mirror is above her the whole time and she never looks up into it.
10. **[TURN]** At about 8:50 a.m., when she can stand, she stands facing the door, and the first thing she reaches for is her phone, still in its foil sleeve in her jacket pocket. Not to call anyone.

- **Sensory lead:** Sound first. Her own crying returned by the tile, late and close. The tick of the cistern. A neighbour's shower through the wall at seven. Then temperature: the cold through the tile, the cold enamel of the bath against her spine. Smell: the suite in her hair, with the isopropyl from the wipes sharp on top of it (and the solvent biting up behind her eyes in the car). Texture: dried blood cracking at the knuckle creases. Light by source: the basin fluorescent, then grey dawn through frosted glass.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations (on herself):**
  - "I have attended a great many bathroom floors professionally. It is where the body goes when it wants to do something without a witness, and the body is always wrong about that."
  - "I had timed every shower of my adult life. If you want one finding for the night, it is that I didn't take this one."
  - "Tile is the only honest surface in a flat. It tells you exactly what temperature you are."
  - In the car: "I drive at ten and two, like a woman who has never once been pulled over."
- **Body:** Hands first: red, then gloved over, then cracking, then bare. No tremor (ledger: none after kills). The cold returns and becomes shivering, then crying. Her pulse and breath are no longer counted, and the absence of counting is the finding. She is hungry, unfed, and doesn't mention it. The left flank scar pulls where she is folded against the bath; she touches it once with the heel of her hand and doesn't think anything, which the reader should feel as wrong. She is in her clothes from the suite.
- **Motifs:** Hands (red to the wrist, then gloved over the blood). Cold (the return). Voicemail (his spoken report of it, replayed). The meal after (the chicken in the replay, still not taken). The grade, by refusal: she grades nothing. No mirror, deliberately.

## Scene 2 — The Truth Confirmed

1. Before she opens anything, she slides the phone out of the foil and sees her own prints in dried blood on the glass, a thumb and the side of a forefinger, ridges up. Her hands were on her wet face for hours, and the blood on them remembered it was blood. Procedure comes back all at once, the way feeling comes back into a foot. She lays the phone face-down on the rim of the bath. She washes her hands at the basin, nails and webs and backs and up past the wrists, the water running rust, then rose, then clear, her eyes on the plughole. She dries them. She cleans the phone and the inside of the foil with a wipe from the tub in her pocket and puts the foil with the bags. She resumed the protocol before the knowledge, and that order tells the reader something.
2. Mid-morning, at the writing desk, the locked drawer under her knees. The flat is cold. The heating was already off and she leaves it off (it stays off to Ch 27). Her feet are bare on the boards and her knuckles are raw where the blood cracked and the nailbrush finished the job. She opens the county voicemail app. Then the folder she has never opened: Archive.
3. It holds two messages. She keeps everything. Mara once said she would archive a sneeze. In her years of county service she has returned every call or saved it, and she has pressed 7 exactly twice, and both times she was busy. She tells the reader this as the most damning statistic she has ever compiled.
4. The top message is Wednesday 30 September, 11:38 a.m., forty-seven seconds, marked as read, fourteen days old to the day. She doesn't play it. She knows what is on it: a woman saying sorry twice, men outside her house, and behind her a dog shifting its claws on a hard floor.
5. The second is from three years earlier: 17 September, 4:52 p.m., thirty-four seconds, marked as read. She plays it. A breath first, then an office behind it (a phone ringing at someone else's desk, a door), and a young voice, careful with itself, small out of the speaker in a cold room: "My name's Nora Wade." That is where the name surfaces, in the woman's own mouth, and Iris hears it a second time under the sound of celery breaking. "I think we may have..." Then a single stop, the catch she heard once before on a concrete floor. Then "...known the same person. I just wanted to talk." Then a number said slowly, for someone who might be writing it down. She wasn't writing it down. She has never rung it. Don't explain the pause, and don't let Iris name Diane (ch06 carries out).
6. She knows the rest of that day without remembering it, the way you know the route to work. She played the message once, the next morning, between cases. She pressed 7. She knows she ate lunch, because she always eats lunch. She doesn't know what it was, and for a woman who can tell you everything she has eaten since August, that gap is the finding.
7. On the clean laptop, never the phone, she searches the name. It takes one page. An obituary in a newspaper in another county: Nora Elizabeth Wade, 27, "died suddenly at home" on 21 May of the following year. That is eight months later, as he said. Iris knows what "suddenly" means at twenty-seven. It is one of two things, she has signed both, and she won't pick. She could ask that county for the file and doesn't, because a request is a document and documents are read. She closes the laptop.
8. She unlocks the drawer and takes out the Lesson Two master. *Excellent.* is in red beside the subject line, in her hand, and below it *Tessa Quinn* in black, struck through with one line ruled against Brandt's dossier. She lays it beside the phone, the struck name next to the two messages, and it is very simple, the way the bad ones are once the bloods come back. *I did it twice.* Then the arithmetic: Nora had been one of them. Brandt said eleven names and Iris counted herself in and stopped counting. A list of eleven had no room on it for a woman dead two years and more. Nora had signed what Iris signed, found the one other woman she could find, and rung her at work, and the other woman was Iris. The designed failure included her. Not as material but as a part. She had been running it, and she had been eating well the whole time. She names the meal after Tessa's message, the cold lamb with the fat set white, the salsa verde sharp with caper, anchovy and too much lemon, eaten sitting at her desk at work with the fork she keeps in that drawer, every mouthful, as good as it had been the night before. Fourteen days on, she can still itemise it. That is the bill Ch 10 promised.
9. Then the Lesson One text, recited from memory by choice. The red-annotated master is in the drawer under her hand and she doesn't take it out; she wants to know whether she still has it by heart: *Function: to convert the complaint into the file, the file into the folder, the folder into the cabinet, the cabinet into the room no one enters.* Key 7, the archive, the folder nobody opens. She wrote that page two years ago with the first message already in the folder. That is all she says about it. Her stomach makes a long plain noise in the quiet (borborygmi); she notes it and stays where she is.
10. **[TURN]** I had been the Gatekeeper. My page would read: *Confused process for protection.* She takes a blank sheet from the printer tray. In black ink, in the small upright hand she keeps for case numbers, with the nib dragging a little on the paper and her hand steady all the way down (which she would have preferred it not to be), she writes it on the page. The narration does not reproduce the page. It says only that her name is on it, that she doesn't circle anything, and that her desk red pen stays capped in the pen tray. I didn't pin it to anything.

- **Sensory lead:** Sound: the two voices through the phone's small speaker in a cold, quiet flat. Tessa's is not replayed, only remembered. Nora's has a breath before it, office noise behind it, and the catch. Her stomach's noise. Texture: the master page soft at the corners, the slight drag of the nib on blank printer paper, raw knuckles. Temperature: the first cold morning of autumn, her feet bare on the floorboards. Light: grey window light, then the laptop's white on her face. Water running rust, rose, clear at the basin.

## Scene 3 — The Choice

1. Afternoon. She sits at the desk with the page face-up in front of her and does what she does with a body she doesn't yet understand, which is lay it out. The syllabus is not justice. It is a way never to face that she could have helped before, twice, and both times pressed a key.
2. The second thing the syllabus is, stated without euphemism: a way to have what she actually wanted, which was this, all of it. The weeks of planning standing at the counter, the suite, the record turning, his skin opening behind the blade, the heat of him through the nitrile at thirty-seven degrees, flexor digitorum superficialis sliding in its sheath, pectoralis major moving when he breathed, his heart in her bare hand, working and then not. She lists them as she would list findings, Latin for the parts and Anglo-Saxon for the wanting, and takes back none of it.
3. The clean exit, laid out the way she laid out the syllabus, in steps. Stop. Do not destroy the kit: bag it, seal it, label it and keep it, the way you keep a specimen for the other side's expert. Go to the field office and ask at the desk for Special Agent E. Reyes, the initial and surname from the foot of the letter to counsel she read on Thu 8 Oct at ten past one (Ch 14). Let it come out in a courtroom, where it can't be managed. Let the other ten be believed, the dead one too (Nora, whom she counts among the signatories; correct per ledger, and the narration neither confirms nor explains it). She touches the scar through her shirt while she writes the last step, because she is thinking. She grades the plan honestly: the best plan she has ever written.
4. She makes tea. Getting the milk means opening the fridge, and the chicken is on the bottom shelf in its brine, under a plate, waiting for the oven she promised it. The skin has gone white and puckered and loose, the way a hand does after hours in a bath, which the trade calls washerwoman's skin, and she knows to the hour how long a hand takes to get like that. She takes the milk and closes the door. Keep this to a single, purposeful opening; Ch 19's Snapper owns the open-and-close. I didn't eat. She makes the tea strong, with milk (the Ch 7 habit), and drinks it standing at the window. It is too hot and takes a layer off the roof of her mouth, then tannic, the dry furred grip of it on her gums, then cold, and she finishes it. The heat goes out of the mug into her palms and out of her palms into the room, and the flat takes it and gives nothing back. There is no plate, and nothing is said about the absence of one.
5. She turns her head and the suite is in her hair: vetiver, rye, warm dust, him. In Ch 17, on the stairs, she turned her head more than she needed to. Now she does it on purpose, slowly, the way you smell a glass before you are allowed to drink it, and her mouth does what it did in the suite. She wants it. That is what decides her. Let the body decide before the sentence does; the sentence comes after, like paperwork.
6. I was going to go on because I wanted to. Not because the philosophy held, which she has just watched fail on her own desk in her own handwriting. Because she wanted to. Keep this plain or plainer. Then she tells the reader it is the most honest sentence in the book, and that the reader should keep it next to the other word, the one from the suite. They are a pair.
7. Continuing is shown as procedure, resumed. The clothes from the suite go into the bags with the towels, not into her machine (heat sets blood, and a drum keeps everything it is given). The bags inside the front door go where they go; disposal is not shown on the page and stays her procedure, as in Ch 7. The instruments are cleaned at the sink, dried and rolled, the rib shears last, the way they went in. Enzyme cleaner and peroxide go down the sink and the basin, because she ran blood down both. The car will get peroxide and a night of its own. She doesn't wash her hair. She grades the exit procedure: competent. She doesn't grade herself.
8. Early evening. She dresses for the late bar in the business hotel by the river, the one from after Ch 12. She gets as far as her front door, hand near the deadbolt, the hall mirror at her shoulder, and does not look into it, and does not go. She says why, plainly: going would mean washing the suite out of her hair, and she isn't finished with it. (This is style 05's second encounter "after 17", declined.) She takes her shoes off in the hall and goes back to the desk.
9. The light outside goes to sodium, block by block. Hunger is present and refused, and her stomach makes its noise again; she logs it as a finding: hungry, refused. She turns the page face-down on the desk. She doesn't lock it in the drawer. It isn't part of the syllabus, so it doesn't go in with the syllabus.
10. **[TURN]** At twenty to seven the phone lights up on the desk beside the face-down page. Mara: "Hope the day off was nice. Saved you a coffee, drank it for you. x" I didn't reply. (This echoes Ch 14's unanswered album text, without comment. This time she doesn't play anything.)
