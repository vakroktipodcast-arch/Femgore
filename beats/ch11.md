## Scene 1: Recon

1. Open in the car, Thursday morning, parked across the service road from a single-storey unit in the warehouse district: brick to hip height, ribbed steel above. There's no sign over the door, but there is a leasing agent's board wired to the chain-link fence. She's eating a char siu pork bun from the bakery at the corner, in her car, at 7:40 a.m. First page: she is already on the job. (She has told the office she has a morning appointment and will be in by ten.)
2. How she got here, in one compressed paragraph. Since Ch 9 she has been doing to him what he did to her. Ten days ago (about Mon 21 Sep) she followed his car from the War Room's hotel garage to this street at 11 p.m. and watched him badge in at a side door and stay forty minutes. She came back once in daylight, Thu 24 Sep, to watch the building from outside. That morning (1 Oct, not before) it's paperwork, which is her medium. The leasing board names only the managing agent, but the city's register of permits, for the fenced diesel generator, names the occupier: Lantern Data Services LLC. ("Nobody on earth can hide a diesel generator from a permit office.")
3. The building, by smell before sight. Through the cracked window: the roof condensers breathing out hot dust and the faint ozone of electronics, a smell like the inside of a hairdryer. Then diesel from the generator cage, and wet cardboard from the bins of the unit next door. The bun is char siu, the glaze lacquered and sticky, the dough steamed soft and slightly sweet. She wipes her fingers on a napkin before she touches the laptop.
4. The register chain on her clean laptop, tethered to a prepaid hotspot (her own phone is at home in its foil sleeve, where it stays until the night's work is done). The city's register of permits first, then the state's register of companies. Lantern Data Services is wholly owned by Ostler Holdings LLC. Ostler is owned by Brightwater Row Partners LLC. Brightwater Row is owned by Fenwick Lane Holdings LLC. Three holding companies deep. She grades the structure with an anatomical joke: three layers, like the meninges, dura, arachnoid and pia, and none of them difficult if you know where the seams are. She gives the structure "a good mark": the work of a man who had read the anatomy and assumed nobody else had.
5. The clone, reported in one paragraph as already done ("The badge was already in my tote"). At 7:20, at the pickup end of the arterial-corner bakery counter, she stood beside a man in a hi-vis vest with his badge on a reel clip at his hip, level with her tote, as she had on the 24th, and while he waited the card reader in the tote took his card's number "the way a swab takes a cheek". He bought two coffees, the second for someone; she notes it and lets it go. Her competence is stated, not dwelt on.
6. The technician. At 8:05 the climate contractor's white van pulls in at the kerb, as it does every Thursday, and the man with the two coffees goes to the side door, touches his badge to the reader and, in the glass vestibule beyond it, keys a code into a panel on the wall. The binoculars are the ones she bought for herons: they don't resolve digits through two panes of glass and a road, they resolve a hand. His goes straight down the middle of the keypad, top to bottom, four presses: the midline. People choose incisions the way she would: the line of least resistance, the one that heals.
7. The operator's pattern, in a sentence. At about 8:15, after the technician, one man, mid-thirties, backpack, no breakfast, badges in at the side door, as he did on the 24th. No guard, no second car. A camera over the door and, presumably, something inside, and not one living person watching. Brandt has built a room that watches itself, and she admires it the way you admire a well-made bone saw. (His leaving at six belongs to S2.)
8. The top of the chain. Fenwick Lane Holdings filed its annual report eleven months before: one officer. The signature has no hesitation in it. She sees the signature before she reads the name, because she looks at the hand first, and she knows it: she sat across a desk from that hand for forty minutes three years ago while it watched hers, and it countersigned the agreement (her NDA) in her drawer.
9. **[TURN]** Officer of record: Diane Pell. The Gatekeeper had kept the Architect's door. She was the pia, the one that sticks. Iris laughs out loud, alone, in a parked car with her mouth full of pork bun, and laughs until she has to put the laptop on the passenger seat so as not to drop it.
10. The tail of the Turn, not a new beat of plot. Still wiping her eyes, she checks the other three companies. Eighteen days dead, Diane is the officer of record of four companies, "which is more than most people manage alive." The register hasn't been told, and Iris knows which of those two facts is on file where. Leave it there.

- **Sensory lead:** smell (condenser exhaust: hot dust, ozone, the hairdryer note; generator diesel; wet cardboard). Taste: the char siu bun, glaze and steamed dough. Sound: the roof condensers' steady roar, which never stops, because the room inside never sleeps. Temperature: a mild first-of-October morning, with the building's warm breath coming across the road through the cracked window. Say nothing about cold.

## Scene 2: The Infiltration

1. The same Thursday night. She left the office at five, was back across the service road by ten to six, and watched the operator come out with his backpack at 6:03 p.m.; then she moved the car two streets away and waited for dark. At 9:05 p.m. she tethers the tablet and opens the War Room feed: Brandt at the glass corner table, jacket on, phone out, going nowhere yet. He is across the city on the 31st floor and she can't watch him once she's inside. She plans to use forty minutes. The pressure is the feed she is about to leave behind in the car.
2. Dress and kit, laid out on the passenger seat like an instrument tray: workwear from a trade-supply shop matching the climate contractor's (hi-vis vest, cap, no logo she would have had to explain), nitrile gloves, the cloned card, a foil survival blanket folded flat, the head torch with its oxblood filter, tension wrench and pick, a blade and a roll of black vinyl, glassine envelopes, and the tap in a padded pouch. Her phone is still at home in its sleeve (S1); the tablet stays in the car. At 9:40 she walks in with her head down, cap brim to the door camera, "the only camera I knew of". To it she is a cap with a vest under it.
3. The side door. The cloned card turns the reader green on the first try. In the vestibule she keys the midline into the panel, two, five, eight, zero, and the panel gives a small polite tone and accepts her. "Breaking and entering is mostly entering. In my trade the breaking is done by the saw, and nobody has ever thanked the saw."
4. The server hall, smell first. Heat comes out to meet her like an oven opened to baste: close, dry air from the racks' exhaust, warm metal, a burnt-dust note like a radiator's first autumn switch-on, the sweetish plastic of new cable. Sound: fans in their hundreds, a wall of white noise "you could have leaned a ladder on", a room you could scream in all night and be filed as a cooling fault; she notes it and moves on. Temperature: the hot aisle is near 30 degrees and she's sweating under the vest within a minute, at the collar and down the spine. The ceiling lights wake on motion and stay dark for her: in the oxblood torchlight she reads the sensor lenses up among the cable trays (small, pale, domed, like the eyes of something blind) as a floor plan, every lens a cone she will not stand in. She also reads the trays for cameras, the smoked domes at the row ends, and finds none: he paid for a room that notices movement, not one that remembers faces. She walks the gaps between the cones, left, straight, left, along the backs of the racks, the route she will reuse (ch12). It's the warmest room she has ever been in where she was not allowed to be, and she likes it.
5. She says it plainly: she is enjoying herself, "without any help from adrenaline", which she has blamed for a great deal and which is not involved. "The last time I had enjoyed a room this much there was a liver in my hands." This is the chapter's declaration of appetite as identity.
6. At the end of the hall a glass-fronted office looks back over the racks: the room the operator runs. Above its doorway a second sensor is mounted on the ceiling, a white box with its own small light, its lens cut to hang a curtain down over the threshold and the first three metres of floor. A second lock on a second door: the room inside the room, and she gives Brandt very high marks for it. She knows the kind: it counts. One pulse is a maybe; it wants a second inside a few seconds before it believes anything.
7. The near-trip, taken on purpose. There is no way in but through the curtain, so she steps into the doorway and lets it have its maybe: the light flickers amber, once. She stops mid-stride, weight on the ball of her right foot, heel not yet down, and holds it. Soleus, the slow postural one, will hold the ankle in plantar flexion a long while; gastrocnemius, built for sprinting and showing off, will not, and it is gastrocnemius that burns. She counts, the way she counts a pulse. Her own is up, filed as a finding, and the finding is appetite, not fear. The flicker stops. Nothing sounds.
8. The recovery, by her own competence. The sensor doesn't see a body; it sees change, something warm crossing something cooler. So she gives it no change: she lets the heel down a millimetre at a time and very slowly shakes out the foil blanket (the phone sleeve's big sister; its crackle is the loudest thing she does all night, and the fans eat it), holds it at arm's length like the sheet at a viewing, and crosses the office at the speed of the slowest thing she knows, a body cooling. "I have timed a great many people losing a degree an hour. I could go slower than that." It takes her four minutes to cross three metres. The light stays dark.
9. The office, sight last, and all of it past the edge of the curtain, in the sensor's blind spot. One desk, one chair, two dark monitors (giving back the racks' green and amber pinpricks and nothing else), a keyboard, a mug with a ring of cold coffee drying in the bottom, for which she docks the operator, and against the wall a gunmetal four-drawer steel filing cabinet with a wafer lock "of the kind they put on cabinets so that nobody feels foolish for not locking them"; wrench and pick have it open in under a minute. She opens every drawer, because she is thorough. The top three hold the shell company's life: incorporation papers, annual reports with Diane's signature at the foot of each, equipment warranties and service contracts. The bottom drawer holds the building's own paper (lease, insurer's schedule, fire certificate) and, hung between the lease and the fire certificate, one manila folder with a name typed on its tab.
10. **[TURN]** Mine. A printed file, three years old, with her name on the tab: the campaign's dossier on Iris Vale. Clipped inside the front cover is a colour photograph taken with a long lens. It's her face, outside her own building, younger.

- **Sensory lead:** smell (rack exhaust, warm metal, burnt dust, new cable), then sound (the fans' white noise, the foil blanket's crackle, which is the loudest thing she does all night), then temperature (the hot aisle, sweat at her collar, warm in every sense the Act II register wants). Texture: the foil's crinkle, the wafer lock's pins under the pick. Sight last: the LEDs, the amber flicker.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**
  - "Breaking and entering is mostly entering. In my trade the breaking is done by the saw, and nobody has ever thanked the saw." (as used)
  - The body-cooling crossing: "a degree an hour. I could go slower than that." (as used)
  - On the fan noise: "a room you could scream in all night and be filed as a cooling fault." (as used)
- **Body:** gloved hands, steady on the card, the keypad and the pick. Her weight frozen on the ball of the right foot, the calf burning, reported anatomically (soleus holds the ankle in plantar flexion; gastrocnemius is not built to, and burns). Sweat. Pulse up and reported as a finding: appetite, not fear. (The hunger held "the way I had held the step" comes in S3, in the car.)
- **Motifs:** hands; the grade (Brandt's security graded, high marks for the room inside the room, the operator docked for the mug); the foil (the phone sleeve's big sister, an echo worth one clause); the room that keeps its sounds (pre-echo of the editing suite in Ch 16-17; one line at most and don't explain it). Mirrors withheld: the office glass and monitors give back LEDs only.

## Scene 3: The Insertion

1. She closes the folder before she gets anywhere near the complaint and puts the file on the desk, squared to the edge. The job comes first. She tells the reader so and is a little proud of the discipline, "the way you are proud of not eating the second biscuit until the work is done". This is the one beat of restraint in a chapter of indulgence.
2. The tap. Back out under the foil and along the gaps between the cones to the rack where the building's outside line comes in. The tap is the size of a pack of cards. The line in from the street is no use to her: everything on it travels sealed. She goes past the box where the seal (the encryption) comes off, to the uplink on its inside, behind the cable management, where the traffic runs bare, "the way the vessels do once you are through the capsule". Seating it inline means cutting the link, and she counts while it is cut: seven seconds. Buildings drop seconds every night and nobody writes them down; she lets hers go into the noise. Its lead goes into a USB port on the rack's power strip; its light comes on and goes off again. It keeps what it hears on a card, for collecting by hand, and sends out only a thin summary and a heartbeat inside the building's own traffic, "like a pulse taken through somebody else's wrist". She read its manual the way she reads a toxicology report and fitted it three times on her kitchen table, and says so in one line.
3. The reasoning she narrates for Brandt, correctly, the way she did in Ch 9. He will know someone was here, because she is about to tell him. He will sweep for a transmitter and find nothing with an aerial. He can't call the police about a break-in at a building he keeps whatever he keeps in, owned three companies up by a woman eighteen days dead whose autopsy Iris signed as reviewer. He can't tell Holt without explaining the building. The three walls still stand, and she is standing behind them.
4. The calling card that isn't a calling card. Back through the doorway under the foil, then at the desk she cuts a single rectangle off the black vinyl, the length of a pair of eyes, with one clean snick of the blade, and lays it on the desk in front of the keyboard, squared to the edge. There's no page and no marker. It's a redaction bar with nothing under it. She means it to say only that she was here (ch12 prose: "I had meant it to tell him only that I had been"). The syllabus is teaching back: this is what a student leaves when she has read the teacher's file.
5. She grades the dossier before she leaves it, because she can't not. Her address (the same flat). Her salary band. Her height, two centimetres out, and everything else right, "a better mark than most of my residents got". A pencilled column of her movements over two weeks: home, work, the butcher, home. A printout of the blind item that ended her credibility, with edits in the margin in Brandt's handwriting, which she knows from his dossier "the way you know a colleague's suturing". Then a typed page headed with a summary of her complaint, which she turns over without reading: "I know how it ends. I was there for the ending." On the routing slip, in Diane's hand, "Reviewed. Contained. D.P." She gives it a pass: it was accurate. She notes that she was filed between the lease and the fire certificate, as a fire risk, assessed correctly.
6. The photograph, looked at properly, last, because she looks after she knows. A six-by-four print, long lens, a little soft. A woman coming out of her building's front door with a paper bag of groceries in the crook of one arm, celery leaves over the top, hair longer than she has worn it since, in a coat she gave away. On the back, in pencil, a date in October three years ago and a reference number. She notes that the paperwork has a number for her face. (She does not recognise the number's format; see Conflicts, control numbers.)
7. One inference, reported and then refused, in the same move as ch10. The woman in the photograph looks more like Tessa Quinn than Iris looks now. Brandt didn't match Tessa to Iris. He matched Tessa to this. She tells the reader she is not going to think about that, and she doesn't, for the length of the chapter.
8. **[TURN, part 1]** She unclips her photograph, slides it into a glassine envelope from her pocket, and leaves the rest. The paperclip stays on the folder; the folder goes back in the bottom drawer between the lease and the fire certificate, and the plunger of the lock goes home. Out under the foil and along the gaps, the midline keyed in to arm the panel, the side door closed behind her at 10:21 p.m. Forty minutes and change.
9. **[TURN, part 2, the Turn proper]** The car, two streets away; she doesn't start the engine. On the tablet, Brandt is still at the War Room table with his jacket on, knowing nothing yet; she watches him for a minute and turns the screen face-down on the passenger seat. Sweat drying on her back under the vest; the condensers a faint roar two streets off, then she stops hearing them. She strips the gloves off inside out and is hungry in the old, frank way, in the stomach and the back of the jaw, and holds it the way she held the step. She takes the photograph out of the envelope by the edges, the way she holds evidence, and bare-handed, the way she never does (a broken rule, not evidence-handling). The satin of it is the first thing she has touched without a glove since she pulled the gloves on at twenty to ten. The sodium street lamp turns the colours of three years ago to tea and ash. She looks at her for a long time; she knows the hour is passing and lets it. She doesn't recognise her either. *("Either": Diane's eyes passed over her in Ch 2 and registered nothing; the campaign filed her and didn't know her; now Iris joins them. Don't explain the antecedent on the page; the reader has Ch 2.)* The scene ends here, on the Turn.

- **Sensory lead:** texture and touch. The vinyl tape's dull sheen and the snick of the blade through it. The glassine envelope. The photograph's satin finish under bare fingertips once the gloves are off: the first thing she has touched without a glove since she pulled the gloves on at 9:40 p.m. (not "since the bun": she handled the tablet and the kit bare-handed before gloving up). Sight: the sodium street lamp turning the print's colours to tea and ash. Temperature: out of the hot aisle into the car, with sweat drying on her back, a long slow evaporation and nothing else (no cold: Act II is warm, and ch18's cold is not to be pre-empted). Sound: the condensers fading two streets away until she stops hearing them, then the engine not starting.
- **Candidate jokes / dry observations:**
  - "Filed between the lease and the fire certificate. I had been assessed as a fire risk. I had been assessed correctly." (as used)
  - "My height, two centimetres out, and everything else right, which is a better mark than most of my residents got." (as used)
  - On turning over the complaint summary: "I know how it ends. I was there for the ending." (as used)
- **Body:** hands gloved, then bare. The bare hands on the photograph are a quiet seed for the bare right hand in Ch 13 and Ch 17; don't underline it. Sweat drying. Hunger reported straight, before the long look. She sits still for a long time, as she did for an hour in Ch 1, and the reader may hear the echo.
- **Motifs:** tape (the black vinyl rectangle, the redaction bar). The grade (the dossier graded; Diane's routing slip graded posthumously: a pass). Paperwork (the reference number on the back of the photograph). Hands (gloved, then bare). Tessa, by face only, not by voicemail: the voicemail motif stays with ch10's timestamp and is not reopened here.
