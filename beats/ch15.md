## Scene 1: Resources Cut

1. Monday 12 Oct, a quarter past six a.m., the flat, bathroom basin under the fluorescent. The stitches come out first, because it is the tenth day ("I keep appointments, even my own"): seven simple interrupted 4-0 nylon, tails long, each knot lifted with the Adson forceps, cut with the suture scissors on the side nearest the skin and drawn out through its own track with a small ticklish drag. (The instruments live in the drawer under the bathroom sink per the ledger; the drawer is not named on the page.) The seam is the pink of boiled ham, shut, a little proud at its lower end where the skin tore when she landed. Healing by primary intention. She puts the seven stitches in a tissue and goes to send an email.
2. 7:40 a.m., a bus-station café on its public wifi, her back to the dome camera over the till. She is on her clean laptop with an americano that tastes of the bottom of the pot and of the paper of the cup. People around her are eating eggs. She files an anonymous complaint with the state's data-privacy regulator, alleging unlawful interception of communications by Lantern Data Services LLC, and copies it to the abuse desk of the upstream provider that carries Lantern's line. She attaches the annual report from the state's public register, with Diane's signature at the foot (officer of record: Diane Pell), and under it Diane's death notice. Then she sends the notice and the report again to the bereavements desk of the bank whose statements came through his mail. Every account in the chain has one signatory and one contact, both Diane.
3. The lever is Diane: nobody in compliance keeps a dead woman's account open for longer than it takes to read the notice. Joke: "I had killed the officer of record, which in my experience is the fastest way to get a compliance department to return your email."
4. She is plain with the reader about the arithmetic. She doesn't need the inquiry to succeed. She needs him dark for forty-eight hours: no feeds, no cameras, no eyes in the warehouse district or anywhere else his systems reach, starting before Tuesday night and running until well after it. The Gatekeeper is still keeping the Architect's door, now by being dead across the threshold.
5. She shuts the laptop, and a thought comes in without knocking. She has just written to the government, and somebody else is writing to it too: E. Reyes, a signature block and an initial, who asked for every agreement under a matter number she knows by heart (Ch 14). She wonders which inbox her complaint will land in, and whether one day the two will lie on the same desk, and decides it doesn't matter for forty-eight hours ("a long time in a body and no time at all in a federal building"). She finishes the coffee because she paid for it, and goes to work.
6. The morgue, a day shift. The suite smells of formalin and the night staff's pine floor cleaner. Mara is at the next table and asks, not looking up, blade an inch above a sternum, whether she listened to it. Iris says "Yes", and, because Mara still hasn't looked up, "Yes" again. She had played it only as far as a needle would have lifted, and no further, and doesn't tell her that. Mara says nothing more; the tops of her ears go pink, helix first, then lobe. Iris has marked the café's coffee and the regulator's online form before nine; she doesn't mark Mara, and doesn't ask herself why. Her own case is a man of eighty-one, natural causes. The heart comes out at four degrees with the cooler's breath still on it; she sets it in the pan of the hanging scale, 460 g, and feels the cold through the glove the way you feel the weather through a window, a thing not yet worth a sentence. "He had a big heart."
7. At ten to five, out of the gown and out to her car in the ME's car park, she takes the clean tablet from her work bag. The tap sends a heartbeat every few minutes, and the tablet is set to alert her if one is missed. It missed one at 4:41. At the same minute the two units in his house went dark, and a tap and two cameras have nothing in common but the line, so it was the line. The abuse desk found a dead woman as its only contact and put the account on hold. Nine hours from filing. She grades the abuse desk: prompt, frightened, a B-plus, docked for not checking whether she existed.
8. Blinding him has blinded her, the half she had not priced in. Her tap went dark with his line, and her eyes in his house with it. The War Room feed still runs, but Holt has seen to it that he is no longer in that room (Ch 14). For two days she has no wire into him at all and will have to see him with her own eyes, like a person. She touches her side, where the seam is.
9. Distrust. Nothing on the syllabus has ever gone faster than she planned it. When things go early, in her experience, it is because somebody at the other end didn't fight. She doesn't let herself finish the thought about who.
10. [TURN] She has more time than she needs, and she doesn't trust it, and so she uses it to cook. On the way home she goes to the butcher's for a whole free-range bird with its giblets in a paper twist, and at the stove she is quick and happy. Salt into hot water, bay, black peppercorns, three curls of lemon peel. When the brine has cooled the bird goes in a lidded tub, breast down, weighed under with a plate, and into the fridge for Wednesday, "for whoever I would be on Wednesday". The neck and wingtips go into a pot for stock. She fries the liver in butter, seared outside and pink in the middle, and eats it on toast standing at the counter, and it is all she eats. "I am a professional. You always taste the liver first."

- **Sensory lead:** Taste first, since the chapter is about what she does and doesn't put in her mouth. The café americano tastes of the bottom of the pot, with the paper of the cup in it. ## Scene 2: The Herding

1. Opens on the joke: "Herding is mostly a matter of which gates you leave open. Sheepdogs know this. So does every HR department I have ever filed a complaint with." With the feeds gone she herds him blind, from what she already has: the dossier, eleven days of his mail relayed by her tap before it died (bodies only, about 2 KB at most, per the ledger; not stated on the page), and the card she carried out of Lantern on the night she bled on his floor (Ch 12).
2. The gate she left open is the one company she did not name to the regulator: a media consultancy he owns silently, with an editing suite on the third floor of a converted printworks east of the river. Its lease, its alarm and its building cameras are paid through a holding company of their own, and the cameras route through Lantern, so they are dark now too. "Facilities mail is the least guarded mail in any company": his gave her the stairwell code and the suite keypad as plain text, like a shopping list. Reading the keypad code again at her own counter on Monday night, the heat comes up her neck into her face, like the first glass of something. The card gave her the fit-out drawing, with the cable grommets along both long edges of the edit desk, and the acoustic contractor's specification for the walls. She has shut every other door on him in order, enjoying each like the last click of a zip-tie's ratchet, and left this one on the latch, "the way you leave a light on for a guest who doesn't know the house".
3. 10:15 p.m. Monday. She rings his personal phone from a second burner; it goes to voicemail, as she meant it to. She leaves a message in her own voice, in the register of a wire-service business reporter, calm, polite and a little bored, asking about Diane Pell's directorship of Lantern Data Services, and says she will try him at the house tomorrow. She has the phrase from his own mail: it is what he writes when he wants somebody to stop sleeping. It puts the voice she gives evidence in onto a phone the police will be holding within the week, and she prices that, and pays it. "I had never left a message I wanted returned less." Now his house is a place where a stranger might be standing on the step in the morning, holding a notebook. This is a message she means to go unreturned, which makes it the inverse of the voicemail motif. It is not a fourth of the three; don't number it with them.
4. Tuesday morning, before her shift, she counts the car among the shut doors. His car service bills a card on the shell's account, the shell's bank has read her notice by now, and a frozen account does not pay for cars. She will know by the evening. Holt walled him out of the War Room on Thursday, by procedure, without a raised voice. "Holt was herding for me, for nothing. He has always been good with staff." The inference is confirmed only by the taxi at 6:20 p.m.
5. The one thing she leaves alone is the Tabard Club. It is Tuesday, his night, the set from nine to eleven. She wants to know whether, with every other door shut, he will choose the room where people listen to him or the room where nobody can.
6. A working day she gets through on competence: two cases, a report signed, her initials on every specimen label. At the sinks in the afternoon Mara hums four bars of the Mingus while scrubbing, more through her nose than her mouth, and stops in the middle of the fifth when she hears what she is doing. Iris says nothing and doesn't mark it either. "That was twice."
7. 5:30 p.m. She parks across the street from the printworks and the engine ticks as it cools. Four storeys of soot-dark brick, the consultancy on the third. One stair serves every floor, behind a column of frosted glass on the street side, with a door at its foot onto the street and another at the back onto the car park. She cannot run the clap test this time; for the first time on the syllabus she is trusting somebody else's paperwork. She has read the acoustic contractor's report twice and given it a B ("The seals were good. The prose was not."). The tub of wipes is in the driver's door pocket, where it lives.
8. 6:10 p.m., the streetlights come on, sodium warming through pink to an orange that makes everybody look embalmed. Hands at ten and two for fifty minutes, still bar once. She hasn't eaten since the liver. The wound pulls from sitting twisted to watch the door; her right hand comes off the wheel to it, stays while she thinks, and goes back.
9. 6:20 p.m., the hour he would have been sitting down to dinner before his set. A taxi pulls in across the street with its indicator going. Not his car: the shell has stopped paying for cars. Her pulse goes up in her throat on the indicator's beat. He gets out in charcoal, with a paper bag showing the neck of a bottle and a record in a card mailer under his arm. The last record of his she touched, she snapped in half and left on his pillow; he is bringing another. (This is where the answer to the broken record now sits.) He looks up the street and down it once, not afraid, the way a man looks when he is early for a table and wonders whether she is too. He doesn't see her. As he crosses under the sodium she looks at him the way she looked at the heart in the pan: eighty-odd kilos, blood heat all through, and under the charcoal the sternal angle, where the shears would start. She stops there. Joke: "He had a bottle. You don't bring a bottle to a place you are hiding in. You bring a bottle to a dinner."
10. He lets himself in at the street door with his own key. Behind the frosted glass the light comes on at the first landing, then the second, and stops at the third.
11. [TURN] He had read the syllabus. He knew what a soundproofed room was for. He went willingly.

## Scene 3: Dressing

1. 7:00 p.m., home. She clears the kitchen table, lays a clean white towel on it and sets out the instruments left to right in the order she will want them, the way you lay a table for a tasting menu so nobody has to ask which fork: the small handle and its blade, the small retractor, the toothed forceps, the larger handle and its blade, the spare blades in their sleeve, the rib shears last, their handles cold through the skin of her palm. Joke: "The rib shears were the cheese course: heavy, optional, and the real reason you book." Off to one side, like cutlery set out for the table, go the rest: a box of nitrile gloves, the thin black leather gloves for the car and the suite door, the red marker, a steel pin, the black vinyl, zip-ties, industrial tape, two foil sleeves (one for his phone, one for her own), ten towels folded, and the bags. Joke: "I had laid a place for him at my own table, and he was not going to be there, which is the usual arrangement with my guests."
2. What is not on the table is a page. She watched him on the feed find his copy on his pillow and laugh and keep it (Ch 13). She is going to a lesson without the lesson, and she trusts him to bring it. She notices that she trusts him and doesn't take it back.
3. She rolls the instruments into the canvas roll the way a cook rolls her knives, ties it and sets it in the bag. Then she showers, hot and short, because the forty-minute shower is for after (Ch 7). The seam on her flank is a line the colour of cooked prawn with seven dots either side, like a zip drawn on by a child. She washes around it and then, deliberately, over it, and it takes the water and soap as if it had never been anything else.
4. Hands: she files her nails short and square over the kitchen sink, so the dust goes down the drain and not onto the towel. Then she scrubs at the same sink with the nail brush and soap, two minutes a hand by the clock on the cooker: palms, backs, between the fingers, each finger as if it had four sides, up to the wrists and past them.
5. Food: "You don't eat before." She names it as a rule from a different kind of evening, and also the rule for the patient, not the surgeon ("Nil by mouth is an instruction to the patient. I had never in my life followed one."). At half past seven she warms a cup of Monday's stock, salts it and drinks it standing, golden with a lens of fat on top, and eats one heel of yesterday's bread with it. Opening the fridge she lifts the tub's lid a finger's width: the brine breathes out bay, lemon peel and raw bird, and the chicken sits on the bottom shelf, pale under its plate, waiting for Wednesday. Putting on her trousers, she feels the waistband against a stomach given almost nothing for two days, and likes it. "I am reporting that."
6. Dressing: a black silk shirt with sleeves she can push to the elbow, cool over scrubbed skin, loose over the seam. Black wool trousers. A black jacket over the silk, with a pocket deep enough for a phone. Flat shoes with a good sole, for stairs. Hair washed and pinned up tight, because she will be working and because her own office keeps her on its elimination register and one hair on his carpet would come home to it. No hair cover in the kit: "You don't wear one to dinner." No jewellery: "Rings tear gloves, and a woman who is going to be elbow-deep in something should not be wearing anything she'd mind losing in it."
7. Then lipstick, which she does not wear to work: oxblood, unscented, because she owns nothing that smells; it tastes of wax when she presses her lips together. She gives the reader a reason (the mouth is the part of a face that gives least away, and a colour on it gives it something to do, so that whoever is looking at you looks there and not at your eyes). It is a good reason; she didn't believe it while she was putting it on, and says so.
8. She goes through the bag once more: the roll, the kit. Then the jacket: her own phone in its pocket, sealed into its foil sleeve at the counter, so the last place it can vouch for is her kitchen and after that it says nothing, which is not an alibi and is a flag of its own, but better than a line drawn from her flat to his door. The two codes, written nowhere, because she has them by heart. Her car keys, with the drawer key on the same ring, as always. Her pulse is up and her face is warm; she doesn't put either down to nerves. She begins to give the preparation a mark and stops, because there is nobody to give it to.
9. [TURN] On the way out, the hall mirror. "You look like you've got a date," she told her reflection, out loud. She didn't correct it.
